ELF's 70.7% sits 7.8 points above Coty (62.9%), 13 points above the last Revlon print (57.8%), and 4.8 points below Estee Lauder (75.5%). The ordering follows price point and cost structure in the way the filings describe them: EL prices at prestige ("a steward of over 20 luxury and prestige brands") and its gross margin is a price outcome that comes with 64% SG&A; Coty owns its Consumer Beauty plants (Ashford, Hunt Valley, Senador Canedo) and attributes its FY2026 margin loss to "higher manufacturing freight and manufacturing expenses as a percentage of revenue" and "higher discounts and promotions"; ELF at about $7 average price with third-party Chinese manufacturing carries no plant and earns a gross margin above every mass peer with plants. That is the cost-position half of the story and it is real. The other half: the gross margin spread over Coty (about 8 points) is entirely consumed at the operating line, because ELF spends 24% of sales on marketing and 62.7% on SG&A in total. The gross margin is high; the operating margin is not.

**(b) Does any competitor with a worse cost position earn a comparable operating margin? The [E2-58] test.** [E2-58] says the exception to the commodity equation is a cost advantage "that is both wide and sustainable" and "by definition such exceptions are few." The test here is whether ELF's gross-margin advantage shows up where a wide cost advantage must show up, in operating margin relative to peers who carry more cost. Answer: yes, a competitor with a heavier cost position earns not a comparable but a much higher operating margin. L'Oreal's Consumer Products Division, which sells NYX and L'Oreal Paris on the same mass shelf, reports 21.4% profitability on EUR 16.1 billion of sales; ELF's best year in the window was 14.6% (FY2024) and its latest is 4.5% (8.0% before the charge). (L'Oreal's owned-factory manufacturing base is not stated in the two press releases cited in section 4 and is not verified in this file; it is the Universal Registration Document that would show it. The comparison in this paragraph rests on the profitability figure, which is cited.) Estee Lauder, with prestige overhead, owned plants (Item 2, verbatim: "We manufacture our products primarily in our own facilities in Belgium, Canada, Japan, Switzerland, the United Kingdom and the United States, and we also leverage global third-party manufacturing networks") and $813M of restructuring in the year, still printed 5.2% GAAP against ELF's 4.5%. The competitors that ELF does out-earn, Coty's Consumer Beauty at (22.1)% and Revlon at 4.0% on the way into Chapter 11, are the ones whose own filings record share loss in mass colour cosmetics ("increased market competitiveness in color cosmetics in the United States"), trademark write-downs on every mass brand they own, and a strategic review to exit. So the row does not support a wide-and-sustainable cost advantage in the [E2-58] sense: the low-cost sourcing model produces a gross margin the mass incumbents cannot match, but the operating result is in the bottom half of the set, and the one competitor at scale in the same channel (L'Oreal CPD) earns roughly three times ELF's best operating margin with a worse cost position. What ELF has beaten is the weakest two members of the set, both of whom were already losing before ELF reached $1B in sales. Caveat stated: L'Oreal's figure is IFRS, EUR, from the IR site, one rung below the SEC filers, and its CPD is not a pure colour-cosmetics division.

**(c) The row's limit: position, not conduct.** [E3-61]: "In some businesses, the participants behave like a demented Kellogg. In other businesses, they don't. Unfortunately, I do not have a perfect model for predicting how that's going to happen." The row shows where ELF stands against the other filers at one date on margin, advertising intensity and write-downs. It cannot show whether L'Oreal's NYX will price against ELF, whether Coty's mass brands will be sold to an owner who fights harder or one who harvests, whether Target and Ulta will fill freed shelf with their own labels (no filer discloses private label), or whether ELF's own 24%-of-sales marketing spend is the price of holding position or of buying it. Two named gaps stay open in the row itself: the EFTS sweep of who names e.l.f. Beauty (blocked by a 403 on efts.sec.gov), and EL's advertising ratio for FY2022 and FY2023 (obtainable, not fetched). Neither changes the shape of (a) or (b).
