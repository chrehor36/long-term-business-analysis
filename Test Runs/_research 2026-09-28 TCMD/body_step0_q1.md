## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**
- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language.** Tactile makes air pumps with inflatable sleeves and garments that squeeze a swollen arm, leg, trunk or neck in sequence, pushing lymph fluid out of it; a patient with lymphedema (swelling after cancer surgery or radiation, or from failing leg veins) uses one at home for up to an hour a day. A doctor or therapist refers the patient; Tactile's own staff then assemble the paperwork, get the insurer's approval, ship the pump to the patient's home, train the patient, and bill the insurer and the patient for it, either as a sale or as rent-to-own over three to ten months. It sells a second product, a battery-powered vibrating vest that shakes mucus out of the lungs (AffloVest), not to patients but to medical-equipment dealers who bill the insurers themselves. **The money is a spread between what the payer's fee schedule allows for the device and what it costs to make, sell and bill it.** In 2025: revenue $329.5M; the device cost $79.4M to make and deliver (gross margin **76%**); finding and serving the patient cost **$121.2M in sales and marketing (37% of revenue)** and **$88.7M in reimbursement, general and administrative (27%)**; research $8.5M; operating income **$29.3M (8.9%)**. The factory is small (capital expenditures $2.4M a year; *"We believe our manufacturing model permits us to operate with low capital expenditure requirements"*); the capital is the sales force (166 account managers and 166 specialists for lymphedema at 2025-12-31), the billing department and the receivables. About 84,000 patients were served in 2025, at roughly $3,300 of lymphedema revenue each.
- **The scarce input this business controls.** Candidates, stated before Q2 tests them: (a) **the billing position**: *"we are contracted or enrolled as an in-network provider with payers covering over 278 million lives"*, *"We began doing business with Medicare in 2007"*, national accreditation and state licences, an *"80% or greater approval rate on commercial payers and 90% or greater approval rate on Medicare claims"*; (b) **the advanced-pump code**: Flexitouch Plus is reimbursed under HCPCS *"E0652"*, the advanced pneumatic compression code, while Entre Plus and Nimbl are billed under the basic *"E0651"*, and the filer claims *"Our Flexitouch Plus is the only pneumatic compression system offering the flexibility for treating upper and lower extremities, the trunk and chest, and the head and neck"*; (c) **clinical evidence** (*"more than 26 studies"*, a 236-patient head-and-neck randomised trial reported in 2025); (d) **the direct channel**: *"We believe we are the only pneumatic compression home-therapy device company with a meaningful U.S. market position supported by a direct sales force."* Whether any of these lets Tactile set its price is Q2's question; the filer's own sentence is placed before it: *"Reimbursement rates are established by fee schedules mandated by private payers, Medicare, the Veterans Administration, and certain Medicaid programs."*
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** In kind, yes. The FY2016 10-K describes the same device (Flexitouch, cleared 2006), the same direct-to-patient billing model, the same payer mix question and the same sentence about *"anticipated price erosion"* that the FY2025 10-K carries. What moves is the payer regime, and it moves by decree: the Medicare local coverage determination for pumps was *"retired"* on 2024-11-14; from *"April 13, 2026, CMS will require prior authorization requests (“PARs”) for pneumatic compression devices"*; the devices *"are not currently subject to the competitive bidding process"*, which the filer names as a risk; a coding contractor *"could remove all coding or assign a different HCPCS code with a lesser payment rate"*. None of this makes the business hard to understand; it makes the price someone else's decision, which is Q2's question.
- **Relatively simple and stable in character [E3-31]? A decision in five minutes [E4-46]?** Yes. A device maker that is also a medical-equipment supplier, in one segment, whose revenue, payer mix, gross margin, selling cost and billing cost are all filed; the one thing that takes reading is the receivables (a Medicare appeals backlog carried as a non-current asset from at least 2014 until it was collected in 2023-2024), and the notes explain it.
