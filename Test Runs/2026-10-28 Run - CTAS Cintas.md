## Q1: CAN I UNDERSTAND IT? Where its economics will be in ten years, inside my perimeter. STOP.
- **The test as the draft states it, applied to the filings.** Understanding is "a reasonable fix on about what the earning power and competitive position will look like in five or 10 years" **[M2012-065]**. Cintas is a route business: about 12,500 local delivery routes from 496 facilities (210 rental processing plants) serve more than one million business customers, none above one percent of revenue (10-K FY2026, Items 1 and 2). Over 95% of revenue is fees for route servicing (Note 1). The parts: Uniform Rental and Facility Services (FY2026 revenue $8,621.6M, 76.5%), First Aid and Safety ($1,391.9M), and All Other, Fire Protection ($929.1M) and Uniform Direct Sale ($322.1M) (Note 2).
- **The key variables, and whether they are foreseeable** **[M1998-044]**: the number of customers and the products sold into each (penetration), the price per stop against labor, energy and garment cost, the cost of the garments put in service (amortized over 18 to 30 months), and route density, which sets the cost per stop. Each is reported or inferable year by year: organic growth by quarter, segment margins, uniforms in service against revenue (between 10.8% and 11.7% of revenue in every year FY2016 to FY2026), and capital spending by segment. None turns on technology; the services are laundering, delivering and restocking garments, mats, restroom supplies, first-aid cabinets and fire equipment, much of it driven by safety rules and the customer's wish not to do it in-house. The ten-year question is important and knowable **[M2006-076]**: whether the businesses that rent uniforms today will still rent them, and from whom, and at what margin. The insiders would write that forecast down; the industry is old and slow, unlike the fields of **[M2000-105]**.
- **What could put it outside the circle.** "high labor content" is present (48,100 employees), but the second half of **[M2007-116]**, a product "shipped in from abroad very easily", does not fit: the garments are sourced abroad, the weekly service at the customer's door cannot be. The 10-K names artificial intelligence as a cost and risk inside the company, not as a substitute for the service. The FY2016 10-K's discontinued document-shredding business (Shred-it, sold FY2016) is gone from the window.
- **Routing.** Not a fast-changing industry, not a financial institution, not a holding company. The parts are read separately at Q2.
- **VERDICT: IN.** The ten-year economics of a route-based rental and service business can be pictured from its own filings **[M2012-065]**, **[M1998-044]**.

- **Why the castle is still standing, and how permanent** **[M1995-038]**. The 10-K calls the markets "local in nature and highly fragmented" and names "price" among the competitive elements, with the customer's option to do it in-house (Item 1, Competition). That is the description of a commodity-like field. The rows give the way through one: "Another way to prosper in a commodity-type business is to be the low-cost operator." **[L2004-007]**; "being the low-cost producer is all-important" **[L2000-017]**. Cintas is the largest operator in North America by revenue ($8.6B in the deciding part against UniFirst's $2.5B and Vestis's $2.7B in total), and in a route business the cost per stop falls with the stops on a route. The margins below are the evidence that the scale is a cost advantage and not only a size. Service also lifts it out of the pure commodity class: the customer is buying weekly delivery, cleaning and restocking at its door, not a garment **[M2001-014]**.
- **The low-cost position, read from the rivals' own filings** (the competitor row below): over the whole span the deciding part's margin ran 15.6% to 24.1% while UniFirst's whole company ran 5.4% to 13.7% and Aramark's uniform segment, then Vestis, ran 2.4% to 12.5%. - **The attacker with money** **[M2011-015]**. The test has been run in the field by others. Aramark, a large and well-financed company, owned a uniform business of $1.6B to $2.6B of revenue for the whole span, bought AmeriPride (Aramark 10-K FY2018: segment sales up about 28%, "primarily attributable to the acquisition of AmeriPride"), and earned falling margins (12.5% in FY2016, 5.0% in FY2021); spun off as Vestis in 2023, it earned 2.4% and a net loss of $40.2M in its FY2025.
