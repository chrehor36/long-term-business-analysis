## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**
- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language.** Core Molding buys resins (polyester, vinyl ester, polyethylene, polypropylene, dicyclopentadiene), glass fibre and fillers, compounds some of them into sheet, and presses or injects them in 82 presses (250 to 5,500 tons) into large structural plastic and fibreglass parts: truck hoods, roofs, fenders and air deflectors, snowmobile and watercraft body parts, and housings and panels for building, utility and industrial customers. Each part is made to the customer's design for a specific vehicle or product programme, in a mould the customer usually pays for (the "tooling" sales, $41.6M of FY2025's $273.8M, which the MD&A calls *"sporadic in nature"*). The customer awards the programme after bidding (Item 1: growth comes from *"resourcing of existing structural products from another supplier"* and *"obtaining new structural products through a selection process in which an OEM solicits bids"*), then orders weekly against its own build schedule (*"typically considered firm for approximately four weeks"*). **The money is the price per part, fixed at the award with some raw-material adjustment, less resin, glass, labour and the cost of the presses, times however many vehicles the customer builds.** Five customers were 65% of FY2025 sales: International Motors 28%, BRP 14%, PACCAR 11%, Yamaha 8%, Volvo 4% (14% in 2024). The truck market was 44% of FY2025 product revenue and 56% of 2024's. Operating income was $14.2M on $273.8M of sales in FY2025 (5.2%).
- **The scarce input this business controls.** **None that the filings name as scarce.** Presses and moulding know-how across several processes in North America, including Mexico, and the supplier position on a programme once it is won (the moulds are built for that programme). Against that, the registrant's own words: *"Our manufacturing facilities utilize various production processes; however, end products are similar and are not unique to a facility or customer base"*; *"the Company does not believe that any single patent, trade name, or trademark or related group of such rights is materially important to its business or its ability to compete"*; *"The Company generally has supplier alternatives for each raw material"*. Whether the programme position is a franchise is Q2's question.
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** **The mechanism, yes; the customers and the programmes, not on the record.** The FY2006 and FY2025 10-Ks describe the same business: SMC compression moulding for North American truck OEMs led by the former parent (International, then Navistar, now International Motors), plus recreational vehicles (Yamaha then, BRP and Yamaha now), under a supply arrangement that holds only *"as long as the Company remains competitive in cost, quality, and delivery"* (FY2006 10-K; the phrase is in every 10-K from FY2006 to FY2019 and absent from FY2020 on). The list of major customers turns over: Freightliner (FY2006) is gone, Volvo arrived (FY2015) and is leaving (*"transitioned from production programs that the Company supplied to new programs that the Company does not support"*), Universal Forest Products appeared in FY2018 (the year of the Horizon Plastics purchase) and is gone, BRP arrived. Understanding how the money is made does not require predicting which programmes are won; Q2 asks whether winning them is a franchise.
- **Relatively simple and stable in character [E3-31]?** Simple: a contract moulder paid per part on programmes it bids for. Stable in mechanism, volatile in level (operating income $16.4M in 2006, $2.5M in 2009, −$11.5M in 2019, $26.5M in 2023, $14.2M in 2025). No technology the business turns on. **Not [E4-46]'s months-of-study case.**
- **VERDICT: [x] IN**: the money is made visibly, as a per-part price on programmes won by bid from a handful of OEMs, times their build rates, less resin, glass, labour and presses. **IN is not a finding on the franchise.**
