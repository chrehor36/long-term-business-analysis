## Q2 — IS IT A FRANCHISE? **[E3-03]**

*Every Kirby figure below is read from the filed 10-Ks for 2006 to 2025 (Items 1 and 1A, the MD&A pricing tables and segment discussions, the segment notes) and the 10-Q to 2026-06-30, or from SEC XBRL companyfacts checked against the filed statements (Step 0). Extractions in `Test Runs/_research 2026-09-26 KEX/`: `pricegrep.py` (`pricegrep_out.txt`), `margins.py`, `seg2.py`, `segassets.py`, `roc.py` (`roc_out.txt`), and the peer filings and script in `peers/`.*

**The hypothesis I formed while reading, stated so it can be attacked [E4-26]:** *Kirby is the largest operator in a market the Jones Act closes to foreigners, but inside that closed market it sells an undifferentiated service (a day of a barge's capacity) against about twenty-five other operators and the customers' own captive fleets, so its price is set by the industry's barge supply against refinery and chemical volumes. It should show [E2-58]'s equation: prosperity, a building wave, a glut, years of falling prices, and returns on the capital that are ordinary across the cycle. KDS, a distributor of other makers' engines winning work by competitive bid, is not a franchise on its face.* The evidence against, above all the scale and the Jones Act, is hunted and answered before the verdict.

**[E3-03], criterion by criterion.** *"An economic franchise arises from a product or service that: (1) is needed or desired; (2) is thought by its customers to have no close substitute and; (3) is not subject to price regulation."*

- **(1) Needed or desired [x]: passes.** Refineries and chemical plants are sited on the water to use it (*"The use of marine transportation by the petroleum and petrochemical industry is a major reason for the location of United States refineries and petrochemical facilities on navigable inland waterways"*), and a barge is the cheapest way to move the cargo overland (*"The cargo capacity of a 27,500 barrel inland tank barge is the equivalent of 46 railroad tank cars or 144 tractor-trailer tank trucks"*).
- **(3) Not subject to price regulation [x]: passes.** Rates are negotiated (*"at a set rate (affreightment) or at a daily rate (time charter)"*; spot rates *"at the current “market” rate and are subject to market volatility"*). The Jones Act regulates who may carry the cargo, not the price; the fuel tax is a cost.
- **(2) Thought by its customers to have no close substitute [ ]: FAILS, in the registrant's own words, for marine and for KDS.**
  - *Marine, the market in the company's words.* 2025 Item 1: *"The Company operates within the United States inland tank barge industry, a diverse and independent mixture of approximately 25 large integrated transportation companies and small operators, as well as captive fleets owned by refining and petrochemical companies"*; *"The tank barge industry is very competitive. Competition in this business is based on price and reliability"*. Item 1A: *"The inland and coastal tank barge industry remains very fragmented and competitive. The Company’s primary competitors are noncaptive inland tank barge operators and coastal operators. The Company also competes with companies who operate refined product and petrochemical pipelines, railroad tank cars and tractor-trailer tank trucks. Increased competition from any significant expansion of or additions to facilities or equipment by the Company’s competitors could have a negative impact on the Company’s results of operations."* Kirby's share: *"The Company’s 1,105 inland tank barges represent approximately 28% of the industry’s approximately 4,004 inland tank barges"*; coastal *"approximately 11%"*. Contracts are short (*"The majority of the marine transportation contracts with its customers, by revenue, are for terms of one year"*; *"there is no assurance that any individual contract will be renewed"*), so the customer re-chooses every year.

  - *KDS, in the company's words.* *"While price is a major determinant in the competitive process, reputation, consistent quality, expeditious service, experienced personnel, access to parts inventories and market presence are also significant factors. - **High returns on capital: no, and never high for long.** The best pre-tax return on the marine segment's assets was 19.7% (2008, the top of the pre-recession boom); it ran 11-16% in 2009-2015 and **1.4-8.1% in every year 2016-2025**. The margin that the brief's `level_shift` 1.73 reads as a STEP UP is the recovery from the 2021 trough (4.8%) to 19.3%, which is where the margin already stood in 2006-2015: a cycle, not a new level [E4-41].
- **Pricing aggressively: no, on the filer's own account.** The price table above rises with utilisation and falls with it; in 2016-2017 it fell for two years *"due to excess industry capacity"*.
- **[E2-58] names the class, and the filings trace its whole equation.** *"For the great majority of companies selling “commodity” products, a depressing equation of business economics prevails: persistent over-capacity without administered prices (or costs) equals poor profitability. ... When they finally occur, the rebound to prosperity frequently produces a pervasive enthusiasm for expansion that, within a few years, again creates over-capacity and a new profitless environment. In other words, nothing fails like success"*. The 2012 10-K: *"Due to the improved demand during 2011 for inland petrochemical and black oil barges and federal tax incentives on new equipment, the Company estimated that industry wide 260 tank barges were placed in service during 2012"*; the 2016 10-K: *"260 new inland tank barges placed in service during 2015, 36 of which were for the Company"*; the industry grew *"from 2,750 in 2006 to approximately 4,000 by the end of 2019"* *"from increased barge construction and deferred retirements due to strong demand and resulting capacity shortages"*; then the price fell for three years and the segment's return went from 13.5% to 3.8%. Kirby built into the glut with everyone else, which is [E2-27]'s parade: *"viewed collectively, the decisions neutralized each other and were irrational"*. The one exception [E2-58] allows, *"a cost advantage that is both wide and sustainable"*, requires producers who *"consistently do well"*; a ten-year run of 1.4-8.1% on the segment's assets is not that record.
- **[E3-43] names the class too**: *"“a business” earns exceptional profits only if it is the low-cost operator or if supply of its product or service is tight. Tightness in supply usually does not last long."* Kirby's two good runs (2006-2015 and 2024-2025) are both periods the filer itself describes as tight supply.

- **What the row shows.** **Genesis's small fleet runs the same cycle on the same dates** (troughs in 2017-2018 and 2021-2022, peaks in 2015 and 2024), at a cash margin level with or above Kirby's; its 10-Ks give the industry reason in Kirby's own terms (2019: *"the day rates currently being offered by the market are still near cyclical lows"*; 2023: *"a lack of new supply of similar type vessels (primarily due to higher construction costs and long lead times for construction) combined with the retirement of older vessels in the market"*). **Martin's tiny fleet earned far less than Kirby** in 2014-2016 and said why in the same words Kirby used: *"The abundance of supply of inland marine tank barges in our predominantly Gulf Coast market has had a direct impact on our utilization as well as decreased transportation rates."* So scale may make Kirby the lower-cost operator against the smallest rivals; it does not lift Kirby's cash margin above a mid-sized one, and it does not hold Kirby's price up in a glut.

**The remaining Q2 tests.**
- **[E2-44], the two characteristics.** (1) Fails, above. (2) Fails: marine segment assets rose from $1.05bn (2006) to $4.71bn (2025), more than fourfold, for revenue up 2.4 times, and much of it was bought (K-Sea 2011, SEACOR's fleet 2016, Higman 2018, Cenac 2019, Savage 2020; $95.8M of used barges and towboats in March 2026).
- **[E3-33] / [E5-28], untapped pricing power.** None: the class requires *"a monopoly or a near monopoly"*; Kirby has 28% of the inland fleet and 11% of the coastal fleet in a market it calls *"very fragmented"*.
- **[E4-37], the agony metric.** The filer does not describe price as a choice; it reports the market's rate each quarter, and the 10-K for 2025 and the 10-Q show it turning down with utilisation.
- **[E2-53], the dominance test.** Not dominant; its economics follow industry utilisation, not its position (2021 margin 4.8% as the largest operator).
- **[E2-45], the attacker's test.** The attack needs only capital and a US yard slot: the industry placed 260 new barges in service in 2012 and 260 in 2015, and 60-70 are on order for 2026. The Jones Act keeps out foreign capital, not domestic.
- **[E2-59], the regime.** The Jones Act is a closure administered by government, the class [E2-59] describes: it protects the whole domestic industry from foreign vessels, but Kirby's pricing shows it does not administer the domestic price, and the filer records waivers in 2017, 2021, 2022 and 2025 (*"Waivers of the Jones Act, whether in response to natural disasters or otherwise, could result in increased competition from foreign tank vessel operators"*). What the regime shelters belongs to the regime, not to Kirby.
- **[E4-32], direction.** Recovering from the 2021 trough to 2025, and turning in the second half of 2025 (term renewals (3)%-(5)% in Q4, spot down since Q3; KMT margin 16.4% in Q2 2026 against 20.1% a year earlier).
- **[E4-55], units.** Kirby's own fleet grew from 71 barges (1988) to 1,105, largely by purchase; industry barges have been flat at about 4,000 since 2019. Kirby's tonnage series was not built.
- **[E4-36], which cause of success?** Scale (*"extreme maximization or minimization of one or two variables"*) is the case for Kirby; the record shows scale earning the cycle, not escaping it.
- **The unit of the test [E4-08].** The company. KMT is 57.5% of revenue and about 74% of 2025 segment operating income; KDS fails criterion (2) on its face, so the company verdict turns on marine.
- **[E4-04] and the 2026-09-20 ruling.** The perimeter close is reserved for a name that passes [E3-03] and whose durability cannot be judged. Kirby does not pass [E3-03]; the question does not arise.

**THE STRONGEST EVIDENCE AGAINST THIS VERDICT, hunted hardest [E4-26, E3-41, E4-51].**
1. **Scale and network.** The largest fleet by far (28%), the largest fleeting service, scheduled linehaul tows, its own shipyard and dispatch system, and a customer base that vets carriers on safety and wants one carrier across the whole system (*"Customers also require that their supplier of tank barge services have the ability to handle a variety of requirements, including distribution capabilities throughout the inland waterway system"*). Its margin held at 23.6% in the 2009 recession, and Martin earned a fraction of it.
2. **The Jones Act.** No foreign-built barge or foreign capital may enter, and a new US barge now costs more and takes longer (Genesis 2023; Kirby's own risk factor on *"Significant increases in the construction cost of tank barges and towing vessels"*); the industry fleet has not grown since 2019, and 350 barges are over 40 years old.
3. **Contracts.** 70% of inland revenue on term contracts, 59% of that on time charters; long-standing customers; in 2025 all coastal revenue on term contracts, with coastal term renewals up 24-26%.
4. **Consolidation.** Kirby has bought one rival fleet after another, so its share is rising.
5. **[E3-47]**: a wrongly closed file on an understood business is the costliest error.

**Answered.** (1) Scale is real and it is the argument for [E3-43]'s *"low-cost operator"*, which is the definition of "a business", not of a franchise; the corpus's commodity exception [E2-58] needs a cost advantage that lets its holder *"consistently do well"*, and the largest operator earned 1.4-8.1% pre-tax on its marine assets for ten straight years, its price falling in every glut with the rest of the industry and its cash margin no higher than Genesis's 80-barge fleet. The 2009 margin held because about 80% of 2008 marine revenue was on term contracts, *"of which approximately 50% are up for renewal during 2009"* (2008 10-K), priced at 2008 rates (*"Rates on term contracts ... renewed during the 2009 first quarter were generally renewed at existing rates"*); it fell in 2010 as they renewed. (2) The Jones Act shelters every domestic operator and is the regime's, not Kirby's [E2-59]; the domestic industry grew its fleet by about 1,250 barges net (2,750 to about 4,000) into its own glut under it. The current tightness (no net fleet growth since 2019) is [E3-43]'s *"supply ... is tight. Tightness in supply usually does not last long"*, and the filings already show its end in progress: 66 new barges in 2025, 60-70 ordered for 2026, inland term renewals negative in Q4 2025 and spot negative for four quarters. (3) Term contracts smooth the price; they do not set it: *"The majority of the marine transportation contracts with its customers, by revenue, are for terms of one year"*, and each renewal is at the market (the table). Coastal's 24-26% renewals come in a market where *"The Company is aware of no specialized coastal articulated tank barge and tugboat units (“ATB”) that were delivered in 2025 and no further ATBs currently under construction"*, after Kirby itself exited Hawaii and retired coastal barges in 2021 ($97.5M impairment on the sales and retirements, and $219.1M of goodwill written off in *"one reporting unit in the marine transportation segment"* in the coastal test): the same cycle in a smaller pool, now at its tight end. (4) Consolidation to 28% has not given pricing power: the 2016-2021 price falls happened at a filed share of 23% (2016), 26% (2018), 27% (2020) and 26% (2021); it has put more capital at the cycle's mercy. (5) I weighed [E3-47]. The file closes on the registrant's own account of how its price is made, twenty years of that price, and a decade of single-digit returns on the segment's assets; **[E5-42] keeps the judgments apart**, and nothing here says the fleet is badly run.

**The analyst's own incentive, stated [operator rule 9, E4-27, E3-41].** This would be the fourth Q2 OUT today in the same general shape (AYI, ST, OSK); the pull toward the pattern is a bias, and so is the opposite pull toward finding a franchise after a run of failures, where the Jones Act makes a tempting candidate. Both were tested by putting scale, the Jones Act and the 2009 margin at the head of the case against, by reading the renewal prices year by year from twenty 10-Ks rather than taking the risk factor alone, and by building the verdict from this registrant's filings and not from the previous files' reasoning.

**Why OUT and not the perimeter close (UNKNOWABLE at Q2).** The 2026-09-20 ruling reserves UNKNOWABLE for *"A name that passes [E3-03] and whose durability cannot be judged from filings"*. Kirby does not pass [E3-03]: criterion (2) fails in its own Items 1 and 1A, and the demonstration is absent over twenty years. The evidence is in; it answers the class of the business, not its future.
