## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**
- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language.** Two businesses. **(a) ABF Freight, the LTL carrier, two thirds of revenue.** It picks up shipments too small to fill a trailer (the average in 2025 weighed about 1,090 pounds: $532.18 billed per shipment at $49.02 per hundred pounds) from many shippers, sorts them across docks at its 239 service centers, moves them between cities in shared trailers by road and rail, and delivers them. It is paid by weight, class and distance: in 2025 **$49.02 per hundredweight including fuel surcharges**, on **11,104 tons a day**. Its costs are mostly fixed by the network: *"Costs associated with an expansive LTL network ... are primarily fixed unless service levels are significantly changed"*, and labor, *"52.2% of Asset-Based revenues for 2025"*, is set by a Teamsters master agreement (the 2023 ABF NMFA, to June 30, 2028, top wage and benefit rates *"estimated to increase approximately 4.2% on a compounded annual basis"*). So the carrier's profit is the thin difference between yield times tonnage and a largely fixed, contractually escalating cost base: its operating ratio (expenses over revenue) was **93.7% in 2025**, 91.2% in 2023 and 2024, 87.3% in the best year (2022). **(b) Asset-Light, a third of revenue.** It sells truckload capacity it does not own (*"a network of more than 70,000 approved contract carriers"*), managed transportation, expedite, ocean and air forwarding and U-Pack moving, and keeps the spread between what the shipper pays and what the carrier is paid; the segment lost money in 2023 ($12.3M) and 2025 ($15.3M), and earned $58.4M in 2024 only with a $90.3M release of the MoLo earnout liability.
- **The scarce input this business controls.** The candidates, stated before Q2 tests them: (a) **the LTL network itself**, service centers and doors in *"more than 99% of U.S. cities with a population of 30,000 or more"*, which the filer calls *"significantly more infrastructure-intensive than truckload operations and, as such, has higher barriers to entry"*; (b) **a century of customer relationships** and its claim to handle freight *"generally considered difficult to handle"*; (c) the combination of its own LTL capacity with brokered capacity for large shippers. The filer credits none of them with price-setting; it says competition *"is based primarily on price, service, and availability of flexible shipping options"*. Whether any of them lets ABF set its price is Q2's question.
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** Yes, for the carrier: the Exhibit 13 of the 2003 10-K reports ABF's operating ratio (*"ABF's improvement in its 2003 operating ratio to 94.3% from 94.6% in 2002"*), the 2005 one its billed revenue per hundredweight ($23.90) and pounds per day, and the 2025 10-K reports the same business with the same key figures (tonnage per day, billed revenue per hundredweight, operating ratio). Asset-Light is a more recent addition (formed as ABF Logistics in 2013, MoLo bought in 2021, rebranded and restructured in 2026) and its composition has changed repeatedly (FleetNet sold in 2023, Panther and MoLo brands retired in 2026), but it is freight brokerage, a trade whose unit economics are a spread over purchased capacity. The technology bets (Vaux) are small and one was discontinued in July 2026.
- **Relatively simple and stable in character [E3-31]? A decision in five minutes [E4-46]?** Yes: an LTL trucking network plus a freight broker; revenue is price times weight, and every 10-K since 2003 publishes the tonnage, the yield and the operating ratio. Nothing requires months of study; the questions are whether ABF sets its price or takes it, and what the owner earnings are after the fleet is renewed, which are Q2's and Q4's.
