## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**
- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language.** Karat buys single-use foodservice goods (cups, lids, straws, take-out containers, bags, cutlery, gloves, janitorial supplies, bubble-tea ingredients), mostly finished, from about 150 overseas factories (about half the purchases from Taiwan, 15% from China in FY2025, the rest increasingly Malaysia and Vietnam), ships them by sea to its own warehouses in California, Texas, New Jersey, Washington, Illinois, Arizona and Hawaii, and sells them by the case to restaurant chains, foodservice distributors, small shops and online buyers, with custom printing for chains; about 9% of sales are made in its own US plants. The money is the gap between the landed cost of a case (the vendor's price, ocean freight, import duty and tariff) and the price the customer pays, less the cost of warehousing and delivering it: gross margin **24.8% (FY2018) to 38.9% (FY2024), 36.8% in FY2025**, operating margin **1.2% (FY2018) to 10.4% (FY2023), 8.9% in FY2025** (filed statements). The filer names the swing variable itself: *"Most of our products are sourced from vendors abroad and as a result we incur freight costs from these overseas import shipments, which could be a significant component of our cost of goods sold."* Freight and duty ran from **6.8% of sales (FY2020) to 14.5% (FY2022), 7.5% (FY2023) and 11.8% (FY2025)** (MD&A, each year).
- **The scarce input this business controls.** Little that is scarce in the legal or physical sense. *"Our intellectual property portfolio includes 12 active trademarks, including Lollicup, Karat, Karat Earth® and Total Clean, and 5 registered copyrights"*; no patent is claimed. The supply is not exclusive: *"our arrangements with our suppliers and manufacturers are not exclusive. As a result, they could produce similar products for our competitors, some of which could potentially purchase products in significantly greater volume."* What Karat controls is **a sourcing network and the know-how to switch it** (the move of about seven points of purchases from China to Malaysia and Vietnam in one tariff year), **a warehouse and delivery footprint** close to the customers, **a catalogue of about 8,400 SKUs** and **a customer list** (*"Our major customer retention rate, defined as year-over-year retention of our top 100 customers, was 99% from 2024 to 2025"*). Whether any of it is a franchise in [E3-03]'s sense is Q2's question.
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** **Yes, in mechanism**: restaurants will still buy disposables and a distributor will still import, stock and deliver them. The material mix will keep moving under regulation (California's foam ban from 2025-01-01 and the 2032 recyclable-or-compostable requirement, seven state packaging-responsibility laws, all named by the filer), and the landed cost will keep moving with freight, tariffs and the New Taiwan dollar; those move the margin, not the mechanism.
- **Relatively simple and stable in character [E3-31]?** Simple: buy abroad, import, warehouse, deliver. I can state it in five minutes **[E4-46]**, with no technology or scientific judgment in it. It is not stable in its results (gross margin moved 6.5 points in one year, FY2022 to FY2023, on freight), but [E3-31] asks whether I understand the character, and the instability is an economic finding for Q2 and Q4, not a gap in understanding. The one opaque piece, the related-party procurement agent, is a Q3 question, not a Q1 one.

**The hypotheses I formed while reading, both stated so they can be attacked [E4-26].** *(a) Karat is a sourcing-and-logistics franchise: a founder-built importer that learned to buy cheaper in Asia than domestic makers can produce, carries about 8,400 SKUs, keeps 99% of its top hundred customers, custom-prints for national chains, and has earned 14-21% pre-tax on its operating capital every year since FY2020 with almost no debt of its own; its margins rose from 25-30% gross before FY2020 to 37-39% in FY2023-FY2025.* *(b) Karat is a price-taking distributor of commodity disposables: its selling prices rose with ocean freight in FY2021-FY2022 and were cut in each of FY2023, FY2024 and FY2025 while its volumes grew; its suppliers are not exclusive and make the same goods for its rivals; the filer says in its own words that the market sets its prices, that falling costs start price wars and that cheap freight lowers the barrier to entry; the FY2023-FY2025 margin coincides with the collapse of ocean freight and a strong dollar against the New Taiwan dollar.* Each is tested on the filings below.

**[E3-03], criterion by criterion.** *"An economic franchise arises from a product or service that: (1) is needed or desired; (2) is thought by its customers to have no close substitute and; (3) is not subject to price regulation."*
- **(1) Needed or desired [x]: passes.** Restaurants, chains and distributors buy disposables continuously; FY2025 net sales $467.7M, *"an increase of 10.7% in net sales amount and 11.2% in volume"* (10-K FY2025, MD&A).
- **(3) Not subject to price regulation [x]: passes.** No product is priced by a government in any filing read. Regulation bears on what may be sold (the foam and plastic-bag bans, the packaging-responsibility laws) and on the import cost (tariffs, anti-dumping duties); neither sets Karat's selling price.
- **(2) Thought by its customers to have no close substitute: FAILS, in the filer's own words, in every year read.**
  - **Who sets the price.** FY2025 10-K and Q2 2026 10-Q, MD&A: *"Since negotiated sales contracts and the market largely determine the pricing for our products, we are, at times, limited in our ability to raise prices and pass through any impacts of inflation to our costs. Conversely, periods of deflation, where raw material costs decrease, may create pricing pressure and start price wars, potentially requiring us to lower prices, which could also affect our gross margin."*
  - **The competition, Item 1A FY2025**: *"The single-use disposable foodservice products industry is extremely competitive and highly fragmented. Many of the companies that compete in our industry are significantly larger with greater resources, have greater brand recognition and have a larger product offering. We may be unsuccessful in our efforts to compete against such large and established companies. In addition, our current or potential competitors may offer products at a lower price"*; and *"Competitors, many of whom are larger and have greater financial resources than we do, may respond to challenging market conditions by lowering prices in an attempt to attract customers."*
  - **The barrier to entry is the freight rate, in the filer's words** (MD&A, FY2025 and Q2 2026): *"Steady or dropping ocean freight could yield significant opportunities for us to expand our margin. However, it could also reduce the barrier of entry, intensifying the competition."*
