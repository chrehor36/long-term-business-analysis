## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**

**Unit economics in my own words, no management language.**
Autoscope is not, in any meaningful sense, a manufacturer. It is **a two-person-office
claim on somebody else's income statement.** By a **"Manufacturing, Distributing and
Technology License Agreement dated June 11, 1991"** — named in the FY2025 annual report's
own exhibit list, and amended by a letter agreement (1997), four numbered modifications
(2000, 2001, 2008, 2011) and a 2006 settlement — the predecessor company handed Econolite
Control Products an **exclusive** right to manufacture, market, distribute and support the
Autoscope machine-vision vehicle-detection system across the United States, Mexico, Canada
and the Caribbean. Econolite buys the parts, builds the boxes, sells to the
traffic agencies, carries the warranty, and employs the salesmen. Then it computes the
**gross profit** on those sales and **splits it fifty-fifty** with Autoscope. That half is
Autoscope's revenue. Against it sits essentially no cost of goods — reported cost of royalty
revenue in FY2025 was **zero**, and gross margin on royalties was **100.0%**.

So the whole company is: a royalty cheque from one counterparty, minus about **$6.4M a year**
of R&D, G&A and marketing spent keeping the algorithms competitive enough that Econolite's
customers keep buying. In FY2025 the cheque was **$8,885k** and the overhead was **$6,433k**.

**How dominant is the cheque? Completely.** Royalties were **99% of revenue in 2025 and 97%
in 2024**, and Econolite's receivable was **100% of accounts receivable at both year-ends.**
Product sales — the entire direct business in Europe, Asia and the Middle East — were
**$129k**, which is 1.4% of revenue and less than the CFO's salary.

**The scarce input this business controls.** Two things, and only two: (1) the Autoscope
detection algorithms and the trade secrets around them, which it must keep re-earning with
$2.7M of annual R&D; and (2) **the contract itself** — the exclusive North American licence.
Note which of these actually produces the money. The algorithms are the asset; the contract
is the toll booth. Autoscope owns no factory the business depends on, no distribution, no
customer relationship, and **no accounts receivable from an end user.**

**Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** **No, and the filing says so
in a single sentence:** *"In 2008, the term of the Econolite License Agreement was extended
to **2031**. The agreement can be terminated by either party upon **three years' notice**."*
Ten years from today is 2036. The contract that is 99% of revenue expires **five years** from
today, and either side can start the three-year clock at any time. This is the QCOM finding
in miniature and it is carried forward to Q2, where it belongs.

## Q2 — IS IT A FRANCHISE? **[E3-03]**

### THE PRIOR I WAS TOLD TO HUNT HARDEST [E4-26]

Ten consecutive names have closed at Q2, and the brief is right that a single-product
royalty licensor with a long-lived installed base in municipal infrastructure is **exactly
the shape that can be a real franchise.** So I looked for the franchise first and on its own
terms, and I record what I found in its favour before what I found against:

- The economics are the real thing. **Gross margin on royalties was 100.0% in 2025.** There
  is no factory, no inventory risk on the royalty line, no warranty, no salesforce, and no
  end-customer receivable. Revenue converts to operating income at whatever rate overhead
  allows.
- The capital base is trivial and the return on it is high (see the metric below).
- The installed base is genuinely large and genuinely sticky: **"more than 160,000
  installations in over 80 countries worldwide"** (FY2025 AR). Traffic agencies do not rip
  out working intersection detection.
- The counterparty needs it. The company's own words: *"we believe sales of our products are
  **a material part of Econolite's business**"* (FY2025 AR, Item F). That is leverage.

**That is a serious case, and it is not enough.** What follows is why.

### [E3-03] — the three criteria

**(1) Needed or desired — YES.** Vehicle detection at signalised intersections is not
discretionary; it is how the traffic light knows a car is there. Federal, state, city and
county DOTs buy it as infrastructure.

**(2) Thought by its customers to have NO CLOSE SUBSTITUTE — NO. This fails, and the
company's own report is the evidence.** Two sentences, both from the FY2025 annual report,
Item D:

> "For ITS applications, we also compete with providers of in-pavement loop detectors and
> estimate that **more than 60% of the traffic management systems currently in use in the
> U.S. use in-pavement loop detectors.**"

> "Among the companies that provide direct competition to Autoscope video worldwide are
> **Iteris, Inc., Miovision Technologies Incorporated, NoTraffic, Derq Incorporated, ITS
> Plus Incorporated, Wavetronix, LLC, FLIR Systems, Inc., Cubic Transportation Systems,
> Signal Group Inc. (Peek), Sensys Networks Inc., and Smartmicro Inc.**"

The substitute is not hypothetical and it is not a niche: **it is the incumbent, holding
more than 60% of the American installed base**, and eleven named direct competitors sit on
top of it in the above-ground segment. Criterion 2 is not a close call.

**(3) Not subject to price regulation — the criterion passes on its literal terms and the
structure fails on the substance.** Nobody sets Autoscope's price by statute. But the brief
asked for care here and the care is warranted twice over:

- **The customers buy by competitive bid.** Municipal and state DOT procurement is a
  tendering process, and a tender is a price constraint imposed by the buyer instead of by a
  regulator. The FY2025 AR concedes the mechanism: against loop detectors *"we compete
  principally on ease of installation and the total cost of ownership."*
- **More fundamentally, Autoscope does not set its own price at all.** Its revenue is
  **50% of Econolite's gross profit** — a number determined by Econolite's list price and
  Econolite's component costs, neither of which Autoscope controls. A licensor whose revenue
  line is the residual of somebody else's margin has no pricing lever to be regulated.

### [E4-04] — must the moat be continuously rebuilt?

**Yes, and worse: the rebuilding is what broke the year.** The framework's own scope test
asks *"does the spending defend the same advantage, or buy its replacement?"* Autoscope
spent **$2,667k of R&D in 2025 — 29.6% of revenue** — and the FY2025 MD&A says this, verbatim:

> "Revenue from royalties was $8.9 million in 2025 compared to $13.2 million in 2024, **a
> decrease of 32.7%.** Sales of Autoscope Vision, which drive royalty revenues, decreased in
> 2025 compared to 2024 **due to lower volumes and lower average sales prices as the market
> transitions to adoption of Autoscope OptiVu** and our channel partners drew down high
> inventory levels."

The product line went Autoscope Vision → IntelliSight → OptiVu, and **the transition cost a
third of the revenue.** That is Mitsui's Rhodes Ridge, not Coca-Cola's advertising: the
spending buys the *replacement* asset, it does not defend the same one. This is the class
[E4-04] excludes, and Munger's name for the mechanism — **competitive destruction** — is
what a machine-vision algorithm business in an AI decade is exposed to by construction.

### THE TERM. This is the QCOM finding, and it is dated.

> "In 2008, the term of the Econolite License Agreement was **extended to 2031**. The
> agreement **can be terminated by either party upon three years' notice.**"
> — FY2025 Annual Report, Item 9(B)

Ninety-nine per cent of revenue rests on a contract that **expires in five years** and that
either side may end on **three years' notice** — so the guaranteed remaining runway as of
today is **three years, to roughly September 2029**, not five. QCOM's QTL earned 72% before
tax and still failed Q2 because its licences expire on a schedule the filing publishes;
AATC is the same finding at 1/5000th the size. Per **[E4-55]**, whether the term is
disclosed at all is itself a finding — **here it is disclosed, and the disclosure is the
problem.** The company has told me the date its franchise ends.

There is no renewal mechanism described. There is a first-refusal and a first-negotiation
right — both of which run **in Econolite's favour**, not Autoscope's.

### [E4-55] — THE PHYSICAL SERIES. **It is not published, and that absence is the finding.**

I looked for royalty units, licences, installations, intersections, or camera shipments in
the FY2023, FY2024 and FY2025 annual reports and the Q2 2025 quarterly. **There is no annual
unit series of any kind.** The only physical number the company publishes is a cumulative
marketing claim, and it moves in a way no analyst can use:

| report | the claim |
|---|---|
| FY2023 AR | "more than **150,000** installations in over 80 countries" |
| FY2025 AR | "more than **160,000** installations in over 80 countries" |

"More than" is a floor, not a measurement; the figure is **cumulative, never retired**, so it
cannot fall even as the business shrinks; and it is a *marketing* line in the overview, not a
disclosed operating statistic. Under the COLM ruling, **the absence IS the finding** — and
here it is aggravated, because the one place the company does describe volume it describes it
going the wrong way in both terms at once: *"lower volumes **and** lower average sales
prices."* Dollar revenue is not being flattered by pricing here; **both legs fell together.**
That is worse than the Precision Steel case [E4-55], where price rises at least held the
dollar line.

### [E2-44] the two-characteristic test, and [E4-37] the agony metric

- *Can it raise prices when demand is flat and capacity is not fully utilised?* **No.** In
  the one year the filing describes pricing, average selling prices **fell**. There was no
  prayer session over a penny — there was a price cut. [E4-37] says you can *"almost measure
  the strength of a business over time by the agony they go through in determining whether a
  price increase can be sustained."* This business did not get to have the agony; its price
  is set inside Econolite's margin, one company removed.
- *Can it grow dollar volume with only minor additional capital?* **Yes** — trivially, and
  this is the half it passes. Total capex over four years was **$1,172k**, against $44.3M of
  revenue.

**[E3-33] untapped pricing power: NO.** There is no unexercised lever. The split is 50/50 by
contract; raising it requires Econolite's consent, and Econolite is the party holding the
first-refusal rights. **[E5-28]** scopes the claim: real pricing power means *"a monopoly or
a near monopoly"*, and a supplier facing 60%-share loop detectors plus eleven named rivals is
neither.

### [E2-45] THE ATTACKER'S TEST — *"how I would like … to compete with it"*

With ample capital and skilled personnel, I would not attack Autoscope. **I would attack
Econolite, or I would simply be Econolite.** Every asset that touches the customer —
manufacturing, distribution, support, the agency relationships, the brand at the point of
sale — belongs to the counterparty. Autoscope owns an algorithm and a contract with a 2031
end-date. The cheapest way to take this business is to wait: serve the three years' notice,
build or buy detection software (NoTraffic, Derq, Miovision and Smartmicro are all doing
exactly this), and keep 100% of the gross profit instead of 50%. **The attacker's best move
is available to the one counterparty that already has the customers.**

### DEFECT 1 — `acquisition_flag()` fired on a **2010** acquisition and called it "inside the window"

**The brief said $8M of cash paid for businesses *inside the window*, 27% of market cap, and
that on a company this size it is a genuine perimeter question. It is not inside any window.
It is from fiscal 2010 — sixteen years ago.**

The only value the tag ever carries for this company:

| tag | period | value | tagged in |
|---|---|---|---|
| `PaymentsToAcquireBusinessesNetOfCashAcquired` | **2010-01-01 → 2010-12-31** | **$8,316,000** | 10-Ks filed **2012-03-19** and **2013-03-27** |

$8,316k ÷ $31,106k = **26.7%** — the queue's "27%", reproduced exactly.

**The cause, in one line of `Screens/floor_screen.py`:**

```python
acq = annual(facts, ACQ_TAGS)
ends = sorted(acq)[-window:]          # <-- the last five KEYS THAT EXIST, not the last five years
```

`annual()` returns every annual duration ever tagged in any 10-K. `sorted(acq)[-window:]`
then takes the five most recent **tagged acquisition periods**, whatever their dates. AATC
has exactly one such period — 2010 — so the slice returns it and the note asserts it is
"inside the window." **The window is measured relative to the acquisition series itself, not
to the run date and not to the owner-earnings window.**

**This is not a deregistered-filer problem; it affects the whole master queue.** Any filer
that made one acquisition years ago and none since has that old cash divided by today's
market cap. The docstring's measured distribution — *"74 are at or above 15% of market cap …
the read-the-filing tier is 112 names, THIRTY PERCENT of the queue"* — is therefore
**contaminated by an unknown number of stale deals**, and the 30% figure should not be relied
on until the window is bounded.

**Suggested fix, and it adds no step (the tooling test):** intersect the acquisition periods
with the fiscal ends the owner-earnings window already uses, which the screen has computed
one function earlier. A deal outside that window cannot have moved the numerator, so it
cannot create the numerator/denominator mismatch the flag exists to detect.

**Verdict on the flag for AATC: it did not fire on anything. There is no perimeter question.
The owner-earnings history and the market cap describe the same company** — the only
perimeter event in the readable record runs the other way, the **sale** of the RTMS radar
line to Sensys Networks on 2023-08-31, which is properly presented as discontinued operations
and is handled in the computation above.

### DEFECT 2 — `level_shift()` has a sign bug, and it bites exactly on tier 3

```python
if earlier == 0:
    return None
r = recent / earlier
if r < 0.70:
    v = "STEP DOWN - the series has changed level; a tight spread here is not safety"
```

**Zero is guarded. Negative is not.** When the earlier-window mean is negative and the recent
mean is positive, `r` is a large negative number, which trivially satisfies `r < 0.70`, and
the function reports **STEP DOWN on a series that stepped UP**.

Reproduced here: AATC's capex-end owner earnings are `[-236, -321, 5286, 4730, 4084]`.
earlier = (−236 + −321)/2 = **−278.5**; recent = **4,700**; r = **−16.88**; verdict
**"STEP DOWN."** The series went from losing money to earning $4.7M a year.

**Why this matters beyond this run: tier 3 of the queue is defined as "negative owner earnings
at the bottom boundary" and lists fifteen names — SWK, ARM, CALX, BE, MU, ROKU, RGTI, ORCL,
BA, ACMR, ALKT, INTC, ACVA, NEGG, FLNC.** A negative early window is that tier's defining
property, so this is the population where the reading is worst, and a recovering business
there will be labelled STEP DOWN. `best_year_dependence()` is distorted the same way by the
same sign (0.238 here, "ONE YEAR CARRIES THE WINDOW", on a series whose "one year" is only
dominant because two others are negative).

**Suggested fix:** return `None` when `earlier <= 0`, or return the ratio with an explicit
`SIGN CHANGE - the series crossed zero; a ratio is not defined` verdict. A sign change is a
real and important event; it is simply not a step down.
