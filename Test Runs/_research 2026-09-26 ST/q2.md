## Q2 — IS IT A FRANCHISE? **[E3-03]**

*Every Sensata figure below is read from the filed 10-Ks for FY2010 to FY2025 (Items 1 and 1A, the MD&A net-revenue discussions, the statements and the segment notes; FY2008-FY2009 from the FY2010 10-K) and the 10-Q to 2026-06-30, or from SEC XBRL companyfacts checked against the filed statements (Step 0; every cash-flow line used was read off each filed statement by `cfgrep.py`, output `cfgrep_out.txt`). Extractions in `Test Runs/_research 2026-09-26 ST/`: `pricectx.py` (`pricectx_out.txt`), `bsgrep.py` (`bs_is.json`), `roc.py` (`roc_out.txt`), and the peer filings in `peers/`.*

**The hypothesis I formed while reading, stated so it can be attacked [E4-26]:** *Sensata's parts are hard to dislodge once designed into a platform, but the choice of supplier is made in a competitive award among three or four qualified makers (two of them, Bosch and Denso, also its customers), and after the award the customer takes a price reduction every year. The customer thinks it has close substitutes at the moment it buys, and the lock-in lasts only a platform's life. That is [E3-43]'s "business", a low-cost operator whose cost gains pass to the customer as price, and the returns should show it: high on tangible capital because the parts are small and the plants cheap, but falling as the price-downs outrun the cost-downs.* The evidence against, above all the design-in switching cost and the returns, is hunted and answered before the verdict.

**[E3-03], criterion by criterion.** *"An economic franchise arises from a product or service that: (1) is needed or desired; (2) is thought by its customers to have no close substitute and; (3) is not subject to price regulation. The existence of all three conditions will be demonstrated by a company's ability to regularly price its product or service aggressively and thereby to earn high rates of return on capital."*

- **(1) Needed or desired [x]: passes.** Every vehicle, compressor, appliance and aircraft needs pressure, temperature and position sensing and circuit protection; much of it is required by emissions and safety rules (FY2025 10-K: *"government regulation of emissions ... require advanced sensors to achieve these performance metrics"*).
- **(3) Not subject to price regulation [x]: passes.** No tariff or fee rule sets the price; regulation sets the need, not the price.
- **(2) Thought by its customers to have no close substitute [ ]: FAILS, in the registrant's own filings, every year for sixteen years.**
  - *The market, in the company's words.* FY2025 10-K Item 1: *"Within each of the principal product categories in Automotive, we compete with three to four suppliers per key application served."* Item 1A: *"We operate in markets that are highly competitive and competitive pressures could require us to lower our prices or result in reduced demand for our products"*; *"low-cost, country-based suppliers, primarily in China with respect to the Industrials segment, have entered the markets or increased their per-unit sales in these markets by delivering products at low cost to local OEMs. In addition, certain of our competitors in the transportation sensor market are influenced or controlled by major OEMs or suppliers, thereby limiting our access to these customers. These customers may choose to develop relationships with additional suppliers or elect to produce some or all of these products internally, primarily to reduce risk of delivery interruptions or as a means of extracting more value from us."* The FY2013 10-K named the rivals: *"Our principal competitors in the market for automotive sensors are Robert Bosch GmbH and Denso Corporation, which are in-house, or captive, providers, and Nagano Keiki Co., Ltd. and Schneider Electric, which are independent. Our principal competitors in the market for commercial and industrial sensors include Saginomiya Seisakusho, Inc. and Schneider Electric"*, and in controls *"divisions of large multinational industrial corporations and fragmented companies"*, among them *"Asian manufacturers"* in basic motor protection; the FY2010 10-K adds *"Robert Bosch Gmbh, who is one of our largest customers with respect to our control products, also competes with us with respect to certain of our sensors products."*
  - *The price, in the company's words.* FY2025 Item 1A, carried in substance since the FY2010 10-K: *"Many of our customers, including transportation manufacturers and other industrial and commercial OEMs, demand annual price reductions. If we are not able to offset continued price reductions through improved operating efficiencies and reduced expenditures, these price reductions may have a material adverse effect on our results of operations and cash flows."* And on costs: *"It has historically been difficult to pass increased prices for manufactured components and raw materials to our customers through price increases."* The first sentence is the negation of [E2-44]'s first characteristic; the second is the same thing seen from the cost side.

**The remaining Q2 tests.**
- **[E2-44], the two characteristics.** (1) *"an ability to increase prices rather easily (even when product demand is flat and capacity is not fully utilized) without fear of significant loss of either market share or unit volume"*: the filer reports price reductions every stated year and writes that *"It has historically been difficult to pass increased prices ... through price increases"*. Fails. (2) *"only minor additional investment of capital"*: capital spending ran 1.0-1.26 times depreciation (Q4), but the growth came by purchase: about $4.3bn of acquisitions FY2011-FY2022 on the filed cash-flow lines ($319.9M, $995.3M plus $298.4M, $996.9M, $228.3M, $32.5M, $64.4M, $736.1M, $631.5M), against revenue that grew from $1.54bn (FY2010) to $3.70bn (FY2025), and the FY2021-FY2022 purchases have since been largely impaired or sold. Fails on the whole record.
- **[E3-33] / [E5-28], untapped pricing power.** None: the class requires *"a monopoly or a near monopoly"*, and the filer competes with *"three to four suppliers per key application"*, two of which are its own customers.
- **[E4-37], the agony metric.** Worse than agony: the filer does not decide whether to raise prices; the customer decides by how much to cut them (*"demand annual price reductions"*).
- **[E2-53], the dominance test.** *"Once dominant, the newspaper itself, not the marketplace, determines just how good or how bad the paper will be."* Sensata's economics are set by its customers' annual price demands and by its own cost programme; the marketplace decides.
- **[E2-45], the attacker's test.** The filing names the attackers already inside: captive in-house suppliers (Bosch, Denso) *"limiting our access to these customers"*, customers that *"may develop in the future, the capability to internally produce the products that we sell to them"*, and Chinese makers *"delivering products at low cost to local OEMs"*.

**THE STRONGEST EVIDENCE AGAINST THIS VERDICT, hunted hardest [E4-26, E3-41, E4-51].**
1. **Switching costs, in the registrant's words.** *"Once our solutions are designed into an application, we are well positioned as the incumbent supplier ... This results in high switching costs for automotive manufacturers once a sensor is designed into a particular system or platform. We believe this is one of the reasons that sensors are rarely changed during a platform lifecycle"*; top-ten customer relationships *"for an average of 36 years"*. That is a claim that the customer, once committed, has no close substitute.
2. **The returns.** 33-88% pre-tax on tangible operating capital every year FY2010-FY2025, and segment margins of 24-29% before corporate costs. [E3-03] says the conditions are *"demonstrated by"* high returns; [E3-46] asks the returns question first.
3. **A narrow field.** *"three to four suppliers per key application"*; a small part in a large system, where [E2-44]-style pricing power often hides because the part's cost is trivial to the buyer.
4. **The mission-critical nature.** A failed brake-pressure or high-voltage contactor costs the customer a recall; quality and qualification, not price, might govern the choice.
5. **[E3-47]**: a wrongly closed file on an understood business is the costliest error.

**Answered.** (1) The switching cost is real and it is bounded: it runs for a platform's life (*"five to seven years"*), it is won in a competitive award against three or four qualified rivals, and even inside it the customer takes a price reduction every year, which the filer reports as a fact of its market for twelve consecutive stated years. A customer who can demand and get a lower price each year from a locked-in supplier does not think the supplier irreplaceable; he thinks the next platform's award is contestable, and it is. (2) The tangible returns are high because the parts are small and the plants are in low-cost countries; [E3-03]'s demonstration is a return earned **by** pricing aggressively, and this one was earned while pricing went the other way, and it has fallen by more than half over fifteen years. On the capital the owners actually paid, including the 2006 buyout goodwill and every acquisition, the return has run 7-12.6%. (3) A narrow field of qualified suppliers is an oligopoly of bidders, and the bidders include the customers' own captive units; the filer's price record answers the question of who holds the power. (4) Mission-criticality raises the bar to qualify; it does not stop the qualified rivals from bidding, and the filer names *"commercial competitiveness"* (FY2025) and *"price"* (FY2013-FY2016) among the key competitive factors. (5) I weighed [E3-47]. The file closes on the registrant's sixteen-year account of its own prices, not on the manager or the share price; [E5-42] keeps the judgments apart, and nothing here says the operation is badly run: through 2022 it earned what the best listed peer's automotive segment earned, in a business that takes a price cut every year.

**The analyst's own incentive, stated [operator rule 9, E3-41].** An OUT here ends the run and follows the AYI file of the same morning in shape; the pull toward a pattern is itself a bias. It was tested by writing the strongest case against first, by putting the design-in quotation at the head of it, and by checking that the verdict rests on this registrant's own filed price record and not on the previous file's reasoning.

**Why OUT and not the perimeter close (UNKNOWABLE at Q2).** The 2026-09-20 ruling reserves UNKNOWABLE for *"A name that passes [E3-03] and whose durability cannot be judged from filings"*. Sensata does not pass [E3-03]: criterion (2) fails in its own Items 1 and 1A and in its MD&A price record. The evidence is in; the question it answers is the class of the business, not its future.
