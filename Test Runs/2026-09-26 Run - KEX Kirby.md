## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**
- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language.** **Marine (57.5% of 2025 revenue, $1,935.3M; segment operating income $374.5M).** Kirby owns about 1,100 river tank barges and hires out their capacity, with its own or chartered towboats, to refiners and chemical makers who need liquids moved between plants and terminals on the Mississippi system and the Gulf Intracoastal Waterway (inland, $1,548.1M), plus 28 larger coastal barges on the three coasts ($387.2M). It is paid by the day (a time charter) or by the trip; most revenue is on contracts of one year or more (*"During 2025, approximately 70% of inland marine transportation revenues were under term contracts and 30% were under spot contracts"*), and fuel is largely passed through (*"term contracts typically include fuel escalation clauses, or the customer pays for the fuel"*). The money is the day rate times the share of days the barges are working, less crews, maintenance, insurance and the wearing-out of the fleet (marine depreciation and amortisation $213.9M in 2025). A barge is the equivalent of *"46 railroad tank cars or 144 tractor-trailer tank trucks"*, so the service is cheap against the land alternatives. **Distribution and services (42.5%, $1,428.7M; $130.8M)**: an authorised distributor and repairer of other companies' engines, transmissions and generators (EMD, Caterpillar, Cummins, Allison, MTU and others), a builder of power-generation packages for data centres and backup power ($610.2M, now the largest KDS market), and a builder of fracturing equipment for oilfield service companies ($167.2M, down 32% in 2025); it earns a margin on parts, labour and equipment, much of it won *"by competitive bids"*.
- **The scarce input this business controls.** **Not a resource; a share of a closed market.** The Jones Act closes US domestic shipping to foreign-built, foreign-owned vessels, and Kirby holds *"approximately 28% of the industry’s approximately 4,004 inland tank barges"* and *"approximately 11%"* of the ~260 smaller coastal barges, with a network others lack (the largest barge fleeting service, linehaul tows on schedules, a shipyard, a dispatch system). The closure is the statute's, shared by every domestic operator; the fleet can be built by anyone with a US yard's order book (the industry grew from 2,750 barges in 2006 to about 4,000 by 2019). KDS controls distribution rights granted by manufacturers, some exclusive for the nuclear industry (*"the exclusive worldwide distributor of EMD, Cooper, Woodward, Nordberg, Norlake and Hannon parts for the nuclear industry"*).
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** **Yes, for marine; KDS's mix moves.** The 2006 10-K describes the same four cargo groups (petrochemicals, black oil, refined products, agricultural chemicals), the same waterways, the same term and spot contracts with fuel escalators; the plants are sited on the water (*"The use of marine transportation by the petroleum and petrochemical industry is a major reason for the location of United States refineries and petrochemical facilities on navigable inland waterways"*). KDS was a marine-engine repairer in 2006, an oilfield-equipment maker by 2012-2014 and 2017-2019, and a power-generation supplier by 2025: the service is the same, the customer shifts with the cycle.
- **Relatively simple and stable in character [E3-31]?** The mechanism is simple (rent capacity by the day; fuel passed through), and the instability is named in the filings: industry barge supply, refinery and chemical volumes, navigation delays, and the oilfield cycle in KDS. Not a business that would take months to understand [E4-46].
- **VERDICT: [x] IN**: the money is made visibly, as a day rate on a large fleet of river and coastal barges in a market closed to foreign vessels, and as a margin on distributed parts, service and built equipment. **IN is not a finding on the franchise.**
