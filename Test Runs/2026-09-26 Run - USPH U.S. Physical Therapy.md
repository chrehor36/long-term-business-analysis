## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**
- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language.** A patient is sent to a local clinic, almost always by a doctor (*"A clinic’s business primarily comes from referrals by local physicians"*, 10-K FY2025 Item 1). Each visit is billed at a rate the patient's insurer has already fixed: Medicare by its national fee schedule, commercial insurers and managed care by contract, workers' compensation by state schedules. In FY2025 the clinics delivered **6,150,104 visits at a net $105.76 each = $650.4M** of net patient revenue; payor mix 48.5% managed care and commercial, 35.8% Medicare and Medicaid, 10.2% workers' compensation, 5.5% other. A visit costs **$86.30** to deliver (GAAP, per visit; $62.04 of it salaries), so the clinic keeps about $19 of every $106 (clinic gross margin **19.2%**). Head office takes 8.9% of revenue, leaving an operating margin of **11.1%** on $781.0M of total revenue (FY2025). Of the $58.0M of net income, **$18.4M belongs to the therapist-partners** who own the rest of each clinic partnership, and $39.6M to USPH shareholders. A second segment, industrial injury prevention (IIP, $114.4M of revenue, 18.9% gross margin), sells on-site injury prevention and testing to employers under contract.
- **How it grows.** By buying majority stakes in existing therapist-owned practices (typically 50-75%, *"Our equity interests generally range from 65% to 75% (with an overall range of 30%-99%)"*), leaving the seller as partner and manager with a put right on the remainder *"calculated at a predetermined multiple of earnings performance"*, and by opening satellite clinics inside existing partnerships. In FY2025 revenue from clinics owned since before 2024 (*"Mature Clinics"*) was **$523.6M against $523.2M, +0.1%**; the whole of the 16.0% growth in net patient revenue came from clinics added.
- **The scarce input this business controls.** Stated plainly, **it controls less than it appears to.** The scarce input is the local therapist who holds the physician referral relationships (*"Our therapists are the front line for generating these referrals and we are dependent on their talents and skills to successfully cultivate and maintain strong relationships with these physicians"*, Item 1A). USPH owns the majority of the partnership that employs that therapist, under a multi-year employment agreement with a non-compete of *"up to two years thereafter"*; it does not own the relationship. What it controls itself is the capital to buy practices, a national billing and payor-contracting platform, and the call right on a departing partner's interest. This is recorded here as a fact about the unit economics; whether it is a franchise is Q2's question.
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** Yes, as far as the filings can show. The FY2007 and FY2010 10-Ks describe the same model in nearly the same words (the FY2010 note: *"the Company generally owns a 1% general partnership interest and a 64% limited partnership interest. The managing therapist of each clinic owns the remaining limited partnership interest"*); the clinic count went from 392 (end 2010) to 780 (end 2025) doing the same thing. Visits billed per fee schedule, therapists as the cost, physicians as the referral source. The things that could change it (payment reform to bundled or outcome-based payment, named in Item 1A) change the price, not the comprehensibility.
- **Relatively simple and stable in character [E3-31]?** Simple: yes; a clinic is a room, a therapist and a fee schedule. Stable: the demand is stable; the price is set by others (Q2).

## Q2 — IS IT A FRANCHISE? **[E3-03]**

*Every figure below is read from the filed MD&A tables and statements of the 10-Ks named at Step 0; the extraction is saved as `rpv_out.txt`, `cf_FY*_flat.txt` and `peers/`. Tagged data was used only to locate years and is not the source of any figure here.*

**[E3-03], criterion by criterion.** *"An economic franchise arises from a product or service that: (1) is needed or desired; (2) is thought by its customers to have no close substitute and; (3) is not subject to price regulation. The existence of all three conditions will be demonstrated by a company's ability to regularly price its product or service aggressively and thereby to earn high rates of return on capital."*

- **(1) Needed or desired [x]: passes.** Post-operative and injury rehabilitation is prescribed by physicians and paid for by every class of insurer; 6,150,104 visits in FY2025.
- **(2) No close substitute [ ]: fails, in the registrant's own words.** 10-K FY2025, Item 1, COMPETITION: *"The healthcare industry, including the physical therapy business and the industrial injury prevention services business, is highly competitive. The physical therapy business as well as the industrial injury prevention services business are both highly fragmented with no company having a significant market share nationally."* And: *"Our clinics compete, directly or indirectly, with many types of healthcare providers including the physical therapy departments of hospitals, private therapy clinics, physician-owned therapy clinics, and chiropractors."* The patient goes where the doctor sends him and the insurer will pay; the filing names who else can take him. The company's claimed advantage is not a customer-side one: *"We believe that our partnership strategy provides us with a competitive advantage. Our clinics are partly owned by therapists who have developed exceptional reputations in their local communities"*, which is an advantage of the local therapist (see [E4-23] below), not a product the customer thinks has no substitute.
- **(3) Not subject to price regulation [ ]: fails.** **35.8% of FY2025 net patient revenue ($232.7M) and 40.1% of visits** are Medicare or Medicaid, paid on the government's fee schedule: *"The Medicare program reimburses outpatient rehabilitation providers based on the Medicare Physician Fee Schedule (“MPFS”)."* The schedule was **cut in each of the five years 2021 to 2025**: *"decreases of approximately 3.5%, 0.75% and 2.0%"* (2021-2023), *"an approximate 1.8% reduction"* (2024 after Congress intervened), and for 2025 *"decreased Medicare reimbursement for therapy services by approximately 2.9%"* (MD&A; Item 1 of the same filing says *"approximately 1.5%"* for 2025, an inconsistency inside one document, recorded and carried beneath the close as a Q3 prompt). Medicare also pays assistant-delivered care *"at 85% of the otherwise applicable Part B payment amount"*. The other 64% is priced by contract with payors who set the terms: *"we are obligated to provide services for the reimbursement rates stipulated in the payor contracts"* (critical accounting policies) and *"If insurers or managed care companies from whom we receive substantial payments were to reduce the amounts they pay for services, our profit margins may decline, or we may lose patients if we choose not to renew our contracts with these insurers at lower rates"* (Item 1A). Workers' compensation is priced by state schedules. **This is [E2-59]'s administered price, and it runs the wrong way for the owner:** the regime does not floor a commodity business here; it caps and cuts, and *"That day is gone"* is how an administered price ends.
