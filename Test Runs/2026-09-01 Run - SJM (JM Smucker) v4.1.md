## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**

- **Unit economics in my own words.** SJM buys agricultural commodities — green coffee,
  peanuts, oils and fats, flour, sugar, fruit, corn, protein meals — converts them in owned
  plants, puts a 50-to-125-year-old trademark on the package, and sells $9.05bn a year of
  it to a handful of very large retailers, of which **one takes 34%**. The profit is the
  gap between what the shopper will pay for the name and what the input plus the plant
  cost, less roughly 5% of sales spent on marketing and advertising to keep the name worth
  paying for. Five distinct businesses sit inside that description and they do not behave
  alike: **coffee** (36.5% of sales, 35.0% of segment profit — a branded converter of a
  volatile traded soft commodity), **frozen handheld and spreads** (20.5% / 22.2% —
  Uncrustables, a genuinely growing proprietary product, plus Jif and Smucker's),
  **pet** (17.7% / 23.6% — Meow Mix, Milk-Bone, the highest-margin segment at 29.6%),
  **sweet baked snacks** (10.7% / 4.8% — Hostess, the acquisition, margin 10.0% and
  falling), and **away from home** (9.7% / 11.0% — foodservice, growing).
- **The scarce input this business controls:** shelf position for #1 or #2 trademarks in
  categories a shopper buys without thinking. It does **not** control the price of green
  coffee, and FY2026 is the proof: +22 percentage points of price in coffee and segment
  profit still fell $93.6M.
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** Yes as to mechanism —
  people will drink coffee, eat peanut butter and feed cats, and the same brands will most
  likely be doing it. The doubts are about the *level* and about who captures the margin,
  which are Q2 and Q4 questions, not intelligibility failures.
- **VERDICT: [x] IN** — relatively simple and stable in character [E3-31]. Nothing here
  requires five months of study; the arithmetic is not complicated [E3-27].

## Q2 — IS IT A FRANCHISE? **[E3-03]**

- Needed or desired **[x]** — staples in the shopping basket.
- Not price-regulated **[x]**.
- No close substitute **[ ] — FAILS in the aggregate, on the subject's own filing.** SJM's
  10-K names **"Private label brands | Various"** as a competitor in **every one of its
  product categories** — all three coffee tiers, peanut butter, fruit spreads, frozen
  handheld, pet food, sweet baked snacks and foodservice. It quantifies the substitute:
  *"private label held a **14.0** dollar average market share during the 52 weeks ended
  April 19, 2026 … as compared to a **13.7** … in the prior year"* (US Retail + Sweet Baked
  Snacks), and **22.2%** in Away From Home. It has a dedicated risk factor headed *"**We
  must leverage our brand value to compete against private label products and lower-priced
  alternative brands**"* and states that it *"closely monitor[s] **the price gap, or price
  premium**, between our brands and private label brands."* A company that monitors its
  price gap to a cheaper substitute in every category it sells is describing a premium the
  retailer can compress, not the absence of a substitute. **Read fairly, this is materially
  milder than FLO's 26% of dollars / 35% of units** — but it is rising, and it is only
  half the story. The other half is the customer.

### The two-characteristic test [E2-44] — 1 of 2, and the failing half is the one that pays owners

**1. Can it raise prices when demand is flat and capacity is not fully utilised? YES —
demonstrably, and better than any peer.** FY2026 consolidated net price realisation
**+9 pp** against volume/mix −4 pp; coffee **+22 pp** against −5 pp; Q4 FY2026 +10 pp; Q1
FY2027 +4 pp with volume/mix **+1 pp**. Against the row: GIS −1 pt of price, CAG +1.0%,
KHC +0.7 pp, HSY ≈+6%. **SJM took more price than anyone in its industry and kept most of
its volume. This characteristic passes, and it is the single strongest fact in SJM's
favour — the exact opposite of FLO, whose FY2025 price/mix went negative.**

**But [E3-62] asks the second-step question and the filing answers it.** *"how much is
going to stay home and how much is just going to flow through to the customer."* In
FY2026 the answer was: **almost none stayed home.** Adjusted operating margin **20.9% →
18.5%**; coffee segment margin **28.3% → 21.2%** (−710 bp) on a 22-point price rise;
adjusted operating income **−8%**; adjusted EPS **−10%**. The trademark collected the price
increase and handed it to the green-coffee market. Hershey is the confirming case in the
row: +6% price, gross margin −1,380 bp on cocoa, Q2 2026 confection volume **−10 pts**
against **+14 pts** of price. **[E2-58]: where the input is a traded commodity and the
brand is not administering the price of the input, the ratio of supply-tight to
supply-ample years sets the profitability, not the trademark.**

**2. Can it grow dollar volume with only minor additional investment of capital? NO. This
fails outright and it is the verdict.** Net sales FY2021 $8,002.7M → FY2026 $9,050.9M =
**+13.1% over five years**. To get it, SJM spent **$5.4bn of acquisition consideration**
and **$2,192.6M of capital expenditure**, took on **$5.0bn of new debt**, and wrote off
**$3,254.3M**. The result, in the company's own MD&A: **net sales +2% CAGR, adjusted
operating income +2% CAGR, adjusted earnings per share flat, and operating cash flow
−1% CAGR.** Cumulative GAAP net income over those five years is **−$85.1M**, against
$2,206.9M of dividends paid; shareholders' equity fell from $8,124.8M to $5,543.8M. **A
franchise converts its position into owner earnings. Over five years this one converted
$7.6bn of deployed capital into nothing.**
