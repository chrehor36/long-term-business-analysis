## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**

- **Unit economics in my own words:** OXM designs resort-lifestyle apparel under seven
  owned trademarks — Tommy Bahama (56% of sales), Lilly Pulitzer, Johnny Was, and four
  small Emerging Brands — has it sewn by third parties in Asia (FY2025: China 29%,
  Vietnam 24%, Indonesia 10%; China cut to a ~15% run-rate going into FY2026), imports
  it over a tariff wall ($95M of duties paid in FY2025 at a ~30% average rate), and
  sells 82% of it direct to consumer at ~61% gross margin through 355 owned stores and
  outlets, brand e-commerce sites, and 28 Tommy Bahama restaurants/Marlin Bars that
  double as brand theater (stores adjacent to a restaurant do ~2x the sales per square
  foot). The remaining 18% is wholesale to department/specialty stores — a channel
  shrinking and consolidating (Saks Global bankruptcy cost $5M of credit losses in
  FY2025). The model stacks brand price premium on retail margin; the offsets are
  355 leases ($572M of undiscounted rent), fashion risk, and a fully imported cost base.
- **The scarce input the business controls:** the trademarks — above all Tommy Bahama's
  niche (affluent, 45+, warm-weather resort identity, with licensing and F&B halo) —
  plus the resort-location store fleet. Nothing else is proprietary: manufacturing is
  rented, fabric largely originates in China even when assembly moved, and the customer
  is a discretionary shopper.
- **Ten years:** apparel branding and DTC retail will work the same way. What is
  genuinely open is whether Tommy Bahama means anything to the 45-year-old of 2036 —
  brand relevance across a generation is a fashion question, and the filing's own
  consumer definition ("older than 45... household income in excess of $150,000") makes
  the cohort exposure explicit. That is a Q2/Q4 matter; the mechanism is legible.
- **VERDICT: [x] IN** — a simple, stable-character business model; what is in doubt is
  the franchise and the cost regime, not how it makes money.
