**Read against the like-for-like peers, Eagle's cement is at the pure-play peer's level, not above it.**
Summit's cement segment, the closest comparison (the same product, the same river region, filed on the
same basis), earned an operating margin of 26.2% against Eagle's 26.0%, and a higher EBITDA margin (38.0%
against 33.5%). Martin Marietta's Texas plants earned a 33% gross margin, a measure that sits above an
operating margin by SG&A. The bundled peers (Amrize, Cemex, CRH) are lower because readymix, asphalt and
paving pull them down, as Eagle's own Concrete and Aggregates segment (4%) would pull Eagle down if it were
blended in; they do not measure a cement cost gap and are shown for coverage, not ranking. On price, Amrize
files an average U.S. cement price of **$169.33 a ton** for 2025 (Item 2) against Eagle's **$156.67** net of
freight for FY2025; the bases differ (freight and trading treatment) and the two are not ranked. **No peer
figure shows Eagle's cement earning a wide margin over others inside the same supply-tight structure.
The economics are the structure's.**

**Here the row is on Eagle's side, and it is recorded as the strongest evidence against the verdict.**
In the glut Eagle broke even while USG lost money every year and Lafarge's gypsum division (later CBPX)
lost money; in the recovery Eagle earned 32-35% while USG earned 10-18% and CBPX 11-21%. **The relative
cost gap is wide, about fifteen to twenty points, and it lasted through a full cycle.** Eagle did this at a
*lower* realized price than USG in the glut ($92 against $111-117 a thousand), which is what a cost
advantage looks like. **What the row cannot do is turn a relative advantage into the absolute one
[E2-58] asks for.** The exception is not "loses less than the others"; it is that the producer can
*"consistently do well"*, and Eagle's wallboard earned 0.4-2.9% for four straight years. USG's own filings
give the reason in the equation's terms: industry utilization of *"approximately 52%"* (2009), *"51%"*
(2010), *"53%"* (2011), and *"At such a low level of capacity utilization, we expect there to be continued
pressure on gypsum wallboard selling prices and gross margins"* (FY2009 10-K). CBPX's 10-K says it without
qualification: *"Many of the wallboard products sold by our competitors are close substitutes for our
products. Because of this substitutability, competition among manufacturers is based in large part on
price"* (FY2014 10-K `0001193125-15-062550`, Item 1A). **A cost advantage that holds a producer at zero
while the rest lose money is real, and it is a survival strength (a Q4 matter). It is not the franchise
[E3-03] describes, nor [E2-58]'s exception: the profit came back only when the industry's price did.**
