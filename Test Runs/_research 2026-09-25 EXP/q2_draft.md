## Q2 — IS IT A FRANCHISE? **[E3-03]**
- Needed or desired **[x]** · no close substitute **[ ] FAILS** · not price-regulated **[x]**
  (cement, wallboard and aggregates prices are not regulated; what regulation there is governs
  **entry**, through air permits and zoning, and is taken below)

**Clause (2) fails on the registrant's own words.** Item 1A, FY2026 10-K (`0001193125-26-230979`):

> *"Many of the products sold by us are commodities, and competition among manufacturers is based
> largely on price. Prices are often subject to material changes in response to relatively minor
> fluctuations in supply and demand, general economic conditions, and other market conditions beyond
> our control. [...] Currently, there continues to be significant excess nameplate capacity in the
> gypsum wallboard industry in the United States."*

Item 1: *"The products we make are basic necessities, and competition is often based largely on price,
with consistent quality and customer service also being important considerations. Thus, being a
low-cost producer is a competitive advantage"*; and the forward-looking statement in every 10-K, 10-Q
and earnings release names *"the fact that our products are commodities and that prices for our
products are subject to material fluctuation due to market conditions and other factors beyond the
Company's control"*. For wallboard, Item 1: *"There are currently six manufacturers of gypsum wallboard
in the U.S., operating a total of 59 plants with a total of 69 lines"*, capacity *"approximately 32.7
billion square feet per year"* against 2025 shipments of *"25.4 billion square feet"* (78%), and
*"Three customers collectively accounted for approximately 64% of our Gypsum Wallboard segment
sales"*. **A buyer can take the same ASTM-grade cement or the same sheet of wallboard from another
producer within freight range, and the registrant says the choice is made on price. [E3-03]
criterion (2) is not met.**

**So the question is [E2-58]'s one exception, and the registrant claims it by name.** [E2-58]: *"In
many industries, differentiation simply can't be made meaningful. A few producers in such industries
may consistently do well if they have a cost advantage that is both wide and sustainable. By
definition such exceptions are few"*; and [E3-43]: *"“a business” earns exceptional profits only if it
is the low-cost operator or if supply of its product or service is tight. Tightness in supply usually
does not last long."* Eagle's Item 1 names both routes: *"Low-cost producer position"* and, for
cement, *"The difficulty in adding cement capacity, coupled with limited alternatives, leads to high
U.S. cement manufacturing utilization rates, as well as the need for imported cement"*. The test has
two halves, and both are run below from filed figures: **did Eagle "consistently do well"** (its own
series, through the whole cycle, [E4-40]'s exposure rather than the recent experience), **and is its
advantage "wide"**, which only the competitor row can show, because a cost advantage is relative.

- **Must the moat be continuously rebuilt? Does success depend on a great manager? [E4-04]** Cement,
  wallboard and aggregates are not in the rapid-change class, so [E4-04]'s perimeter close
  (UNKNOWABLE) is **refused in writing**: the filings answer the franchise question. The business
  is not [E3-74]'s stay-smart retailer either; a kiln and a quarry are "smart once". The relevant
  limits are [E2-58] and [E3-43] above.

  **Two segments, two shapes.**

  **Wallboard is [E2-58]'s equation, run to completion inside the filed record.** From a net price of
  **$161.86** (FY2007) the price fell to **$91.79** (FY2011), 43%, and volume fell 36%, and **the
  registrant that calls itself the low-cost producer earned $1.2M, $1.4M, $1.2M and $6.3M of operating
  earnings in FY2009-FY2012: four consecutive years at 0.4% to 2.9% of revenue, $10.1M on $912.2M of
  revenue.** The FY2008 10-K saw it coming in the equation's own terms: *"The industry has recently
  added approximately 5 billion square feet of capacity and shuttered approximately 3 billion square
  feet of capacity, for a net increase in capacity of approximately 5%"* while consumption fell 14%.
  A cost advantage that leaves the low-cost producer at break-even for four years is not one that lets
  it *"consistently do well"*. **And the recovery did not come from Eagle's costs; it came from the
  industry's price.** The FY2014 10-K (`0001193125-14-211568`): FY2013 revenue *"increased 41% [...]
  primarily due to a 27% increase in the average net sales price"*, and *"The increase in average net
  sales price was due to the implementation of price increases in January 2012 and 2013"*; FY2014,
  *"price increases in January 2014 and 2013"*, and *"recently announced a 15% price increase effective
  in January 2015"*, with *"Our market share was essentially unchanged"*. **That industry-wide price
  action is the conduct the Domestic Wallboard Antitrust Litigation alleged was collusive** (FY2018
  10-K `0001564590-18-014244`, Item 3): class actions from December 2012 alleging that the
  manufacturers *"conspired to increase prices through the announcement and implementation of
  coordinated price increases, output restrictions, and other restraints of trade, including the
  elimination of individual “job quote” pricing"*; a direct-purchaser class certified for purchases
  *"from January 1, 2012 through January 31, 2013"*; Eagle's motion for summary judgment denied on
  2016-02-18; **Eagle and American Gypsum settled for about $39.1M (the two classes) and $6.0M (the
  homebuilders), in agreements "in which the Company and American Gypsum deny all wrongdoing"**; and
  the FY2019 10-K (`0001564590-19-020349`): *"On August 24, 2018, the Antitrust Division notified us
  that the investigation had been closed."* **This run makes no finding that anyone colluded; the
  Department of Justice closed its investigation and the settlements deny wrongdoing.** The Q2 point
  does not depend on that question: whether the 2012-13 price increases were lawful parallel conduct
  or not, **the margin they restored belongs to the industry's pricing conduct, not to Eagle's cost
  position.** [E2-59] names the three ways a commodity producer escapes the equation, *"(a) legally
  through government intervention [...] (b) illegally through collusion, or (c) “extra- legally”
  through OPEC-style foreign cartelization"*, and none of them is a franchise; [E3-61] states the row's
  limit, *"I think you'd have to know the people involved"*, which is exactly what a filing cannot
  show about an oligopoly's pricing. **The recent record is the second half of the equation**: price
  $226.08 in FY2026, down 4%, volume down 7%, industry shipments down 7%, the registrant's *"significant
  excess nameplate capacity"*, and **Eagle's own $330.0M Duke expansion adding 25% (300 MMSF) to its
  largest plant in calendar 2027**, [E2-58]'s *"the rebound to prosperity frequently produces a
  pervasive enthusiasm for expansion"*.

  **Cement is the stronger leg, and the stronger thing against this verdict; it is taken on in full
  here.** It never lost money in the worst U.S. construction collapse since the 1930s: margin
  28.3% (FY2009) to a trough of **15.2% (FY2013)**, 24-28% every year since FY2015, and net price down
  only 16% peak to trough ($96.04 to $80.83) while volume fell about 13%. The mechanism is in Item 1:
  freight (*"generally limits shipments by truck to a 150-mile radius from each plant"*), a national
  capacity deficit (*"the U.S. consumes more cement than can be produced domestically. Imported cement
  consumption represented nearly 23% of total U.S. sales"*), and permitting (*"we anticipate that zoning
  and permitting of new capacity additions will remain difficult. This could potentially enhance the
  value of our existing facilities"*). The FY2008 10-K: *"The U.S. Cement Industry has been sold out for
  the last thirteen years as a result of a domestic capacity deficit."* **That is a durable
  supply-tight structure. But it is the industry's, not Eagle's**, and the competitor row below shows
  it: every U.S. cement producer that files inside that structure earns the same margins or better.
  [E3-43]'s *"supply [...] tight"* case, not the low-cost operator case; the permit that keeps supply
  tight protects every incumbent kiln equally, which is [E2-59]'s regime again (government
  intervention, here on entry rather than price). The price ceiling is the import terminal, which is
  why the registrant says *"no less than approximately 5% to 10% of total U.S. consumption will
  consistently be served by imported cement"*.

  **Concrete and Aggregates** (13% of FY2026 segment revenue) earned 4.3% in FY2026 and lost $8.8M in
  FY2025; **Recycled Paperboard** (22% margin) sells about 40% of its output to Eagle's own wallboard
  plants and 50% under two contracts with other wallboard makers; it is a supplier to the wallboard
  leg and rises and falls with it.
