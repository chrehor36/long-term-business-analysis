## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**
- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language.** AbbVie invents, buys, tests, gets approved and sells prescription medicines, and (about an eighth of sales) sells botulinum-toxin injections and dermal fillers for cosmetic use and eye-care products. The money is made in the years a product is protected by patent and regulatory exclusivity: while no one else may sell the same molecule, the price is set far above the cost of making it (gross margin *"70 | % | 70 | % | 62 | %"* of net revenues in 2025/2024/2023, **after** amortization of acquired intangibles, which *"was included in cost of products sold"* ($7,377M in 2025); before that amortization, about 82% in 2025). Research ($9,096M in 2025), bought candidates (*"Acquired IPR&D and milestones | 5,016"*) and purchased companies are the cost of having something to sell when a current product's protection ends. The medicines are paid for mostly by third parties (Medicare, Medicaid, insurers and pharmacy-benefit managers in the US, with *"substantially all of AbbVie's pharmaceutical product net revenues in the United States"* going *"to these three wholesalers"* (10-K 2025, Note 11); national payers abroad); the cosmetic products are paid for by the patient, through physicians. When a patent ends, *"The expiration or loss of patent protection for a product typically is followed promptly by substitutes that may significantly reduce sales for that product in a short amount of time"* (Item 1A). The record has a worked example of each end: **Humira**, 61% of 2015 net revenues (*"approximately 61 percent of AbbVie's total net revenues in 2015"*), *"lost exclusivity in the United States"* on January 31, 2023, and fell from **$21,237M (2022) to $4,540M (2025)**; and **Skyrizi and Rinvoq**, 42% of 2025 revenue, whose *"United States composition of matter patents ... are expected to expire in 2033"*.
- **The scarce input this business controls.** **The legal exclusivity on each approved product, for a filed and dated term** (risankizumab and upadacitinib compound patents 2033 in the US; *"no generic entry for Rinvoq tablets is expected prior to April 2037 in the United States"* under the September 2025 settlements), **the ability to extend a product's protected life with later patents and settlements** (Humira's US compound patent was *"expected to expire in December 2016"* (10-K 2015) and US biosimilar licences began in 2023: six further years), and behind it **the clinical, regulatory and commercial machine in immunology** that turned one antibody's indications into the next generation's (Skyrizi and Rinvoq displacing Humira in the same diseases; *"Rinvoq demonstrated superiority versus Humira"* in the SELECT-SWITCH study). In aesthetics, the scarce input is the Botox and Juvederm brands among physicians and patients who pay cash. The molecules are scarce while protected and are not afterwards. Whether that is a franchise in [E3-03]'s sense is Q2's question.
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** **The mechanism, yes**: in 2036 AbbVie will still be selling protected medicines at high margins to government and insurer payers and replacing them as they expire. **The products, not on the filer's own dates**: the Skyrizi and Rinvoq compound patents end in 2033, with Rinvoq generics not before April 2037 by settlement; Imbruvica is already under a government-set Medicare price from January 2026, Vraylar and Linzess from January 2027 and Botox from January 2028; Humira is in biosimilar decline (−49% in 2025). The sales AbbVie earns in 2036 will come, in material part, from products that are candidates or recent purchases today (Apogee's zumilokibart; Cerevel's tavapadon; ImmunoGen's Elahere; Vyalev). That is [E4-04]'s question, carried to Q2 under the 2026-09-20 ruling that applies it as a competence limit there.
