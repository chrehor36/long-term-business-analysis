## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**

**Unit economics in my own words, no management language.**
Bath & Body Works buys fragranced consumables — candles, hand soap, body lotion, mist — from about
ninety mostly-domestic vendors, and sells them at roughly **twice cost** through **1,927 small
leased boxes it operates itself** plus its own websites. A store averages **2,851 selling square
feet** and turns **$2.92 million of sales a year**, which is **$1,026 per selling square foot**.
Of every dollar of sales, **43.7 cents is gross profit**; **28.3 cents** goes back out as selling,
marketing and general expense; **15.4 cents** is operating income. Roughly $1.1 billion of operating
cash comes in, about $237 million goes back into stores and systems, and the rest services $3.9
billion of borrowed money, pays a $167 million dividend, and until this year bought back stock.

The engine is **promotional cadence, not price**. The company runs its business on semi-annual
sales and near-continuous multi-buy offers, and it re-scents its assortment constantly so that a
$26 candle feels new. Internationally it does not own anything: **573 partner-operated stores in
45+ countries** under franchise, licence and wholesale, which the filing calls an *"asset-light
business model"* — the partners *"make investments"* and BBWI collects royalty and wholesale margin.

**The scarce input this business controls.**
Not a mineral, not a licence, not a network. It is a **fast, vertically-integrated, predominantly
domestic supply chain fused to a fragrance library and a 40-million-member loyalty file**. The 10-K
names it: *"We benefit from a fast, predominantly domestic, vertically-integrated supply chain."*
That is genuinely scarce among specialty retailers — most import from Asia on 90-to-120-day lead
times and cannot chase a scent that starts working. **It is a real advantage and it is an
operational one, not a structural one**, which is the distinction Q2 has to rule on.

**Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?**
Yes. People will still buy hand soap and scented candles; they will buy them somewhere. The
question of *where* is Q2's, not Q1's. Nothing here is complex or subject to constant change in the
[E3-31] sense: there is no technology risk, no regulated price, no reserve estimate, no float, no
long-cycle contract, and the whole business is described in a 10-K that runs to a normal length in
plain English. **[E4-46]'s five-minute test passes** — this business could be explained to a
non-specialist in five minutes and the explanation would still be right in ten years.

>

**BBWI's spending buys the replacement, and the 10-K says so in its own words.**

The asset the advantage sat on — a mall store fleet — is being described by its owner as

Rhodes Ridge case, not the Coca-Cola case: the spend buys a replacement deposit, it does not defend
the same trademark.** Coca-Cola's advertising defends a trademark that does not move; BBWI's capital
relocates a store fleet that must.

Formula, **we launched on Amazon in the U.S. in February 2026**, allowing more consumers to discover

substitute-comparison engine ever built. Going there converts an owned channel into a rented one and
places the product one scroll from every private-label alternative. It may well be the right
commercial decision. **It is not the behaviour of a franchise; it is the behaviour of a brand that
has concluded it must go where the traffic already is.**

### The four causes of extreme success — [E4-36] — which one is this?

> extreme max/min of one or two variables; a nonlinear combination; extreme performance over many
> factors; or **wave-riding** — *"because only some of them are ownable."* — **[E4-36]**

The record being priced is FY2020–FY2021: revenue from $5,405M to $7,882M in two years, operating
margin to 25.5%. **That was a wave** — a global pandemic that shut restaurants and cinemas, put a
premium on hand soap and sanitiser, and confined a nation to homes it wanted to make smell nice.
**[E3-51]**: *"when a surfer gets up and catches the wave and just stays there, he can go a long,
long time. But if he gets off the wave, he becomes mired in shallows."* Four consecutive years of
decline off the peak is what getting off the wave looks like. **A surfing run is not a moat; the
advantage lives in the wave, not the surfer.**

**And the honest counterweight, stated because [E4-51] requires the other side stated well:**
revenue is still **35% above FY2019** and did not round-trip. BBWI kept most of the pandemic
*volume*. What it did not keep is the *margin* — operating income is only **+8.3%** against FY2019
on **+34.9%** of revenue, and the operating margin is **15.4% against 19.2% pre-pandemic**, below
where it started. **The wave left the volume and took the economics.** A widening moat does not do
that.
