## Q2 — WHY IS THE CASTLE STILL STANDING, and what keeps it standing ten to twenty years? STOP.
The question: "why is that castle still standing? And what’s going to keep it standing or cause it not to be standing five, 10, 20 years from now." **[M1995-038]**. The file closes on the deciding part, Nuclear (Q1). The other parts are read so that what does not decide is known.

**The castle tests, on Nuclear, each with its filing fact.**
1. *The key factors and how permanent they are* **[M1995-038]**. The factors are the ownership of Devonport Royal Dockyard and Rosyth, the nuclear site licences that let submarines be docked, defuelled and refitted there, and the incumbent operation of the two naval bases. The company calls itself the "sole through-life support provider for the UK's nuclear submarine fleet (including the deterrent)" (AGM update, 16 September 2026). The facilities are being extended at the customer's expense: Devonport's 9 Dock was completed and 15 Dock re-opened in FY26; the Major Infrastructure Programme brought £438m of revenue in FY26 (results statement pp.21 to 22); and the MOD's "Royal Oak" programme of August 2026 puts £7bn into Devonport and £15bn into HMNB Clyde over ten years (AGM update). These factors last as long as the UK keeps a nuclear submarine fleet. They do not depend on "the genius of the lord in the castle".
2. *The money test* **[M2011-015]**. A rival with billions could not build a licensed nuclear submarine dock and its regulated workforce in any span that matters; the customer is the only buyer and would not pay for a second one. The answer is "no", which is the See's answer. **The one attacker that matters is the customer**, who can re-let the operating contracts, take work in-house, or split them. The Gateway replacement for FMSP was, at 16 September 2026, still being "finalised", on a six-month bridge from 1 April 2026 (results statement p.22; AGM update). "one competitor is frequently enough to ruin a business" **[M2012-108]**; here the competitor that could ruin it is the buyer itself.
3. *Pricing power and the agony before a rise* **[M2005-020]**. There is none in the ordinary sense. The price is negotiated with one customer. Single-source MOD contracts sit under the SSRO's annually set "baseline profit rate", and the regulations "are subject to review by the UK Government, which could lead to lower returns for industry" (AR2025, principal risk 2). FMSP carried "agreed savings targets" (AR2023, Audit Committee report). **Through the last inflation:** about two-thirds of group revenue had "some measure of protection for inflation"; the rest were firm fixed-price contracts, and labour is about half their cost base (AR2023 strategic report). A UK pay deal cost "c.£25 million" above what contracts recovered in FY23. Nuclear's margin fell to 5.4% in FY23 "reflecting the impact of future inflation", and rose to 9.5% by FY26 (AR2023 Nuclear review; results statement p.22). The price behaviour shows a business that absorbs a shock and recovers it at the next negotiation, not one that sets its price. "If you name some business that has incredible pricing power, you’re talking about a business that’s a monopoly or a near monopoly." **[M2010-092]**: this is a near monopoly whose customer is also a monopoly, so the power runs both ways.
4. *Volume.* Nuclear revenue grew from £1,009.7m in FY22 to £2,070.4m in FY26 (note 3 of AR2023 and AR2026), on submarine support, Cavendish civil nuclear (+18% in FY26) and the infrastructure programme. Contract backlog in Nuclear fell from £1,983.9m to £1,792.7m in FY26, because FMSP ran down to a bridge.
5. *Would the customer still choose it over the low bid?* **[M2017-009]**. For submarine docking and nuclear refit there is no low bid on the public record; no other UK facility is licensed for it. In the other parts the low bid is real: Marine lost the Swedish frigate competition in May 2026; Land and Aviation win and lose re-competed contracts.
6. *Widening or narrowing* **[M1999-108]**. In physical position it is widening: new docks, the HII partnership on US Virginia-class work, and AUKUS. In terms it is under negotiation: the replacement contract is unsigned at the price date. On the evidence, the position is widening and the margin is not shown.
7. *What could destroy, modify or reduce it, five to fifteen years out* **[M2000-014]**. Three things: a customer decision to change who operates the bases; a re-basing of the single-source profit rate; and the company's own contract execution, which in the FY21 review produced write-offs on Vanguard and the dockyard contracts (AR2023, Audit Committee report).
8. *Ask the competitors.* Not possible from the public record. The report names no competitor (below).

**The field's returns over the last full cycle** (section C). The field earns a single-digit return on sales and lives on customer advances. Babcock's own pre-tax return on invested capital (its KPI: underlying operating profit plus joint ventures, over equity plus net debt plus any pension deficit) was 15.2% in FY19 and about 15% in FY20 (AR2020 financial review), 17.4% in FY22, 18.8% in FY23, 26.0% in FY24, 37.0% in FY25 and 30.3% in FY26 (AR2022 to AR2026). Two things are written beside those figures.
- **First**, the cycle is not those years alone. Between FY20 and FY21 the company wrote off £1.7bn of goodwill and took £274.7m of contract-review charges. The return on the capital owners actually put in over the decade is far below the KPI, which is computed on what was left after the write-off. Equity was £2,911.0m in FY18 and £629.3m in FY26, with £1.3bn of negative retained earnings (figures.csv).
- **Second**, invested capital is small because the customer funds the work. Contract liabilities were £786.2m and trade payables £1,041.5m at 31 March 2026, against a total equity of £629.3m. The high return is a return on the customer's money as much as on the owners'.

HII, the closest peer, went from 12% margins to about 5% over the same decade, and its return on equity from about 35% in 2016 to about 12% in 2025 (XBRL facts). The field's returns are ordinary on sales and higher on capital, and fall when the one customer presses.

**The judgment.** The castle around the deciding part stands on the evidence. It is not being filled in, and no attacker but the customer can cross it. What it protects is not a price but a place. The return earned in that place is set in negotiation with a monopsony buyer under a regulated profit-rate regime. The rows treat such a return as a second best, not a castle's excellent return: "there’s no way we get rich on our utility investments. But there’s no way we get poor, either." **[M2009-104]**; the second-best business is entered on "reasonable expectations of earning decent returns on the incremental sums they invest" **[L2009-012]**. Section C's exception sends "A return set by a regulator" to Q3 as a second best, and I read the MOD's single-source regime as that case. **That reading is mine and is confessed:** the SSRO rate is set by a statutory body for single-source contracts. Not every Nuclear contract is single-source, and the Gateway terms are not yet known. Two rows are weighed and not used as verdicts: "it isn’t the kind of business, basically, that we have a 20-year view on" **[M1994-063]**, said of a defence prime in 1994 and limited by its speaker the same day, "just what I’ve said" **[M1994-086]**, under the one-company-row convention. The alternative close, TOO HARD because the Gateway terms are unknown, was weighed and rejected. A castle "whose future cannot be judged" is one whose position is in doubt, "a moat that’s tenuous in any way" **[M2000-019]**. Here the position is not in doubt; the price earned in it is, and the price is weighed at Q3 and Q7.

**VERDICT: IN**, on the deciding part (Nuclear), with its return carried to Q3 as the second best of a regulated price. Carried forward against the name: the full-cycle destruction of capital (Q4, Q6), Marine's fixed-price exposure (Q4, Q9), and the unsigned Gateway contract (Q7, Q9).
