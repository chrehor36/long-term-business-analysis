## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND IT? Where its economics will be in ten years, inside my perimeter. STOP.
**Lines of evidence, chosen by me** (the brief's departure: the dispatcher wrote none). From the equivalent of Item 1, the strategic report's business model and the four sector reviews (AR2026; results statement of 22 June 2026). From the equivalent of Item 1A, the principal risks (AR2025 pp.112 to 124 and the AR2026 equivalent). From the equivalent of Item 7, the financial review and the notes on segments (note 3), revenue recognition, provisions, pensions, share capital and remuneration (AR2026), with the same notes read back through the AR2017 to AR2024 reports for the decade.

**What the business is, from the filings.** Revenue in FY26 was £5,177.7m in four sectors (AR2026 note 3):
- **Marine:** £1,591.5m. It builds Type 31 frigates at Rosyth under a fixed-price design-and-build contract, supports surface ships, makes liquid-gas equipment for civil ships, and works abroad in Canada, Australia, New Zealand and Indonesia.
- **Nuclear:** £2,070.4m. It is "the sole through-life support provider for the UK's nuclear submarine fleet (including the deterrent) and incumbent site operator for HMNB Devonport and Clyde" (AGM update, 16 September 2026). It owns Devonport dockyard, and its Cavendish Nuclear arm does civil new build (Hinkley Point C), generation support and decommissioning.
- **Land:** £1,084.4m. British Army vehicle support (the DSG contract), equipment in southern Africa, and rail.
- **Aviation:** £431.4m. Military flight training and emergency helicopter services.

Defence is £3,822.1m of revenue (74%) and the UK £3,645.0m (70%). The Nuclear sector's revenue is £2,068.5m UK out of £2,070.4m, and 86% of it is UK defence. The UK Government's share of group revenue is not stated as one figure in the notes read; on the sector splits it is well over half.

**The test applied to the deciding part.**
- *Where will it be in ten years?* Understanding means "a reasonable fix on about what the earning power and competitive position will look like in five or 10 years" **[M2012-065]**. The competitive position in ten years can be pictured. The UK nuclear submarine fleet must be maintained where the licensed docks are, and Babcock owns or operates them (Devonport, Clyde, Rosyth). The MOD announced in August 2026 a ten-year, £26bn naval infrastructure programme on those sites (AGM update).
- *The key variables and how predictable they are* **[M1998-044]**. There are three. (1) The volume of UK nuclear support work: tied to a standing fleet and the deterrent, and predictable within a band. (2) The price: set in contracts negotiated with one customer. Some are subject to the Single Source Contract Regulations, under which "the SSRO sets the baseline profit rate for single source contracts let by the MOD on an annual basis" (AR2025 principal risk 2). The rate is not foreseeable to the decimal, but its regime is. (3) Cost-to-complete on long contracts, which drives the reported figures.
- *Do the past statements tell me the future ones?* "do I understand enough about this business so that the financial statements will tell me the information that’s useful to me in making a judgment about what the future financial statements are going to look like" **[M2008-033]**. This is the weakest leg. The FY21 review found material prior-period errors. Among the matters in the former auditor's FY17 and FY18 audits that the regulator sanctioned were the Vanguard refit, the MSDF naval-base contract and the RD57 Rosyth settlement, all in the submarine and dockyard business (AR2023, Audit Committee report). And about £50m a year of revenue comes from re-estimating "performance obligations satisfied or partially satisfied in previous periods" (£50.1m in FY26 excluding Type 31, "broadly in line" with FY25; AR2026 note 3). The current statements since FY22 are read on a consistent basis and say what they are doing. I can read them; I cannot read the decade before FY21 from its own statements, and that weighs at Q4.
- *Important and knowable?* **[M2006-076]** The deciding variable, Babcock's place as the UK's nuclear submarine support provider, is knowable from the filings and the customer's own published plans. The Gateway margin is important and will be knowable when signed.
- *Would the insiders write it down?* **[M2000-105]** Yes. The MOD and Babcock sign multi-year contracts on this work, and the company publishes medium-term guidance. This is not a field whose insiders call the ten-year forecast "too hard".
- *Is the forecast about technology?* The submarine support forecast is about a customer's standing need, not about a technology that could change the economics fast. The company's talk of autonomy and AI (results statement p.5) is about new programmes, not about the deciding part.
- *Doubt.* "if you have doubts about something being into your circle of competence, it isn’t." **[M2002-092]**. My doubt is real but narrow. It concerns the level of margin a monopsony customer will allow, which Q2 and Q7 measure. It does not concern whether the economics can be pictured. The parts that do not decide carry more doubt: Marine's fixed-price shipbuilding has produced two loss charges on one contract in three years. Those parts are carried, not decided on.

**VERDICT: IN, narrowly**, on the deciding part. What matters is "that I understand the economic dynamics of the industry" **[M2011-014]**: one customer, owned and licensed infrastructure, prices set or bounded by that customer. The filing facts: Nuclear's sole-provider position (AGM update, 16 September 2026), its 45.2% share of five-year profit (note 3), and its 99.9% UK revenue. Carried forward: the unreliability of the pre-FY21 statements (to Q4), and the margin set by the customer (to Q2 and Q3).
