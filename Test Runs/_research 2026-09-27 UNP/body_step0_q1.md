## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**
- Unit economics in my own words, no management language: Union Pacific owns a 32,889-route-mile railroad in 23 western states and sells the movement of loaded cars and containers over it. It is paid per car (2025: 8,447,000 carloads at an average $2,749, freight revenue $23,220M, of which $2,300M was fuel surcharge passed through to the shipper), and it spends about 60 cents of every revenue dollar running trains (labour, fuel, equipment rents, purchased services, depreciation), leaving an operating ratio of 59.8% and operating income of $9,846M. The track, bridges, signals, locomotives and cars must be renewed continuously, so about $3.4-3.8bn a year goes back into the plant, more than the $2.5bn of depreciation. What is left after renewal is paid out as dividends ($3,236M) and buybacks ($2,679M in 2025). **Profit is (price per car x cars) minus a largely fixed network cost, less the capital to keep the network whole.** Three traffic groups: Bulk (grain, fertilizer, food, coal: $7,586M), Industrial (chemicals and plastics, metals and minerals, forest products, energy: $8,604M), Premium (automotive and intermodal containers: $7,030M).
- The scarce input this business controls: **the right of way.** A continuous, graded, permitted corridor from the Pacific and Gulf ports to Chicago and the Mexican border, including, in the 10-K's words, *"the only railroad serving all six major Mexico gateways"*. The 10-K states the asymmetry against the other modes itself: *"we must build or acquire and maintain our rail system, while trucks, barges, and maritime operators are able to use public rights-of-way maintained by public entities."* Whether that input is scarce against the other western railroad, and whether it can be priced, is Q2's question, not Q1's.
- Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years? **Yes.** Freight has moved over this network for more than a century and the filings describe no change of character; the traffic mix will keep shifting (coal carloads 1,176,000 in 2018, 797,000 in 2025), and a merger would change the perimeter, not how the business works.
- **The perimeter [E3-31], stated:** the combined company would be the same business on a larger map (Norfolk Southern: 22 eastern states, the same unit economics). The merger does not change the answer to Q1 on either perimeter.
