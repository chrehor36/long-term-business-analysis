- Must the moat be continuously rebuilt? Does success depend on a great manager? **[E4-04]** **The moat is defended, not rebuilt.** The right of way is the same corridor it was a century ago; the spending renews it (ties, rail, ballast, bridges: *"Total road infrastructure replacements | 1,987 | 1,933 | 1,904"*) and does not buy a replacement basis. That is the [E5-23] and [E3-49] class the framework's scope paragraph admits, not the rebuilt-from-zero class. The [E4-04] competence branch (UNKNOWABLE at Q2) was considered and refused: the durability of a rail network can be judged from the filings, and nothing in the business is *"prone to rapid and continuous change"*. **Key-person dependence [E4-23]: none found as a moat defect.** The 2019-2025 operating-ratio gains came under two chief executives (Fritz, then Vena from 2023) with the same method; the network does not go when a CEO goes. (The merger release says Mr Vena *"has committed his intent to remain at Union Pacific for at least the next 5 years"*; that is a Q3 matter.)

**Two eras, and the filer names the first one.** **2003-2014: real revenue per car ex fuel +34.9%**, operating ratio 81.5% to 63.5%, carloads +4.2%. The filings say what it was: *"Since 2004, we repriced approximately two-thirds of our business"* (FY2006 10-K); *"we have repriced approximately 85% of our business since 2004"* (FY2009 10-K), under the heading of *"improving the reinvestibility of our business"*. That is [E3-43]'s demonstration, *"a company's ability to regularly price its product or service aggressively and thereby to earn high rates of return on capital"*, performed in the filed record: the price rose faster than inflation for eleven years while volume held, and the return on the plant roughly doubled (operating income on average net property 9.3% in 2009, 19.4% in 2014, companyfacts, same construction as the row below). **2014-2025: real revenue per car ex fuel -11.3%**, carloads -12.2% (ex coal -2.6%: 7,857,000 to 7,650,000), and the operating ratio went on improving, 63.5% to 59.8%, **on cost, not price**: average employees 47.2 thousand (2014) to 42.0 (2018) to 29.3 (2025). Mix does not explain the second era: holding the 2018 carload mix fixed, revenue per car rose **+12.3% nominal, -12.4% real from 2018 to 2025**, and it fell in real terms in **every one of the ten commodity lines** (`units2.py`): grain -8.7%, fertilizer -6.3%, food -6.0%, coal -21.1%, chemicals and plastics -8.7%, metals and minerals -25.4%, forest products -1.1%, energy and specialized -8.1%, automotive -3.3%, intermodal -15.6%, **with a larger fuel surcharge in 2025 ($2.3bn) than in 2018 ($1.7bn) inside the numerator**. Split inside the second era: real ex-fuel revenue per car was **flat from 2014 to 2019** (+0.8%, nominal +8.8% against CPI +8.0%) and **fell 12.0% from 2019 to 2025**, the years of the CPI surge. The company's own claim is in dollars, not in rates: the Q2 2026 release affirms *"Pricing dollars in excess of inflation dollars"*, which on a cost base of 60% of revenue is consistent with price rising more slowly than CPI.

**[E2-44], both halves, in the filed record.** (1) *"an ability to increase prices rather easily (even when product demand is flat and capacity is not fully utilized)"*: in every volume-down year of the record the nominal ex-fuel price still rose (2009 carloads -16%, ex-fuel ARC +2.6%; 2015 -6%, +2.6%; 2016 -7%, +1.4%; 2019 -6%, +1.1%) except 2020 (-7%, -0.4%), and each MD&A of those years reports *"core pricing gains"*. **In nominal terms, passes; in real terms, it has not done it since 2019.** (2) *"an ability to accommodate large dollar volume increases ... with only minor additional investment of capital"*: **fails outright.** Capital investments were **1.72x depreciation** cumulatively over FY2003-FY2025 ($71,496M against $41,656M, filed cash-flow statements), and the 10-K's own inflation note says *"For capital-intensive companies, inflation significantly increases asset replacement costs for long-lived assets. As a result, assuming that we replace all operating assets at current price levels, depreciation charges (on an inflation-adjusted basis) would be substantially greater than historically reported amounts."*

**[E3-03] criterion by criterion.**
- **(1) needed or desired: passes.** 8.4 million carloads a year; the only railroad at all six major Mexico gateways.
- **(2) no close substitute: passes for the carload traffic tied to the network, fails for part of the rest, in the filer's words.** *"We face competition from other railroads, motor carriers, ships, barges, and pipelines. Our main railroad competitor is Burlington Northern Santa Fe LLC. Its primary subsidiary, BNSF Railway Company (BNSF), operates parallel routes in many of our main traffic corridors."* *"Motor carrier competition exists in all three of our commodity groups."* *"Because of the proximity of our routes to major inland and Gulf Coast waterways, barges can be particularly competitive, especially for grain and bulk commodities"* (the KEX run of 2026-09-26 found that barge business itself a commodity). *"Many movements face product or geographic competition where our customers can use different products (e.g., natural gas instead of coal, sorghum instead of corn) or commodities from different locations."* Intermodal is 40% of carloads and 20% of freight revenue, and its revenue per unit fell 7% in 2024 and 2% in 2025 in nominal dollars. **What is not substitutable is the corridor for the shipper who sits on it**, and that is where the returns are earned.
- **(3) not subject to price regulation: partly fails, in the filer's words, and the regime has not bound.** *"The STB has jurisdiction over rates charged on certain regulated rail traffic"*; *"The STB also continues to explore changes to the methodology for determining railroad revenue adequacy, the possible uses of revenue adequacy in regulating railroad rates"*; the rulemaking on *"reciprocal switching and commodity exemptions"*; and the risk factor that regulators may change *"increasing the amount of our traffic subject to common carrier regulation, ... the prices we charge"*. **The regulated share of revenue is not disclosed in any filing read** (the 10-K, the 10-Qs, the proxy); the document that would state it is the carrier's Waybill and R-1 reporting to the STB, off EDGAR. It does not decide this criterion, because the filed outcome does: under this same regime the operating ratio fell from 87.4% (1997) to 59.8% (2025) and operating income on average net property rose from 9.3% (2009) to 16.7% (2025); the cap has not bound. **It is recorded as [E2-59]'s regime risk, in the cap direction, and it is live**: the regime also decides the merger (below). **The distinction from the MATX run (2026-09-26), where criterion (3) failed outright:** there the filer said the STB had jurisdiction over its domestic ocean rates as a class, *"a rate presumed reasonable within +7.5% / -10% a year"*; here the jurisdiction is over *"certain"* traffic and no band governs the rest.

  **What the row shows.** Union Pacific has the **lowest operating ratio of the six over 2021-2025 (59.9 against 61.5 to 66.3)** and the **highest return on its plant over every window measured**; against its only western rival, BNSF, the gap is **5.8 to 8.2 points of operating ratio in every year 2022-2025 (6.7, 6.7, 8.2, 5.8) and 4-5 points of return on plant**, same construction. The row also shows what the advantage is made of: CSX ran a lower ratio than Union Pacific in 2018-2021 and CPKC in 2015 and 2020, because **the operating method (scheduled railroading) is copied by every Class I**; what is not copied is the western network, of which there are two. The position is a **duopoly per region** in the filings' own words (BNSF *"operates parallel routes in many of our main traffic corridors"*). **The row's limit [E3-61]:** it shows position; it cannot show whether the two western carriers will price like a demented Kellogg. The price series above says that since 2019 neither has raised real price (BNSF's revenue per car/unit fell about 12% in real terms 2018-2025, `Test Runs/2026-09-02 Run - BRK Berkshire Hathaway.md`, from Berkshire's 10-K; Union Pacific -12.4% at fixed mix over the same years).
- Peers named: **5** (BNSF, CSX, Norfolk Southern, CPKC, CN) of the industry's **5 other Class I railroads**: all of them. Not rowed, and named: the substitute modes (truckload carriers, barge lines, pipelines), which are substitutes rather than same-metric peers; the KEX run found barges a commodity. **Class not PROVISIONAL**: every same-industry peer is filed.
- **Untapped pricing power** [E3-33]: **none evidenced.** [E5-28] would require near-monopoly; the filer names a parallel rival in *"many of our main traffic corridors"*, and the real price has fallen since 2019.
- **The attacker's test [E2-45]**: *"how I would like, assuming I had ample capital and skilled personnel, to compete with it."* A new western network cannot be assembled: the filer notes that it *"must build or acquire and maintain our rail system"* while other modes use public roads and water. The attack comes from the other modes and from BNSF, not from entry. **Passes, as a duopoly.**
- **[E3-62], the second step:** who kept the gains of the operating-ratio programme? Since 2018 operating income on plant is up and real price per car is down 12%: **part stuck, part flowed to the shipper.** That is neither the Oshkosh newspaper nor the loom.
- **Direction [E4-32]: not widening in price, widening in cost.** Real price flat 2014-2019 and down 12% 2019-2025; carloads down 12% since 2014; the operating-ratio lead over BNSF widened from 4.1 points (2017) to 8.2 (2024) and narrowed to 5.8 (2025).

### THE STRONGEST EVIDENCE AGAINST THIS VERDICT, stated as its holders would state it [E4-51, E4-26, E3-47]
1. *"Railroads are the regulated class, not the franchise class. Buffett moved BNSF into Berkshire's 'regulated utility' section and wrote that both it and the utility 'need to be assured that they will be allowed to earn reasonable earnings on future capital investments'. Criterion (3) fails, and [E2-59] says regulation caps a franchise and creates neither class: this is the OTTR and MATX precedent."* **Answer:** the passages are real (shelf context, not ledger rows: `Shareholder Letters/2009 Letter.txt` line 498, *"In the future, BNSF results will be included in this 'regulated utility' section"*; `Shareholder Letters/2010 Letter.txt` line 772, *"Both companies are heavily regulated"*). They describe a regime that **can** cap; the filed record shows it has not: the return on the plant roughly doubled under it, and no filing read states a rate prescribed for Union Pacific. OTTR's utility half had allowed returns of 8.75-10.10% set by three commissions and MATX's domestic rates sat in a statutory band; neither describes this filer. The live regime risk is recorded, not dismissed, and it is why the class is NARROW. **If the STB adopts a revenue-adequacy constraint on rates or a broad reciprocal-switching rule, this answer fails** (Q6 condition 1).
2. *"The price series is the answer, and it is [E4-55]'s: units down 12% since 2014 while nominal price holds revenue; real price down 12% at fixed mix since 2018, in every one of ten commodity lines. A franchise raises real price when capacity is idle; this one has not since 2019."* **Answer:** this is the strongest point and it is why the class is NARROW and the direction is not widening. It is not the Precision Steel case: ex-coal volume is -2.6% in eleven years, not -33%, and coal's decline is the filer's named substitution (*"natural gas instead of coal"*), not a lost network. The real decline is concentrated in 2019-2025, the years of the CPI surge, against contracts the filer escalates on its own cost inflation (*"Pricing dollars in excess of inflation dollars"*); 2014-2019 was flat in real terms. **Whether 2019-2025 is aberrational or a slippage is the Q6 monitoring question [E4-17], and it is written there as a condition.**
3. *"The margin gain since 2018 is a cost programme every Class I copied (CSX ran a lower ratio than Union Pacific in 2018-2021), which is [E3-43]'s low-cost operator, 'a business', not a franchise."* **Answer:** the low-cost ratio is shared; the network is not, and the western rival on the same kind of network earns 4-5 points less on its plant in every window. The first era (2003-2014) was price, and price on a network is the franchise demonstration [E3-43] names.
4. *"[E2-44]'s second half fails outright and [E5-20] says of all railroads that depreciation will not keep them in place: a business that must reinvest 1.7x depreciation to stand still is not a franchise."* **Answer:** it fails outright, and that belongs to the great/good/gruesome ranking at Q4 [E4-20, E4-43], which the framework says is *"a RANKING, not a gate"*; the corpus's own railroad passage [E5-20] is a (c) instruction, not a franchise verdict. It is carried to Q4 and Q5 in full.
5. *"The merger changes the perimeter to one where half the network faces CSX and where the STB's merger test is whether the combination enhances competition; conditions to get approval (trackage rights, switching) would narrow the moat you are grading."* **Answer:** recorded, and tested on the combined perimeter below.

**THE COMBINED PERIMETER, tested on the same evidence.** Norfolk Southern is the weaker eastern carrier on both metrics (operating ratio mean 65.9 in 2021-2025, 76.5 in the East Palestine year 2023; return on plant 12.3%), against CSX's 61.9 and 15.7. The combination adds a second duopoly position, weaker than the first, and dilutes the return on plant of the whole. The STB's approval test is described by the companies themselves as one that must *"enhance competition"* (joint release: *"will enhance competition, consistent with the test that will be applied in the review of the transaction"*), and Union Pacific has agreed to accept conditions short of a *"materially burdensome regulatory condition"*. **The class on the combined perimeter is still NARROW**, with one more regime risk: the conditions of approval are unknown until the STB rules. The verdict does not change with the perimeter.

**The analyst's own incentives [E4-27], stated (operator rule 9).** This is an unattended overnight cycle with a budget; an OUT at Q2 is the cheapest outcome, and the three runs before it (NUE, KEX, MATX) closed at Q2, which is a pull toward pattern. Against that, the BRK run's NARROW for BNSF and the name's reputation pull toward IN. I tried hardest against the IN [E4-26]: I built the real price series back to 1997 instead of reading the company's "core pricing gains", held the mix fixed to stop mix hiding a price fall (it found one: -12.4% real since 2018), deflated by the issuing authority's CPI, and wrote the regulated-class argument at full strength with the shelf passages that support it. What survived is an IN on the network and the returns, graded NARROW on the price.
