## Q1: CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**

- **Unit economics in my own words, without management's language.** Winnebago builds boxes
  and boats in Indiana, Iowa, Minnesota and Florida and sells them **wholesale to independent
  dealers**, who pay cash, but only because a bank lends the dealer nearly the whole invoice
  against the unit itself ("floorplan"). WGO does not sell to the person who camps; it sells
  to the person who *stocks*. Revenue is therefore **dealer ordering**, which is retail demand
  plus or minus the dealer's decision to add or shed inventory, and that second term is
  larger than the first over any two-year stretch. Cost is bought components: frames and axles
  (Lippert/LCI), furniture and surfaces (Patrick), chassis (Ford, Freightliner, Mercedes),
  appliances (Dometic). WGO's own value-add is design, assembly labour, brand and the dealer
  relationship. Gross margin **13.0%** in FY2025; operating margin **2.0%**. To win a dealer's
  floor space WGO gives "discounts and allowances," and when demand falls the discounts rise. That
  is why FY2025 towable ASP fell 5.4% while units fell only 2.7%. And it **guarantees the
  dealer's bank**: if a dealer fails, WGO buys the units back. That standing guarantee was
  **$1,640.0M** at FY2025 year-end, against a market cap of $850.6M.
- **The scarce input the business controls:** floor space in ~2,000 independent dealer lots,
  and inside that the brand names: Grand Design (the strongest, built from nothing since the
  2016 acquisition), Winnebago (the most recognised name in American RVs), Newmar (premium
  Class A), Barletta (pontoons, #2 and growing), Chris-Craft. It is a **rented** scarcity: the
  dealer reallocates the space every model year, and the filings show WGO paying to keep it.
- **Ten years:** yes, the fundamentals will look the same. Recreational vehicles will still be
  assembled from bought components in Elkhart County and sold through franchised dealers on
  floorplan. Nothing here is complex or subject to constant change [E3-31]. The *level* of
  demand is unpredictable; the *mechanism* is not.
- **VERDICT: [x] IN.** The business is legible in one page and the ten-year mechanism is
  stable. The cyclical level lands at Q4/Q5 as range width; the franchise question is Q2's.

## Q2: IS IT A FRANCHISE? **[E3-03]**

> a franchise is a product or service that "(1) is needed or desired; (2) is thought by its

- Needed or desired **[x]**, 45 million American households camp; the category is real.
- Not price-regulated **[x]**. None.
- **No close substitute: [ ] FAILS, and the filed record is the evidence.** A 30-foot travel

### The [E2-44] two-characteristic test: **0 of 2**
1. **Can it raise prices when demand is flat and capacity is not fully utilised?** **No, and
   the filings say the opposite in their own words.** FY2024 10-K, towable: revenue fell on
   *"a reduction in average selling price per unit related to product mix and **targeted price
   reductions**."* FY2025 10-K, motorhome: revenue fell on *"lower unit volume and **higher
   discounts and allowances**."* Towable ASP $43,038 (FY2022) → **$38,797** (FY2025), −9.9%.
   Q3 FY2026: towable ASP +0.7% while units −26.5%. Price is being given back into slack
   demand. This is the exact inverse of the test.
2. **Can it grow dollar volume with only minor additional capital?** **No.** Revenue rose 41%
   FY2019→FY2025, and it was bought: Newmar (Nov 2019), Barletta (Aug 2021), Lithionics (Apr
   2023, $87.5M) took goodwill + intangibles from $531M to **$941M**, 77% of book equity,

### [E2-45] the attacker's test: answered empirically
The question is how one would attack this with ample capital and skilled personnel. The answer
is that it has been attacked continuously for fifty years and the barrier is a leased building
in Elkhart. **Thor** did it by acquisition (Jayco, Keystone, Heartland, Dutchmen, Airstream,
Erwin Hymer) and is roughly 3× WGO's size. **Forest River** did it and then sold itself to
Berkshire Hathaway in 2005, since when it has had a permanent-capital parent, no quarterly
guidance and no need to defend a stock price. **WGO itself did it**. Grand Design was a
five-year-old start-up when WGO bought it in 2016 and is now the largest part of the towable
segment, which proves the entry barrier is low from the inside as well as the outside.

### [E2-58] the commodity-end doctrine: this is the equation, exactly
*"persistent over-capacity without administered prices (or costs) equals poor profitability"*
and long-term profitability set by *"the ratio of supply-tight to supply-ample years."*
Elkhart's assembly capacity was built for the ~600,000-unit shipment year of 2021; WGO's own
June 2026 forecast for calendar 2026 is **290,000–310,000 units**. That is the over-capacity
term. FY2025's **2.0% operating margin** is the equation's output, and *"Nothing fails like
success"* is how it happened: the 2021 boom financed the capacity that now sits idle across
the industry.
The one exception the corpus allows is *"a cost advantage that is both **wide and
sustainable**"*: WGO has neither. It is the #2 or #3 OEM in a business where the #1 is three
times its size and the other major has Berkshire's balance sheet behind it, and it buys the
same frames from the same two suppliers everyone else buys them from.

### [E5-24] did the acquisitions buy a moat, or just revenue?
- **Grand Design (2016, ~$500M): bought a moat, and it worked.** Best-in-class dealer and
  owner satisfaction, and it is the profitable core of the towable segment.
- **Chris-Craft (2018): bought revenue, and lost it.** Goodwill **impaired $30.3M in FY2024**
  on the Chris-Craft reporting unit, the filed verdict on that deal, written by the company.
- **Newmar (Nov 2019, ~$344M): bought revenue into a shrinking category.** Class A unit
  deliveries 2,997 (FY2018) → **1,211** (FY2025), −60%, with Newmar included the whole way.
- **Barletta (Aug 2021, ~$270M + earnouts): the best of the recent lot.** Marine revenue
  $367.8M in FY2025 at a **7.5% operating margin**, the highest of the three segments, with
  units **+11.7%**, the only unit growth anywhere in the company. A genuinely good purchase.
- **Lithionics (Apr 2023, $87.5M): vertical integration**, not yet visible in the numbers.
- **Net:** two good deals, one impaired, one into a collapsing category, and total RV units
  below the FY2019 level with two more brands inside the box. **The acquisitions bought
  revenue and a decent marine business; they did not buy a moat, and $941M of goodwill and
  intangibles now sits against $283.6M of tangible book.**

### THE CENTRAL QUESTION, ANSWERED: is the 13.81% real, a liquidation artifact, or a cyclical-peak artifact?

**Test 1, the ALG working-capital test [E4-41], run explicitly as tasked.** *A boom absorbs
working capital and a bust releases it, so recent operating cash flow can look strong precisely
because the business is shrinking.* Working-capital change **inside** each year's OCF, from the

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| WC change in OCF | **+$150.0M** | **−$106.3M** | **−$111.9M** | +$12.1M | −$13.5M | +$13.3M |

**The boom-absorption half is confirmed and is enormous: −$218.1M across FY2021-22.** The
bust-release half is **NOT** confirmed. Across FY2023-25 the *net* working-capital contribution
was **+$11.9M on $567.3M of cumulative operating cash flow, 2.1%.** Inventory did release
$131.5M, but accounts payable (−$83.0M) and accrued liabilities (−$117.9M) took nearly all of
it straight back out: WGO paid down its suppliers as fast as it liquidated its stock.

the series is FY2020, $270.4M of OCF containing $150.0M of COVID-shutdown working-capital
release. It sits outside every window the screen used.)* This finding runs against the
hypothesis this run started with, and it is recorded that way per [E4-26].

**Test 2. The boom-earnings test [E4-41], "normalize the mean DOWN for luck."** This is where
the 13.81% comes from, and the arithmetic is unambiguous. The screen's 3-year "bottom boundary"
window is FY2023-25, and:

> **FY2023 alone supplies $200.4M of the window's $358.4M three-year total, 56% of a
> "bottom boundary" from a single year.**

FY2023 is not a normal-demand year. It is the year that **delivered the FY2022 backlog**: total

8.6%, net income $215.9M. Strip it and the two years fully clear of boom backlog produce
**$61.6M–$79.0M**. **The 13.81% is a cyclical-peak artifact, specifically a boom-backlog
artifact, and the window, not the working capital, is what carries it.**

trough that reverts?** The bull case is real and deserves its best statement: RV industry

1. **The pre-boom comparison kills it.** FY2018 owner earnings were $47.2M–$56.7M and FY2019's
