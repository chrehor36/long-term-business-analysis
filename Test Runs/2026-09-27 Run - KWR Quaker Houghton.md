## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**
- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language.** Quaker Houghton buys about 3,000 raw materials (*"animal fats, vegetable oils, mineral oils, oleochemicals, surfactant agents and various chemical compounds"*, most of them *"fourth to sixth generation derivatives of crude oil and natural gas"*), blends them in its own plants to its own formulas, and sells the fluids that metal makers and metal workers use up in production: rolling lubricants for steel and aluminum mills, metal-removal (cutting and grinding) fluids for machine shops and car-parts plants, hydraulic fluids, cleaners, corrosion inhibitors, forming, drawing, forging and quenching fluids, die-cast releases, greases, and (since Dipsol) surface-treatment and plating chemistry. In 2025 metal removal fluids were 19.0% of sales, rolling lubricants 18.3%, hydraulic fluids 12.2%, surface solutions 10.9%. **The money is the spread between what the blend sells for and what the oils and chemicals in it cost**, earned per litre consumed, so revenue follows the customers' production (*"The Company’s financial performance is generally correlated to the volume of global production within the industries it serves"*) and the margin follows the lag between raw-material costs and selling prices (*"We generally attempt to pass through changes in the prices of raw materials to our customers, but we may be unable to do so (or may be delayed in doing so)"*). Sales are made mostly by its own staff, who work inside customer plants (*"The Company’s employees typically visit the plants of customers regularly, work on site"*), and under Fluidcare contracts it manages a plant's whole fluid inventory, including third-party products. Customers are spread: the five largest were about 11% of 2025 sales, the largest about 3%. Gross margin 36.0% (2025), segment operating margin 23.7% (448.4 of 1,888.6) before corporate costs, amortization, restructuring and impairments.
- **The scarce input this business controls.** The filer says it plainly: *"The Company principally relies on its proprietary formulas and its applications know-how and experience to meet customer needs"*, with *"a limited number of patents"*; so the scarce input, if there is one, is **formulas qualified inside customers' processes plus the technical staff on the customer's floor**, and the scale of the largest supplier (*"the Company estimates it holds the leading global position in the market for industrial process fluids"*). Whether that is a franchise in [E3-03]'s sense is Q2's question.
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** **Yes, on the filings.** Steel and aluminum will be rolled and parts machined in 2036 with fluids that are consumed and reordered; the Legacy Quaker record from 2009 to 2018 and the combined record since 2019 show the same mechanism in every year (volume with industrial production, price chasing raw materials). The named drift is the product mix (surface solutions from 5.0% to 10.9% of sales in two years, by purchase) and the automotive shift away from engine and transmission machining, which the filer does not quantify; neither changes how the money is made.
- **Relatively simple and stable in character [E3-31]? Yes.** A formulator's spread over petrochemical and oleochemical inputs, sold per litre to industrial plants, can be stated in five minutes **[E4-46]**, and the filer decomposes every year's sales change into volume, price and mix, currency and acquisitions. The same reading gave Q1 IN at H.B. Fuller on 2026-09-26, another formulator of petrochemical inputs sold into customers' processes.
- **VERDICT: [x] IN**: the money is made visibly, as a spread over raw materials on fluids consumed in customers' production, and the filer reports the parts of every year's change. **IN is not a finding on the franchise.**

- **(1) Needed or desired [x]: passes.** Metal cannot be rolled, cut, ground, formed or quenched at production speed without process fluids; net sales $1,888.6M in 2025 and $1,013.0M in H1 2026.
