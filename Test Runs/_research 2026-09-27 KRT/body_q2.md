## Q2 — IS IT A FRANCHISE? **[E3-03]**

*Every Karat figure below is read from the filed statements (the 2019 S-1 for FY2017-FY2018, the 2021 prospectus for FY2019-FY2020, the 10-Ks for FY2021-FY2025 and the Q2 2026 10-Q; accessions in Step 0) or from SEC companyfacts checked against those statements. Peer figures are from each peer's own SEC companyfacts, fetched fresh for this run, through `peers/row.py`; outputs `peers/<TICKER>_row.txt`, in `Test Runs/_research 2026-09-27 KRT/`.*

**The hypotheses I formed while reading, both stated so they can be attacked [E4-26].** *(a) Karat is a sourcing-and-logistics franchise: a founder-built importer that learned to buy cheaper in Asia than domestic makers can produce, carries about 8,400 SKUs, keeps 99% of its top hundred customers, custom-prints for national chains, and has earned 14-21% pre-tax on its operating capital every year since FY2020 with almost no debt of its own; its margins rose from 25-30% gross before FY2020 to 37-39% in FY2023-FY2025.* *(b) Karat is a price-taking distributor of commodity disposables: its selling prices rose with ocean freight in FY2021-FY2022 and were cut in each of FY2023, FY2024 and FY2025 while its volumes grew; its suppliers are not exclusive and make the same goods for its rivals; the filer says in its own words that the market sets its prices, that falling costs start price wars and that cheap freight lowers the barrier to entry; the FY2023-FY2025 margin coincides with the collapse of ocean freight and a strong dollar against the New Taiwan dollar.* Each is tested on the filings below.

**[E3-03], criterion by criterion.** *"An economic franchise arises from a product or service that: (1) is needed or desired; (2) is thought by its customers to have no close substitute and; (3) is not subject to price regulation."*
- **(1) Needed or desired [x]: passes.** Restaurants, chains and distributors buy disposables continuously; FY2025 net sales $467.7M, *"an increase of 10.7% in net sales amount and 11.2% in volume"* (10-K FY2025, MD&A).
- **(3) Not subject to price regulation [x]: passes.** No product is priced by a government in any filing read. Regulation bears on what may be sold (the foam and plastic-bag bans, the packaging-responsibility laws) and on the import cost (tariffs, anti-dumping duties); neither sets Karat's selling price.
- **(2) Thought by its customers to have no close substitute: FAILS, in the filer's own words, in every year read.**
  - **Who sets the price.** FY2025 10-K and Q2 2026 10-Q, MD&A: *"Since negotiated sales contracts and the market largely determine the pricing for our products, we are, at times, limited in our ability to raise prices and pass through any impacts of inflation to our costs. There can also be lags between cost inflation and the implementation of price increases, which could negatively impact our gross margin. Conversely, periods of deflation, where raw material costs decrease, may create pricing pressure and start price wars, potentially requiring us to lower prices, which could also affect our gross margin."*
  - **The competition, Item 1A FY2025**: *"The single-use disposable foodservice products industry is extremely competitive and highly fragmented. Many of the companies that compete in our industry are significantly larger with greater resources, have greater brand recognition and have a larger product offering. We may be unsuccessful in our efforts to compete against such large and established companies. In addition, our current or potential competitors may offer products at a lower price"*; and *"Competitors, many of whom are larger and have greater financial resources than we do, may respond to challenging market conditions by lowering prices in an attempt to attract customers."*
  - **The barrier to entry is the freight rate, in the filer's words** (MD&A, FY2025 and Q2 2026): *"Steady or dropping ocean freight could yield significant opportunities for us to expand our margin. However, it could also reduce the barrier of entry, intensifying the competition."*
  - **The supply is not Karat's**: *"our arrangements with our suppliers and manufacturers are not exclusive. As a result, they could produce similar products for our competitors, some of which could potentially purchase products in significantly greater volume. Our competitors could also enter into restrictive or exclusive arrangements with our suppliers and manufacturers"*. No patent is claimed; *"12 active trademarks ... and 5 registered copyrights"*.
  - **The customers shop**: *"Our reputation of being a reliable supplier with strong customer service and competitive prices has led to consistent requests for proposals from our existing customers as they look for new and innovative products and diversify their supplier base."* A customer that issues requests for proposals to diversify its supply base is describing close substitutes.

**What the record shows.** Price followed landed cost up (FY2021-FY2022, *"to pass through"*, *"to partially offset"*) and followed it down (FY2023, *"actively passed on savings ... to customers"*), and then **fell for two more years in which volume rose** (FY2024 −$25.2M price against +$36.7M volume and mix; FY2025 −$6.5M against +$39.7M volume), for competitive reasons the filer names. Across FY2023-FY2025 the price bridges sum to **−$65.0M**, against the FY2022 increase of $43.7M. In FY2025 tariffs doubled and the price still fell. That is the reverse of [E2-44]'s test: not prices raised while demand is flat, but prices cut while demand grows. **[E4-37], the agony metric: present in the filer's own vocabulary** (*"limited in our ability to raise prices"*, *"lags between cost inflation and the implementation of price increases"*, *"price wars"*).

**[E3-03]'s demonstration, [E3-43]: *"a company’s ability to regularly price its product or service aggressively and thereby to earn high rates of return on capital."*** The second half is present and is the strongest fact for hypothesis (a): **pre-tax return on operating capital including goodwill 18.6% (FY2020), 14.4, 16.3, 20.6, 17.7, 20.2% (FY2025)** (`peers/KRT_row.txt`; goodwill is $3.5M, so the tangible figures are within half a point: 19.0-21.0%). The first half, the ability to price aggressively, is refused by the price record above. [E3-43] joins the two with *"thereby"*: the returns are to be the product of the pricing, and the filings attribute Karat's returns to landed cost instead. FY2023 is the filer's own attribution: *"The year-over-year margin expansion is largely due to a significant decrease in total ocean freight and import costs, which as a percentage of net sales was 7.5% during the year ended December 31, 2023, down from 14.5% during the year ended December 31, 2022. Additional margin expansion factors also included strong United States Dollar against Taiwan New Dollar, and our efforts to scale back manufacturing in the U.S. in favor of imports which carry higher margin"*. Two of the three named causes (freight and the currency) are external; the third (buying rather than making) is a choice any importer can make. **Before the freight collapse, in the four years the filings allow (FY2017-FY2020), operating margin was 4.3%, 1.2%, 2.6% and 9.4%.**

**The remaining Q2 tests.**
- **[E3-43]'s classification.** *"In contrast, “a business” earns exceptional profits only if it is the low-cost operator or if supply of its product or service is tight. Tightness in supply usually does not last long."* Karat's case for exceptional profit is a low-cost case (*"asset-light"*, imports over domestic production, Taiwan and now Malaysia and Vietnam), which is [E3-43]'s *"low-cost operator"* door; whether it is the low-cost operator in the trade cannot be shown from the row (the largest competitors are private), and the filer itself says its competitors are *"significantly larger"* and *"could potentially purchase products in significantly greater volume"* from the same factories. The door is claimed, not demonstrated. *"And a business, unlike a franchise, can be killed by poor management."*
- **[E2-58], the commodity equation.** *"In many industries, differentiation simply can’t be made meaningful. A few producers in such industries may consistently do well if they have a cost advantage that is both wide and sustainable. By definition such exceptions are few."* The filer claims differentiation (eco-friendly lines at 34.1% of sales, custom printing, breadth of catalogue); the price record answers whether customers pay for it: prices cut in three consecutive years, the eco-friendly share moving from 33.6% to 34.1%. A cost advantage that the filer attributes to freight rates and the Taiwan dollar is neither wide by demonstration nor sustainable by construction. **[E3-62], the second step**: FY2023's freight savings were *"actively passed on ... to customers"* in the same year, which is the corpus's case of gains that *"flow through to the customer"*.
- **[E4-04], the durability question.** Not the limb this verdict rests on. The perimeter close (UNKNOWABLE at Q2) applies only to *"A name that passes [E3-03] and whose durability cannot be judged from filings"*, which this name does not; it is refused in writing.
- **[E4-23], key-person dependence.** The co-founders run the company (Alan Yu, chief executive; Marvin Cheng), and the procurement agent, the landlord of two facilities and the guarantor of the term debt are all connected to stockholders (Q3 material below). No filing says the business depends on one named person beyond the standard key-personnel risk factor; recorded as no finding at Q2.
- **Untapped pricing power [E3-33] / [E5-28]: no.** *"If you name some business that has incredible pricing power, you’re talking about a business that’s a monopoly or a near monopoly"*; the filer describes an *"extremely competitive and highly fragmented"* trade and cut its prices three years running.
- **[E2-53], dominance.** Not claimed; no customer is 10% of sales and the filer calls its rivals larger.
- **[E2-45], the attacker's test.** *"how I would like, assuming I had ample capital and skilled personnel, to compete with it."* The filer answers it: the same non-exclusive factories are open to an attacker, the barrier is the freight rate (*"could also reduce the barrier of entry, intensifying the competition"*), and Karat itself built its supply base from *"only a handful of vendors initially to nearly 150 active vendors"*, which an attacker with capital could repeat.
- **[E3-46], the second question is a number.** Pre-tax return on operating capital 14.4-20.6% FY2020-FY2025: good, and earned in years whose margin the filer attributes chiefly to freight and currency.
- **[E4-32], direction.** Margin up since FY2020, returns level at 16-21% since FY2022; the price series moved against the company in FY2023-FY2025 while volume grew. **[E4-55], units**: the filer publishes a volume change (*"11.2% in volume"* FY2025) but no unit series; volume up and price down is the direction the filings show.
- **[E4-36], which cause of success?** The record's success is the fourth cause, **wave-riding**: the pandemic take-out wave (FY2020-FY2021 sales +31% and +23%) and then the freight wave in reverse (FY2023). The framework's reading of Munger's surfing model **[E3-51]** governs: a surfing run is not a moat, because the advantage lives in the wave. No extreme on one variable is demonstrated against the trade.

**THE STRONGEST EVIDENCE AGAINST THE VERDICT BELOW, hunted hardest [E4-26, E3-41].** The verdict below is OUT, so the case for IN is set out as its holders would state it:
1. **The returns are real and steady**: 14.4-20.6% pre-tax on capital in six consecutive years, with no borrowing outside the real-estate entity, at twice the operating margin of the largest US distributor and far above the SEC-filing maker on capital.
2. **The customers stay**: *"Our major customer retention rate, defined as year-over-year retention of our top 100 customers, was 99% from 2024 to 2025"*; one national chain grew *"from less than $1.0 million on 5 SKUs for the year ended December 31, 2016, to approximately $12.6 million on more than 20 SKUs for the year ended December 31, 2025, including a paper bag contract signed with forecast annualized revenue of approximately $17.0 million"*; floor-stocking agreements with chains give *"some visibility into future revenue"*.
3. **The margin held through the tariff shock**: gross margin 36.8% in FY2025 with duties and tariffs doubled, because the company moved its buying from China to Malaysia and Vietnam within a year; that is a sourcing skill, and skill in sourcing is the low-cost door [E3-43] names.
4. **The price cuts were chosen, not forced**: FY2023's cuts passed on freight savings, which a distributor protecting its customer relationships might do by choice.

**Answered.** (1) is accepted as fact and is the strongest point; [E3-43] asks for returns *"thereby"* earned from pricing power, and the filer attributes FY2023's step to freight and the currency, and the four years before it earned 1.2-9.4% operating margins. (2) is weighed, not waved away: retention of customers whose prices were cut in three consecutive years is retention at the market's price, and the same 10-Ks say those customers issue requests for proposals *"to diversify their supplier base"*. (3) is a real skill and is recorded as such; it is the skill of every importer in the trade (the factories are shared), it did not stop the price falling in the tariff year, and the filer's own answer to tariffs is *"we may not be able to pass those cost increases through to our customers"*. (4) is partly right for FY2023; it cannot explain FY2024 and FY2025, where the filer's reason is that *"the overall pricing environment remained competitive"*. **[E3-47]** (a wrongly closed file inside the circle of competence is the costliest error) was weighed against OUT in writing; the reversal conditions below name the filed evidence that would reopen the file.
