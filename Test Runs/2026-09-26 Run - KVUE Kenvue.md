## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**
- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language.** Kenvue makes and sells branded over-the-counter medicines (acetaminophen and ibuprofen pain relievers, antihistamines, nicotine gum and patches, antidiarrhoeals, eye drops), skin and hair products (face and body moisturisers, cleansers, sunscreen, shampoo, a minoxidil hair-regrowth product), and household health staples (mouthwash, adhesive bandages, baby shampoo and lotion, nappy cream, sanitary pads and tampons). About 60% of its volume is made in its own plants and the rest by contract manufacturers (*"Our in-house manufacturing footprint delivered approximately 60% of our sales volume during the fiscal year 2025"*). It sells to retailers, pharmacies and distributors in about 165 countries; one customer takes about 12% of sales and the top ten about 41% (*"One of our customers accounted for approximately 12% of total Net sales ... Our top 10 customers represented approximately 41% of total Net sales"*). The money is the gap between the shelf price of a trusted branded pack and the cost of generic molecules, surfactants and packaging: gross margin **56.0% (2023), 58.0% (2024), 58.1% (2025)**, then heavy advertising and selling costs, leaving an operating margin of about **12-19%** as filed (operating income $2,414M on $15,124M in 2025; $1,841M in 2024 after $578M of impairments). The active ingredients are mostly off-patent and sold by every generic maker; what the customer pays extra for is the name and the regulatory standing of the product.
- **The scarce input this business controls.** **The names**, a century old in several cases (Band-Aid, Johnson's, Listerine) and in OTC medicine carrying the doctor-and-pharmacist recommendation built when the products were prescription or hospital brands (Tylenol, Zyrtec, Nicorette); plus the regulatory dossiers (monographs, new drug applications, the OTC switch history) that let a product make a claim on the label. The molecules are not scarce. Whether the names are scarce in [E3-03]'s sense (*"thought by its customers to have no close substitute"*) is Q2's question.
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** **The mechanism, yes.** People will buy pain relievers, mouthwash, bandages and sunscreen in ten years, through the same concentrated retail channel and increasingly online. What is less certain is the standing of particular names: the filings record Skin Health and Beauty volume falling in the United States (*"carryover effects from prior fiscal year execution challenges, as well as current fiscal year competitive pressures"*, 2024), and the acetaminophen allegations of September 2025 against Tylenol, the largest single brand (*"The foregoing actions may depress sales of products containing acetaminophen"*). Those are Q2 and Q4 questions, not a failure to understand how the money is made.
- **Relatively simple and stable in character [E3-31]?** Yes: buy commodity ingredients, make or contract-make branded health products, advertise, sell through retailers. The regulatory layer (FDA monographs, label changes) is a feature of the category, not a technology race. Not a business that would take months to understand [E4-46]. The carve-out perimeter is the one complication, and it is a documents question answered in Step 0, not a competence question.
- **VERDICT: [x] IN**: the money is made visibly, as a branded margin over generic inputs sold through a concentrated retail channel. **IN is not a finding on the franchise.**
