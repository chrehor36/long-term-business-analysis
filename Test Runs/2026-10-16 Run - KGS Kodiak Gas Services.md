Kodiak owns natural-gas compressors, about 4.5 million horsepower in 4,736 units at 2025 year-end, 80% of the horsepower in units above 1,000 hp, and rents them with its own crews to producers and midstream companies under fixed monthly fees, three-to-five-year primary terms for large units, month-to-month after (FY2025 10-K, Item 1). It takes no title to gas or oil (Item 7A, Commodity Price Risk). The work is "trying to identify the key variables in that particular business" **[M1998-044]**; here they are four: revenue-generating horsepower; the monthly rate per horsepower; the cost to run a horsepower (labor, parts, lubricants); and the capital per horsepower with its life. The demand for compression follows the volume of gas produced and gathered in the Permian and Eagle Ford, which is a matter of production, not of a fast-changing technology: the units are reciprocating compressors on gas engines or electric motors (Item 1, Sustainability), a mature machine whose decade ahead can be pictured.
- **Can it be decided off the figures?** Yes in the sense the rows ask: "if you can’t make a decision on something like PetroChina off the figures, forget about going further" **[M2008-069]**. The ten-year economics turn on production volumes in two basins and on the field's price behaviour; neither is a technology race. A dependence on the energy cycle is "never by itself a STOP at Q1 or Q2" under the framework's commodity paragraph; the speakers "don’t think we can predict commodity prices" **[M2016-025]**, and the business is judged on its castle and cost position at Q2.

- **The question** **[M1995-038]**: "why is that castle still standing? And what’s going to keep it standing or cause it not to be standing five, 10, 20 years from now." The castle the filer claims is large-horsepower compression in the Permian under three-to-five-year fixed-fee contracts, mechanical availability "over 99.5%" and "a five-year 99% average fleet utilization" (IPO prospectus, accession `0001193125-23-180561`, Business), with 97.7% fleet utilization at 2025 year-end (FY2025 10-K, Item 7, Operational Highlights).

- **The attacker with money** **[M2011-015]**: "if I had a hundred million dollars and I wanted to go in and take on See’s Candy, could I do it? [...] If the answer had been yes, we wouldn’t have done it." Here the answer is in Kodiak's own history. Kodiak Services was "formed in 2011" and in about a decade "built and operated a substantial fleet" that leads the Permian (FY2025 10-K, Item 1, Overview), with private-equity money (acquired by EQT funds on 2019-02-08, prospectus, note 1). The customer can be the attacker too: producers "may choose to vertically integrate their operations by purchasing and operating their own compression fleets" (Item 1A). A field that one well-funded entrant entered and came to lead inside a decade is one where "you better be running very fast" **[M2012-106]**; the money test fails on the filer's own record.

- **Pricing power and the price behaviour** **[M2005-020]**: "you learn a lot about the durability of the economics of a business by observing the behavior of [...] the price behavior." In the tight market of 2023 to 2026 the field raised prices: Kodiak's contract revenue rose 14.2% in 2025 on "price increases" and more horsepower (FY2025 10-K, Item 7); USAC's revenue per horsepower per month rose 4.7% to $21.38 (USAC FY2025 10-K, accession `0001522727-26-000015`). Kodiak's own filings contain no downturn year (its audited statements begin with 2021), and its claim of 99% average utilization over the five years to 2022 is the one piece of evidence that its large-horsepower Permian niche held volume through 2020; its 2020 pricing is not filed. Kodiak's own Competition paragraph lists "price" among the grounds on which it competes, and its rivals may "adopt more aggressive pricing policies" (Item 1).
