## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND IT? Where its economics will be in ten years, inside my perimeter. STOP.
- **The test as the draft states it.** Understanding is "a reasonable fix on about what the earning power and competitive
  position will look like in five or 10 years" **[M2012-065]**, asked as "can I understand it?" **[M1995-051]**, with
  the precision "a reasonable probability of being able to asses where the business will be in 10 years"
  **[M2000-037]**. Understanding the product is not enough; it is "the predictability of the economics of the situation
  10 years out" **[M2000-104]**.
- **What the business is, from the filing.** Visa runs VisaNet, a network that authorizes, clears and settles card
  payments among issuing banks, acquiring banks and sellers in "more than 200 countries and territories"; it "is not a
  financial institution" and "does not issue cards, extend credit or set rates and fees for account holders" (10-K
  FY2025, Item 1 and Note 1). It takes no credit risk on the cardholder; it earns a fee on volume (service revenue), a
  fee per transaction (data processing), a fee on cross-border volume and currency conversion, and value-added services,
  less client incentives paid to banks and merchants (10-K FY2025, Item 7).
- **The key variables and whether they are foreseeable** **[M1998-044]**. Two variables carry the economics: (1)
  payments volume and processed transactions, and (2) what Visa keeps per dollar of volume, net of incentives. The record:
  payments volume (twelve months to June) $8,137B (FY2018) to $13,894B (FY2025), **7.9% a year**; processed transactions
  212,579M (FY2023) to 257,545M (FY2025), 10% a year; net revenue $20,609M to $40,000M, **9.9% a year**; net revenue per
  dollar of payments volume **25.3 basis points** in FY2018, 25.1 in FY2020 (the pandemic year), 27.0 in FY2023 and
  **28.8 in FY2025** (10-K FY2020 `0001403161-20-000070`, FY2023 `0001403161-23-000099`, FY2025 `0001403161-25-000089`;
  ratio is my division of the filed figures). The first variable is a forecast about customers, the spending of billions
  of people and the cards their banks give them, not about a technology **[M2017-019]**, **[M2023-030]**; the volume fell
  only once in the years read (cross-border and travel in FY2020) and recovered within a year. The second variable is
  where the doubt sits: the take is set by contract with banks (incentives), by regulators (interchange caps, which bear on a fee that is "Generally, IRFs are paid by acquirers to issuers", 10-K FY2025 Item 1,
  not on Visa's own fees, but move volume, routing and the incentives Visa must pay) and by courts (the DOJ
  debit case).
- **Do the past statements tell me the future ones** **[M2008-033]**? Largely yes: one segment, one model, a cash flow
  statement that follows net income, no credit book. The litigation is the one line that does not follow; it is lumpy and
  has recurred for twenty years.
- **Would the insiders write it down** **[M2000-105]**? The test that separates the work from the nature. The 10-K
  itself calls the industry one of "dynamic and rapid change" (contrary item 1). But the insiders' disagreement is over
  how fast new rails take share at the edges (real-time account-to-account systems in some countries, stablecoins in
  cross-border and remittance), not over whether card volume ten years out will be larger than today; Visa is building on
  the newcomers rather than being bypassed in the filing's own account ("we have facilitated the spend of more than $35
  billion in crypto and stablecoin", 10-K FY2025 Item 1). I judge the ten-year shape knowable within a range: volume
  higher, the take per dollar within a band of a few basis points either way, with regulation and the DOJ case as the
  named ways to be "way wrong" **[M2011-084]**. Seeing the industry is not seeing the winner **[M2012-067]**,
  **[L2009-005]**; here the winner is already named in the filing's own table (Visa's 2024 payments volume $13,433B
  against Mastercard's $8,014B and American Express's $1,750B, 10-K FY2025 Item 1), and the question is whether it stays
  the winner, which is Q2's.
- **The doubt rule** **[M2002-092]**. I have one doubt, the take per dollar under regulation and litigation, and it is a
  doubt about the castle's toll (Q2) rather than about whether the economics can be pictured at all. A technology filter
  **[M1998-008]**, **[M1999-063]** would close the file if the network's value depended on staying ahead of a fast
  technology; the filing's numbers show a business whose value depends on acceptance on both sides, which changes slowly.
- **VERDICT: IN.** The two key variables are identified and their record is long and steady **[M1998-044]**; the
  forecast is about customer behaviour, not technology **[M2017-019]**; the earning power and position ten years out can
  be fixed within a range **[M2012-065]**, **[M2011-084]**. The doubt about the take is carried to Q2, where the castle
  tests are asked of it.


## Q2 — WHY IS THE CASTLE STILL STANDING, and what keeps it standing ten to twenty years? STOP.
The question: "why is that castle still standing? And what’s going to keep it standing or cause it not to be standing
five, 10, 20 years from now. What are the key factors? And how permanent are they?" **[M1995-038]**.

- **The castle questions (test 1).** The key factor, from the filing, is acceptance on both sides at a scale no newcomer
  has: 4,805 million Visa cards and $15,927B of total volume in calendar 2024 (10-K FY2025, Item 1, the network table),
  accepted in "more than 200 countries and territories". A bank issues the card sellers accept; a seller accepts the card
  banks issue. In Munger's terms it is an advantage "of scale — scale of market dominance" **[M1995-039]**. Permanence:
  the record read runs from FY2018 to June 2026 with volume up every year but one (FY2020, travel), and the network table
  shows the order of the networks unchanged. It does not depend on "the genius of the lord" **[M1995-038]**: nothing in
  the filings ties the volume to any person.
- **The attacker with money (test 3).** "if I had a hundred million dollars [...] could I do it?" **[M2011-015]**;
  "why are there no new entrants into the field?" **[M2000-077]**. Here the test has been run in the market, by an
  attacker with money and its own cards: Capital One closed its purchase of Discover on 2025-05-18 and now runs the
  Discover and PULSE networks, and its own 10-K says that "increased competition with certain network operators, such as
  Visa and Mastercard, could affect our existing partnerships" (Capital One 10-K FY2025, filed 2026-02-19,
  `0000927628-26-000024`, Item 1 and Item 1A). Its network volume for the part of 2025 it owned them was **$401.8B**
  (same 10-K, Global Payment Network volume), against Visa's **$13,894B** of payments volume in the twelve months to June
  2025. Written down as contrary evidence **[M1997-127]**: the attack is new, funded and from inside the issuing side, and
  "one competitor is frequently enough to ruin a business" **[M2012-108]**. It has not yet shown in Visa's volume
  (payments volume +10% constant-dollar in the June 2026 quarter, Exhibit 99.1).
- **Pricing power and the agony before a rise (test 4).** "you can almost measure the strength of a business over time by
  the agony they go through in determining whether a price increase can be sustained" **[M2005-020]**. Visa names "select
  pricing modifications" as a driver of service revenue, data processing revenue and other revenue in FY2025 (10-K
  FY2025, Item 7), and what it keeps per dollar of payments volume rose from **25.3 to 28.8 basis points** between FY2018
  and FY2025 while the volume grew 7.9% a year (Q1). That is the mark of "a monopoly or a near monopoly" **[M2010-092]**.
  Against it, written down **[M1997-127]**: the share of gross revenue handed back as client incentives rose from 21.0%
  to 28.3% over the same years, so the banks that choose the network take a growing part of each price rise; and the
  regulators cap the interchange that rides on the network in the EU, Australia, New Zealand and parts of Latin America
  (10-K FY2025, Item 1A).
- **Customers mad at you and still growing.** "if you have a business where your customers are mad at you and you’re
  growing, you know, that has met a certain test, in my mind, of utility" **[M2000-032]**. The sellers have sued over the
  fees since 2005 (Mastercard 10-K FY2025, `0001141391-26-000013`, Note on legal matters: "In 2005, the first of a series
  of complaints were filed on behalf of merchants"); Visa has settled with merchants holding about 95% of the opt-out
  volume, new claims were filed in Europe in April to June 2026, and through all of it the volume grew (10-Q Note 16;
  Q1). The sellers stay because their customers carry the card; that is the castle.
- **Unit volume and share of mind (test 5).** Processed transactions 212,579M (FY2023) to 257,545M (FY2025) and 71.7
  billion in the June 2026 quarter, +10% (10-K FY2025; Exhibit 99.1). Share of mind is the consumer test **[M1997-099]**;
  here the "mind" that matters is the bank's and the seller's, and the evidence is acceptance and
  issuance, not advertising.
- **The low bid (test 8).** "it wouldn’t be a question of people buying candy for the low bid" **[M2017-009]**. The
  customer who chooses the network is the issuing bank, and it does take bids: "In order to stay competitive, we may need to
  adjust our pricing or offer incentives to our clients to grow payments volume" (10-K FY2025, Item 1A). The cardholder and the seller do
  not choose on price at all. So the low bid is real at the issuer and absent at the till; the rising incentive share is
  its cost.
- **The competitor row, from the competitors' own filings** (same metrics, latest fiscal year):

  | network | fiscal year | net revenue | growth | operating margin (GAAP) | incentives as % of gross revenue | volume | owner cash (OCF − SBC − capex and capitalized software) | source |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | Visa | to 2025-09-30 | $40,000M | 11% | 60.0% (operating income 23,994 of 40,000) | 28.3% | payments volume $13,894B | $20,680M | 10-K `0001403161-25-000089` |
  | Mastercard | to 2025-12-31 | $32,791M | 16% | 57.6% | 38.5% (rebates and incentives $20,522M) | GDV $10,632B (3,878 + 5,349 + 1,405) | $15,836M (17,648 − 597 − 489 − 726) | 10-K `0001141391-26-000013` |
  | Capital One's Global Payment Network | 2025 (from 2025-05-18) | not reported separately | n/a | n/a | n/a | $401.8B | n/a | 10-K `0000927628-26-000024` |

  Mastercard grew faster in its latest year and pays its banks a larger share of its gross; both are pricing up and both
  are paying more to issuers (Mastercard: rebates and incentives "increased 16%", its 10-K Item 7). The two are not
  beating each other's brains out in the sense of **[M2013-052]**: both earn operating margins above 55% on a model that
  needs little capital (Q3). Visa's 2024 payments volume is 1.7 times Mastercard's (Visa 10-K network table).
- **Ask the competitors (test 9).** The competitors' filings name Visa first among the networks they compete with
  (Mastercard 10-K: "We compete worldwide with payments networks such as Visa, American Express, JCB, China UnionPay and
  Discover"; Capital One as above). The silver-bullet question **[M1999-130]** cannot be put from the outside; the
  filings show where the rivals aim.
- **Widening or narrowing (test 10)** **[M1999-108]**, **[L2005-010]**. Widening: the take per dollar, value-added
  services revenue $7.2B (FY2023) to $10.9B (FY2025), commercial and cross-border volume growing faster than consumer
  (10-K FY2025, Item 7). Narrowing: the incentive share, the U.S. debit routing fight (the DOJ complaint "alleges Visa has
  monopolized" U.S. debit network services; a remedy would most likely narrow Visa's U.S. debit position), the Capital
  One migration, and interchange caps spreading country by country. On the evidence read, the moat is wide and its edges
  are being worked on; the notch is in U.S. debit and in what the banks take, not in the network's position.
- **What could destroy, modify or reduce it, five to fifteen years out (test 11)** **[M2000-014]**. Named, from the
  filing: (a) regulation of the network's own fees, not only interchange, and an adverse DOJ remedy; (b) account-to-account
  and real-time systems backed by governments or banks, which the 10-K names (The Clearing House, Zelle; "government
  involvement in domestic and cross-border payments"); (c) stablecoins in cross-border and B2B flows, which the 10-K says
  "could potentially disrupt existing payment networks" and which touch the most profitable line, international
  transaction revenue ($14,166M of gross revenue in FY2025). None of these is shown on the evidence to be filling the
  moat in now: volume, transactions and cross-border all grew 10% to 13% in the June 2026 quarter (Exhibit 99.1). Each
  modifies or reduces the toll; none in the record read removes the two-sided acceptance that is the castle.
- **VERDICT: IN.** The castle is shown standing on the record and in the market: an attacker with money and its own cards
  carries about 3% of Visa's volume **[M2011-015]**, the customers who sue it keep accepting it as it grows
  **[M2000-032]**, and it has priced up through the period read **[M2005-020]**, **[M2010-092]**. Its future can be judged
  within a range: the threats are named, they reduce the toll rather than the position, and they are carried as
  contrary evidence into the value question, not set aside. Not TOO HARD: the moat is not "tenuous" **[M2000-019]**; its
  width is the thing being contested in court and in Congress, and that contest bears on how much the castle earns, which
  Q7 weighs.

## WHAT IN THE FRAMEWORK WAS WRONG OR UNCLEAR
1. **Multi-class share counts and `tools/run.py`.** The tool printed a market cap from a 2010 cover count times a split
   factor, which landed within 0.1M of the true class A equivalent by coincidence and would have passed unnoticed if it
   had not been checked. The template says "read the charter note before adding classes" but gives no rule for classes
   whose conversion rate changes (Visa's class B rates fall with each escrow deposit). I used the rates in force after
   the latest 8-K. A line in the template saying "convert at the latest filed rate, including 8-Ks after the cover" would
   settle it. Tool defect reported, not fixed: `tools/run.py` should refuse a cover count older than the latest 10-Q
   rather than scale it by a split factor.
2. **Litigation borne by another class.** The covered U.S. litigation is paid in cash by Visa (so it is inside operating
   cash flow) but recovered from the class B holders by a lower conversion rate (so it shrinks the share count). Owner
   cash per class A share is right only if both are read together. Q4's list of real costs does not say how to treat a
   cost that another class of owner bears; I deducted it in cash and took the share count after the deposits. A sentence
   in Q4 would help.
3. **The pre-tax floor against after-tax owner cash** (the HUBB run's point 3, met again). I used Visa's own effective
   rate (17.1%, giving 8.29%) and showed the row's own 6½ to 7% translation beside it; the framework does not say which.
   Here it does not change the box.
4. **The growth cap.** The CONVENTION keeps the shown rate as the top unless it "traces to an absurdity". Visa's shown
   rate (10.3%) is not absurd but depends on continued price rises the Q2 evidence shows are contested; the framework has
   no way to lower the top for a contested source of growth short of calling it absurd. I kept the shown rate and showed
   the full-record rate (8.2%) beside it. Because the price sat near the top, a lower top would only have widened the
   OUT; but for a name priced in the middle of the range the choice would decide the box.
5. **Q6 needs the Q7 range before Q7 is written.** The buyback CONVENTION reads the prices paid against the bottom of the
   range, which the run computes two questions later; the framework says to record it back at Q6, which I did by
   computing Q7 first in the research folder. The template's order and the framework's dependency disagree.
6. **"Fair-price band" and "cheap price"** remain owner-requested terms, not framework terms; I followed the HUBB run's
   definitions so that the two runs can be compared.
