## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**
- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language:** Eagle quarries limestone and burns it
  in eight kilns (one of them, Buda in Texas, owned 50/50 with Heidelberg Materials) to make cement; it
  mines gypsum next to five wallboard plants and presses it between paper that its own recycled-paper
  mill in Oklahoma makes; and it runs 30 readymix plants and six aggregates quarries that sell crushed
  stone and concrete within about 50 miles of each site. Everything it sells is heavy and cheap for its
  weight, so each plant sells mostly to buyers within a truck's reach: the 10-K gives *"a 150-mile
  radius from each plant, up to 300 miles by rail"* for cement. Revenue follows two things, tons or
  square feet shipped and the price per unit, and cost is mostly energy, raw material, maintenance and
  freight on a large fixed plant. FY2026, filed segment tables (10-K `0001193125-26-230979`, Item 7):
  **Cement** $1,299.4M of revenue including the joint venture's half and intersegment sales, 7.47M tons
  at a net $155.18 a ton, **operating earnings $328.3M (25%)**; **Gypsum Wallboard** $764.5M, 2,759
  million square feet at a net $226.08 per thousand, **operating earnings $286.8M (38%)**; **Recycled
  Paperboard** $199.2M, **$44.5M (22%)**; **Concrete and Aggregates** $299.5M, **$12.9M (4%)**.
  Consolidated revenue $2,308.7M, gross profit $652.5M (28.3%), corporate G&A $89.2M, interest $46.5M,
  net earnings $423.8M. **About half the operating earnings come from cement and about 40% from
  wallboard; the concrete business earns almost nothing.** Cement demand is roughly half public
  infrastructure (the 10-K: *"public infrastructure comprising nearly 50% of total demand"*); wallboard
  demand is more than 80% housing and repair (*"residential construction and repair and remodel
  accounted for more than 80% of calendar 2025 industry sales"*).
- **The scarce input this business controls:** **permitted kilns and the limestone and gypsum
  reserves beside them, in regions an importer cannot cheaply reach.** Item 1: *"Environmental and
  zoning regulations have made it increasingly difficult for the U.S. cement industry to expand existing
  facilities and to build new cement facilities"*; *"Our position in the U.S. heartland, away from most
  import terminals, provides a degree of insulation from coastal imports"*; *"We own, or control, at
  least 25 years of primary raw material reserves and resources (and in many instances, more than 50
  years) for each of our cement and wallboard facilities"*. It is not an exclusive input: the same Item
  says the product is sold on price (*"The products we make are basic necessities, and competition is
  often based largely on price"*), and the forward-looking statement in every filing names *"the fact
  that our products are commodities"*. Whether the scarce input is enough to make a franchise is Q2's
  question, not this one's.
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** **Yes.** Cement, wallboard and
  aggregates are made the same way they were in the FY2018 10-K (and for a century before), sold to the
  same trades, and priced per ton or per thousand square feet. The moving parts the filing names are
  all understandable: carbon and ozone regulation of kilns (Item 1, Environmental Matters), imports
  (*"nearly 23% of total U.S. sales"*), and new wallboard capacity. None is a comprehension problem; they
  are Q2 and Q4 questions.
