## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**
- Unit economics in my own words, no management language: Wingstop licenses a chicken-wing restaurant format to independent owners and collects a share of what their restaurants sell. At 2025-12-27 there were **3,056 restaurants, 2,999 of them run by franchisees** (2,529 in the United States under 186 franchisees averaging about 14 restaurants each, 470 abroad under master franchisees) and 57 run by the company. A US franchisee pays **6.0% of sales as royalty** (*"each franchisee is required to pay us a royalty of 6.0% of their gross sales net of discounts"*; the older agreements carried 5%, see Q2), **5.5% of sales into an advertising fund** that the company spends on national advertising, a **$20,000 fee per restaurant opened** and a $10,000 development fee per restaurant committed, and buys all food and packaging from suppliers Wingstop designates, which pay Wingstop **rebates**. The franchisee pays for the restaurant itself (*"a low average initial investment of approximately $580,000, excluding real estate purchase or lease costs"*) and pays for its own site. FY2025, on **$5,343M of system-wide sales**, Wingstop's revenue was **$696.9M**: royalties, fees and other $321.8M (6.0 cents per dollar of system sales), advertising fees $247.6M (passed through: advertising expense was $261.5M), and the company restaurants' own sales $127.5M (cost of sales $96.1M, 75.4% of those sales). Overhead (SG&A) was $128.4M and depreciation $25.1M; **operating income $179.3M** after a $6.5M loss on selling an office building. Capital: property $130.6M, goodwill and trademarks $116.6M; the business carries **$1,220.9M of securitized notes** against a **stockholders' deficit of $736.8M** at 2025-12-27 (Q4). **Profit is (royalty rate x franchisee sales, which is units x sales per unit) plus supplier rebates and fees, less a head office and the company restaurants' margin; growth comes from new units the franchisees pay for.**
- The scarce input this business controls: **the Wingstop brand and trademark, the franchise agreements (a 10-year initial term, royalties payable weekly), the recipes (*"franchisees ... use our proprietary sauces, seasonings, and spice blends and ... purchase them ... only from designated sources"*), and the ordering platform through which 73.2% of FY2025 system sales were placed.** Whether customers regard that brand as having no close substitute is Q2's question, not Q1's.
- Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years? **The way the money is made, yes.** The format (wings, tenders and sandwiches, cooked to order and sauced, eaten in, carried out or delivered) has been franchised since 1997, and the royalty-on-sales mechanism since the first franchise. What has changed is the channel (digital orders 73.2% of FY2025 system sales) and the cost of the system to the franchisee (advertising contribution from 2% in 2016 to 5.5% in 2025), both inside the same mechanism. The unit volume is the question the 2025-2026 record raises: domestic same-store sales fell 3.3% in FY2025 and 8.1% in the first half of 2026, and domestic AUV from $2.14M (FY2024) to $1.89M (trailing, 2026-06-27). That bears on the moat (Q2) and on survival (Q4), not on how the money is made.
- **Simple and stable in character [E3-31]; a decision that can be made on the filings [E4-46].** A royalty on restaurant sales requires no technology forecast; the one moving part a reader must track is the securitized debt, which is filed in full.

## Q2 — IS IT A FRANCHISE? **[E3-03]**

**The question is put at both altitudes, as the MCD run of 2026-09-03 put it.** Wingstop sells to two customers: the **franchisee**, who buys the brand and the system and pays a royalty, and the **diner**, whose purchases are the royalty's base. The MCD run held that *"the diner faces close substitutes on every corner; the franchisee does not"* and rested McDonald's IN on a structure the franchisee cannot leave: the company *"maintains control of the underlying real estate"* at term end, with $31.5bn of contracted rent. **Wingstop has no such structure.** Its franchisee pays for the restaurant and its site (*"a low average initial investment of approximately $580,000, excluding real estate purchase or lease costs"*), signs *"a 10-year initial term, with an opportunity to enter into one or more renewal franchise agreements subject to certain conditions"*, and pays a percentage of sales. What the franchisee buys is access to the diner's demand for the Wingstop product. So the franchise, if there is one, is the diner's, and [E3-43]'s single-company test is read there first, then at the franchisee.

Domestic AUV went from $1.13M to $2.14M in nine years. Over 2019-2025 Wingstop's comparable sales compounded +106.6% against +31.2% at Popeyes, +27.6% at Domino's US and +21.7% at El Pollo Loco. Customers who choose you that much more often than every filed rival do not regard the rivals as close substitutes."* **Answer:** this is the best fact in the file and it is accepted as demand: the diner wanted Wingstop more every year for two decades. But [E3-03]'s second criterion is proved, in [E3-43]'s own sentence, by *"a company’s ability to regularly price its product or service aggressively"*, and the filer's explanation of that record is traffic, not price: rising visits at a price customers regarded as good value is the opposite signature from pricing power, and it is what a wave looks like while it runs [E3-51]. The years that carry the record are the ones the screen row flags: 2020 (+21.4%) and 2023-2024 (+18.3%, +19.9%); remove those three years and the remaining four (2019, 2021, 2022, 2025) compound to about +20% (1.111 x 1.080 x 1.034 x 0.967), below every peer's full seven-year figure. The first year the diner's demand was tested, it fell (item 5 and THE READING).

The royalty went from 5% to 6% on agreements signed after July 2014, the advertising contribution from 2% of sales (2016) to 5.5% (2025), a total take on a new unit from about 7% to 11.5% of sales, and franchisees kept building: 'Existing franchisees accounted for more than 90% of franchised restaurants opened in each of 2024 and 2025', a record 493 net openings in FY2025, 'a franchisee unlevered cash-on-cash return of approximately 70%+' targeted in year two. ### THE READING
**[E3-43]'s own test is single-company: *"The existence of all three conditions will be demonstrated by a company’s ability to regularly price its product or service aggressively and thereby to earn high rates of return on capital."*** Wingstop shows the returns and the traffic; its filings do not show the pricing, and the two filed episodes that test it point the other way.
- **The system price is not filed. A search recorded here, not an absence claim beyond it:** every MD&A of the prospectus, the 10-Ks FY2015-FY2025, the 10-Qs of 2026 and the six releases Q1 2025-Q2 2026 was searched for *price increase, pricing, menu price, average ticket, average check, transaction size* (`pricing_search.txt`). **No system-wide menu-price figure and no price-versus-transactions split for the system was found in any of them**; the one sentence that defines the terms, *"Transaction size increases are driven by price increases or favorable mix shift from either an increase in items purchased or shifts into higher priced items"*, recurs every year without a number. The filer does not file the one series ([E4-55]'s units against price) that would show pricing power; CMG files it every year and passed Q2 on it.
- **The one price series that is filed runs with the commodity, not ahead of it.** At the company restaurants, the food cost ratio moved in the same direction as the filer's stated bone-in wing cost in **every year FY2013-FY2024, twelve of twelve** (table above): the restaurant, not the diner, bore each swing. The test year is **FY2021**, *"a 40.9% increase in the cost of bone-in chicken wings, representing record-high wing inflation"*, in a year of rising demand (domestic same store sales +8.0% on top of +21.4%): company-restaurant same store sales rose **3.4%**, *"driven by an increase in average ticket"*, and the food cost ratio went from **35.9% to 44.8%**. Price was raised, and by a fraction of the cost. (Limit: 57 company restaurants of 2,586 domestic; franchisees set their own menu prices, *"If menu prices are increased by us and our franchisees"*, so this is a sample of the system's pricing, not all of it.)
- **The filer answers [E4-37]'s question in writing** (*"if we raised the price 10 cents a pound, would sales fall off a cliff?"*): *"If menu prices are increased by us and our franchisees to cover increased labor costs, the higher prices could adversely affect transactions which could lower sales and thereby reduce our margins and the royalties that we receive from franchisees."* (FY2024 and FY2025 10-Ks), and every 10-K FY2020-FY2025 warns that *"we may not be able to anticipate or successfully react to changing food costs, including the price of bone-in chicken wings, by adjusting our purchasing practices, increasing our menu prices to pass along commodity price increases to our customers or making other operational adjustments"* (FY2021 text; the FY2025 text is the same with a page number inside it).
- **The first flat-demand test in the record, [E2-44] (1), failed.** [E2-44] asks for *"an ability to increase prices rather easily (even when product demand is flat and capacity is not fully utilized) without fear of significant loss of either market share or unit volume"*. The record had no flat year from 2004 to 2024. In the first, the filer reports the loss of unit volume (*"a 7.5% decline in domestic same store sales contributed by lower transaction volumes, reflecting continued pressure on consumer spending"*, Q2 2026 release; Q1 2026, 8.7%, the same words), the guidance moved down three times in five months (*"Flat to low-single digit domestic same store sales growth"*, 2026-02-18; *"Low-single digit decline"*, 2026-04-29; *"A decline of 4% to 6%"*, 2026-07-29), and the response was *"investments in value"*, not price. **Direction [E4-32]: narrowing.** Domestic AUV $2.138M → $1.893M (−11.5%), transactions falling in the filer's words, franchised openings −31%.

**[E3-03] criterion by criterion.**
- **(1) needed or desired: passes**, strongly: 3,255 restaurants and $5.3bn of system sales.
- **(2) thought by its customers to have no close substitute: fails, on the filed conduct and in the filer's words.** The filer: *"The restaurant industry in general, and the fast casual category in particular, are intensely competitive, and we compete with many well-established restaurant companies on the basis of food taste and quality, price, service, value, location, convenience, digital engagement, delivery and overall customer experience."* and *"Our competitors include individual restaurants and restaurant chains that range from independent local operators to well-capitalized national and regional restaurant companies, including restaurants offering chicken wings, tenders, and chicken sandwiches"*. When money was short the diner substituted: Wingstop −8.1% while El Pollo Loco +4.9% and Domino's +0.5%. The 21-year record shows a product customers chose more often, at a price that was not pressed; it does not show a product for which they would pay more rather than go elsewhere.
- **(3) not subject to price regulation: passes.**
