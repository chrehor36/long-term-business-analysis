## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**
- Unit economics in my own words, no management language: Sanmina builds other companies' electronic products to their designs, in about 20 countries, and keeps a thin slice of what it bills. The customer (an equipment maker: network gear, cloud servers and racks, medical devices, industrial controls, defense electronics, car electronics) owns the design and the brand; Sanmina buys the components, assembles and tests the boards and the finished systems, ships them, and later repairs them. Most of the price it charges is the components passed through. **FY2025: net sales $8,128.4M, gross profit $716.4M (8.8 cents on the dollar), operating income $354.6M (4.4 cents).** About 80% of revenue is the assembly segment (IMS); about 20% is components and products it makes itself (CPS: circuit boards, backplanes, cables, enclosures, machined parts, memory modules under the Viking name, storage platforms, defense products under SCI), which the filer says *"generally yield higher margins than our core IMS business"*. The customers are few and large (*"Sales to our ten largest customers represented 52% of our net sales in 2025"*), sign supply agreements *"with terms ranging from three to five years"* that *"generally do not obligate the customer to purchase minimum quantities"*, bear the cost of materials ordered to their forecast, and in some cases write in *"cost reduction objectives during the term of the agreement"*. Firm orders run *"thirty to ninety days in advance"*. **Profit is (volume built x a conversion margin of a few cents per dollar of bill of materials), less the cost of carrying inventory and receivables for customers; the capital is factories, inventory and working capital, not a brand.** Since 2025-10-27 about 38% of revenue is the ZT Systems business, which builds AI and general-purpose server racks for hyperscale cloud companies on the same principle: in the nine months to 2026-06-27, net sales $10,667.0M, of which *"Communications Networks and Cloud and AI Infrastructure"* $6,883.1M (64.5%, against 37.4% a year earlier), gross margin 9.0%, operating margin 4.2% after $137.0M of acquisition costs.
- The scarce input this business controls: **qualified manufacturing capacity and process know-how placed near customers (facility certifications for medical, automotive and defense work; SCI's *"infrastructure and facility security clearance"*), an in-house circuit-board and component base, and the customer relationships through which programs are won.** The filer names what it does not control: the designs belong to the customer (*"For CDM projects, customers pay for all services and own the intellectual property"*), patents *"have expired or will expire in the near term"*, and the customer may build in-house instead. Whether any of this is a scarce input in [E3-03]'s sense is Q2's question, not Q1's.
- Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years? **The way the money is made, yes; what is built, no.** Contract assembly for equipment makers has worked the same way since Sanmina's incorporation in 1989 and the filings back to FY2009 describe the same model. The products change as fast as the customers' products change (the 10-K's own lists run to *"Optical (400G, 800G, 1.6T)"* and AI racks), and the mix has swung within one year from 37% to 65% cloud and AI. That swing bears on the moat (Q2) and on survival and the reliability of earnings (Q4); it does not change the unit economics, which are a conversion margin on someone else's product.
- **Simple and stable in character [E3-31]; a decision that can be made on the filings [E4-46].** A contract manufacturer earning a few cents on the dollar requires no technology forecast to be understood; the technology risk belongs to the customer and reaches Sanmina as volume and mix. No months of study are needed to know how the money is made.
