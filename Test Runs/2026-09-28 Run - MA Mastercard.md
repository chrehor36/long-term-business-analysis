## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**
- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language:** Mastercard runs the rules, the brand and the switch for a card system in which it lends no money and issues no cards (*"Mastercard is not a financial institution. The Company does not issue cards, extend credit, determine or receive revenue from interest rates or other fees charged to account holders by issuers"*). Banks that issue cards and banks that sign up merchants pay it three kinds of fee: a small percentage of the dollar volume on cards carrying its brands (domestic assessments), a larger percentage when the card and the merchant are in different countries (cross-border fees, including the currency conversion), and a per-transaction charge for authorizing, clearing and settling the payments it switches. **FY2025: $10.6 trillion of gross dollar volume and 175.5 billion switched transactions produced $40.0bn of gross payment-network fees, of which $20.5bn was handed back to banks and merchants as rebates and incentives to win or keep their card programmes, leaving $19.5bn of payment-network net revenue**; a second line of $13.3bn sells fraud scoring, authentication, data and consulting, open-banking data, and account-to-account rails (Vocalink, Nets) to the same customers. Costs are mostly people and computers (operating margin 57.6% in FY2025); the interchange that merchants complain about is paid by the merchant's bank to the cardholder's bank and **Mastercard does not keep it** (*"Although we do not earn revenues from interchange, interchange rates can impact the volume of transactions we see on our payment products."*). The company also guarantees settlement between its customers for a few days (net exposure $72.9bn at the FY2025 year end), a contingent liability read at Q4.
- **The scarce input this business controls:** acceptance and issuance together: a brand that roughly every merchant that takes cards accepts and that banks put on 3.7 billion cards (the 10-Q release: *"the company’s customers had issued 3.7 billion Mastercard and Maestro-branded cards"*), with the rules and settlement guarantee that let any issuer's card work at any acquirer's merchant. A new network must sign both sides at once; the incumbent pays the issuers (the rebates line) to keep its side of the market.
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** The fee on volume, the switch and the two-sided acceptance will; what may differ is how much of the gross is rebated, whether regulators cap Mastercard's own network fees as they have capped interchange, and how much account-to-account and stablecoin payments take (the filer names all three as pressures: *"Continued intense pricing pressure may materially and adversely affect our overall business"*; *"several jurisdictions are demonstrating increased interest about the network fees we charge to our customers ... which could in the future lead to regulation relating to our network fees"*; *"pricing pressure related to real-time account-based payment schemes"*). These are questions about the franchise's width, read at Q2, not about whether the model can be understood. The model is simple and has been the same since the 2006 listing; the ten-year filed record (FY2016-FY2025) shows the same fee structure, same two revenue lines and same cost structure every year.
- **VERDICT: [x] IN  [ ] OUT  [ ] UNRESEARCHED → ____  [ ] UNKNOWABLE → ____** **IN.** A toll on the dollar volume and the transaction count of a two-sided acceptance network, lending nothing, with a services line sold to the same banks; the unit economics are stated in the 10-K in the filer's own fee categories and do not need forecasting to be understood.

### THE STRONGEST EVIDENCE AGAINST THIS VERDICT, stated as its holders would state it [E4-51, E4-26, E3-47]
1. **The pricing is bought back from the customers who choose the network, and the buy-back is rising faster than the price.** Payment-network rebates and incentives were **$20,522M in FY2025 against $19,476M of payment-network net revenue: Mastercard hands back 51.3% of its gross network fees**, up from 46.7% in FY2021 (46.4%, 49.0%, 50.4%, 51.3% in the years between); across the whole company rebates rose from 30.7% of gross revenue (FY2016) to 37.0% (FY2022) and at least 38.5% (FY2024-FY2025, payment-network rebates alone). **Rebates grew 18.3% a year FY2021-FY2025 against 15.6% for gross network fees**, and in FY2020, when gross revenue fell 5%, rebates rose 3% (*"Rebates and incentives (contra-revenue) | (8,315) | (8,097)"*). The first half of 2026: *"Payment network rebates and incentives increased 22%, or 20% on a currency-neutral basis, primarily due to an increase in our key drivers, as well as new and renewed deals"*, against payment network net revenue +10%. The filer, in every 10-K FY2016-FY2025: *"In order to stay competitive, we may have to increase the amount of these incentives and pricing discounts"*, and FY2016-FY2022 (merged into one sentence from FY2023): *"The demand from our customers for better pricing arrangements and greater rebates and incentives moderates our growth."* **For the bank that chooses which brand goes on its cards, Visa is a close substitute, and the rebates are the price of that substitution** ([E3-03] clause (2) seen from the issuer's side).
2. **The larger rival earns more and pays less.** Visa's operating margin was 62.9-66.2% in FY2017-FY2024 on its income statement and 60.0% in FY2025 after a $2,562M litigation provision (64.5-67.2% before provisions, FY2018-FY2025); Mastercard's was 48.7-57.6% (53.1-59.2% before provisions): **Visa ahead in every year FY2017-FY2025, by 2.4-14.2 points as filed and by 7.2-11.7 points before provisions (FY2018-FY2025).** Visa's client incentives were **21.0-28.3% of its gross revenue** (FY2018-FY2025) against Mastercard's **31.5-38.5%+**. The filer: *"Some of our competitors are larger with greater financial resources and accordingly may be able to charge lower prices."* Mastercard is the second network of a two-network market.
3. **Price regulation is filed, on the fee Mastercard sets and, increasingly, on the fee it keeps.** Interchange, which Mastercard sets but does not keep, is capped for US debit (Regulation II, whose validity two courts split on in 2025), in the EU, in Australia, for cross-border cards in New Zealand from May 2026 (*"The New Zealand Commerce Commission approved the implementation of caps on cross-border interchange for most types of card transactions, effective May 2026."*), and by the U.S. class settlement for five years (*"Both Mastercard and Visa agree to provide a 10 basis point reduction in the average systemwide effective interchange rate on U.S.-issued consumer credit and commercial credit transactions at U.S. merchant locations"*; *"The interchange reduction is designed to effectively serve as a cap for a period of five years"*). **On its own fees:** *"several jurisdictions are demonstrating increased interest about the network fees we charge to our customers (in some cases as part of broader market reviews of retail payments), which could in the future lead to regulation relating to our network fees"*; *"Some merchants are increasingly asking regulators to review and potentially regulate our own network fees, in addition to interchange"*; the European Commission's 2024 request *"focuses on Mastercard’s practices regarding network fees related to acquirers"*; the EU already forbade a network to charge fees on domestic co-badged transactions not using its brand (the FY2016 10-K: *"a network can no longer charges fees on domestic EEA payment transactions that do not use its payment brand"*) and *"requires us to separate our scheme activities ... from our switching activities"*. [E2-59]'s point cuts both ways: regulation *"caps"* a franchise, and the cap is being drawn nearer the fee Mastercard keeps.
4. **The acceptance rules that make the network unavoidable are being given up in settlement.** The 2025 class settlement: *"The Agreement enables merchants to make decisions on independently accepting consumer credit and commercial credit cards. Within consumer credit, merchants would have additional flexibility in their decisions to accept “standard" and "premium" credit cards"*, with simplified surcharging. Preliminary approval June 2026, final hearing November 2026. **The honour-all-cards rule is the legal form of "no close substitute" at the merchant counter**, and it is being narrowed.
5. **Substitutes the filer names, and the governments behind them.** *"we face pricing pressure related to real-time account-based payment schemes. These pressures impact both domestic pricing (such as the increased use of schemes that offer increasingly lower or subsidized P2M pricing) and cross-border pricing"*; *"governments in some countries (such as South Africa) mandate switching of domestic payments either entirely in that country or by only domestic companies"*; data localization in *"India, China and Saudi Arabia"*; the EU's payment services rules *"enabling them to route transactions away from Mastercard products"*. Mastercard is buying BVNK, *"a provider of stablecoin infrastructure"*, which is a hedge against a substitute, not proof there is none. On the shelf, Munger in 2018 on this exact class: *"there is one tiny cloud on the horizon of the payments processors and that is the system of WeChat in China. ... I don’t have the faintest idea how important that cloud is, and I don’t think Warren does either."* (`Annual Meetings/2018 Annual Meeting.txt`, line 731; quoted as shelf text, no ledger row cites it).
6. **The litigation is permanent and the new claims are large.** Litigation provisions every year: $117M, $15M, $1,128M, $0, $73M, $94M, $356M, $539M, $680M, $504M (FY2016-FY2025), $82M in the first half of 2026; open: **Block and Intuit, *"seeking aggregate single damages in excess of $ 5 billion"***, a new April 2026 putative class for US merchant credit interchange *"since January 2019"* that challenges the 2018 release, over £0.5bn of UK and European merchant claims (up from £0.3bn at the year end), a UK commercial-card collective action above £1bn, the ACCC's debit case (hearing concluded June 2026), the DOJ's civil investigative demand on US debit, and the EC request on acquirer fees. A network whose rules are litigated in every major market for twenty years is paying, every year, for the position.
