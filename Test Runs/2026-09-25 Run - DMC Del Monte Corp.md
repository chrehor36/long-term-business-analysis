## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**
- **Unit economics in my own words.** The company grows bananas and pineapples on its own farms in Costa Rica,
  Guatemala, Panama, Brazil, the Philippines and Kenya, and buys the rest from independent growers under
  multi-year contracts (*"52% of the fresh produce we sold was grown on company-controlled farms"*; bananas 47% own,
  pineapples 81% own, 2025). It packs the fruit, ships it north and east on its **own refrigerated container ships**
  (six owned, one chartered) and about 11,000 reefer containers, lands it at its own US ports, ripens it in 31
  distribution centers and sells it by the box to supermarkets, clubs, wholesalers and foodservice in more than 80
  countries. Money is the **price of a box less what it cost to grow or buy, pack and carry it**, and the MD&A says
  in its own words that most of the cost is fixed within a year: *"a significant portion of our cost of products
  sold is fixed ... higher volumes produced on company-controlled farms directly reduce the average per-box cost,
  while lower volumes directly increase the average per-box cost"*. So a weather year, a disease year (*"Black
  Sigatoka"*) or a glut year moves the margin a great deal on almost no change in sales.
  - **Four segments since 2026**: fresh and value-added (pineapples, fresh-cut fruit and vegetables, avocados, other
    fruit; **$2,621.9M of 2025 sales at 11.4% gross margin**), bananas (**$1,490.4M at 4.8%**), prepared foods
    (canned and processed fruit in Europe, Africa and the Middle East under a perpetual royalty-free licence, and
    since March 2026 the US canned vegetables, tomatoes and refrigerated fruit bought from Del Monte Foods; **Q2 2026
    $236.1M at 18.9%**), and other (third-party ocean freight on the return legs, a Jordanian poultry business,
    specialty ingredients; $210.0M at 13.7%).
  - **2025 in figures**: sales $4,322.3M, gross profit $399.1M (9.2%), SG&A $212.7M, operating income $137.4M after
    $59.3M of impairments and a $10.3M gain on disposals; net income to the company $90.7M; Walmart about 7% of
    sales, top ten customers about 29%.
- **The scarce input this business controls.** Three things, each named by the filing and each weighed at Q2:
  (1) **pineapple land and know-how**, where *"the high degree of capital investment and cultivation expertise
  required, as well as the longer length of the growing cycle, makes it relatively difficult to enter the market"*
  and *"Our profitability has depended significantly on the sale of our Del Monte Gold® Extra Sweet pineapples"*;
  (2) **the Del Monte® name**, royalty-free and perpetual for fresh produce and, since March 2026, owned outright;
  (3) **the cold chain**: ships, ports, ripening rooms, which the company calls *"a competitive advantage"*. For
  bananas the filing names no scarce input at all: *"Unlike the pineapple market, there are few barriers to entry
  into the banana market."*
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** The mechanism, yes: bananas have been grown in
  Central America and shipped north in refrigerated holds for over a century, and the Del Monte® produce brand
  dates from 1892 by the company's own account. **The mix, less so**: the company has just added a US canned-food
  business that its previous owner took into bankruptcy, and management calls itself *"building Del Monte
  Corporation Across Fresh, Refrigerated, Shelf-Stable, and Prepared Foods"* (Q2 2026 release). That is a Q2
  question (what those businesses earn against their rivals) and a Q4 question (what the filed history can say
  about the new perimeter), not a failure to understand how a fruit grower and shipper, or a vegetable canner,
  makes money.

## Q2 — IS IT A FRANCHISE? **[E3-03]**

**The rule, quoted from the ledger row itself** (`principle_ledger.csv`, E3-03, 1991 letter): *"An economic franchise
arises from a product or service that: (1) is needed or desired; (2) is thought by its customers to have no close
substitute and; (3) is not subject to price regulation. The existence of all three conditions will be demonstrated
by a company's ability to regularly price its product or service aggressively and thereby to earn high rates of
return on capital."* Its companion, same letter **[E3-43]**: *"In contrast, "a business" earns exceptional profits
only if it is the low-cost operator or if supply of its product or service is tight. Tightness in supply usually
does not last long."* **The commodity end has its own row, [E2-58] (1982 letter)**: *"For the great majority of
companies selling "commodity"products, a depressing equation of business economics prevails: persistent
over-capacity without administered prices (or costs) equals poor profitability."* Its one exception is *"a cost
advantage that is both wide and sustainable"*, and *"What finally determines levels of long-term profitability in
such industries is the ratio of supply-tight to supply-ample years."*

- Needed or desired **[x]** (fruit, vegetables and canned food) · **no close substitute [ ] — refuted, below** · not
  price-regulated **[x]**, with a caveat: *"In Ecuador and Costa Rica, there are minimum export prices for the sale
  of bananas, which are established and reviewed on a periodic basis by the respective governments"* (10-K FY2025).
  That administers the grower's floor, a cost to this company, not its selling price **[E2-59]**.
