
## Q2 — WHY IS THE CASTLE STILL STANDING, and what keeps it standing ten to twenty years? STOP.

what "protects excellent returns on invested capital" **[L2007-004]**.

**The castle tests, applied to the deciding part, each with its filing fact.**
1. **Key factors and their permanence** **[M1995-038]**. The filer names one: "our strategy of providing a comprehensive
   suite of engineering, consulting, installation and maintenance services gives us a competitive advantage over smaller
   competitors and competitors with more limited capabilities"; and long relationships, "the average length of our
   relationship with our top 10 clients was 23 years" (10-K Item 1). Against it, in the same Item: "We compete with
   national, regional, and local companies, including many small, owner-operated entities"; the client is often reached
   "through intermediaries such as architects and general contractors who subcontract MEP services to us as part of a
   larger project"; and under plan-and-specification work "our client is responsible for designing the project and we
   install it to their specifications".
2. **The money test** **[M2011-015]**: could a well-funded attacker build this? The filing answers it. The credit
   agreement that funds the company is dated 2020-12-16 (8-K 2026-01-02), and "We have made approximately 24 acquisitions
   since December 2020" (10-K Item 1), to stand today as "a leading provider" with $2.55bn of 2025 revenue. A sponsor with
   money assembled the position in five years, and the same 10-K says "Our Sponsor is not limited in its ability to compete
   with us". The named rival grew the same way: Comfort Systems USA paid for acquisitions in every year from 2009 to 2025
   except 2013 (XBRL `PaymentsToAcquireBusinessesNetOfCashAcquired`, $14.6M in 2009 to $279.6M in 2025). Buying incumbents
   is not the same as beating them, and I set that down against my own reading; but the test asks whether money can put a
   rival into the field at the same scale, and here it did. The row's verdict on a yes: "If the answer had been yes, we
   wouldn’t have done it." **[M2011-015]**. And what is gained quickly can be lost quickly: "usually if something can gain
   competitive advantage very quickly, you have to worry about them losing it quickly, too" **[M2002-050]**.
3. **Pricing power and the agony before a rise** **[M2005-020]**. The filer, in its own words: "If our costs are subject
   to significant inflationary pressures, we may not be able to offset such higher costs through price increases" and "Our
   margins depend on our ability to accurately estimate the amount of labor that each job will require because our
   customer contracts are typically fixed-price" (10-K Item 7). A business that prices each job by bid and absorbs the
   estimating error has no price it can raise; it has a bid it can win or lose.
4. **The low-cost position, the exception in a commodity-like field** **[L2004-007]**: "when a company is selling a
   product with commodity-like economic characteristics, being the low-cost producer is all-important" **[L2000-017]**.
   Same metric, the rival's own filing: gross margin of Installation & Maintenance 14.1% / 15.1% / 16.3% (2023 to 2025)
   against Comfort Systems USA's gross margin 19.0% / 21.0% / 24.1% in the same years. Comfort's figure is company-wide,
   but Comfort is almost wholly the same trade (mechanical and electrical contracting), so the comparison is close but not
   exact, flagged. Bowers, the private mechanical contractor bought in January 2026, earned 18.4% gross and 9.2% operating
   margins on its own audited statements for the year to September 2025 (8-K/A `0001193125-26-114802`, Exhibit 99.1). No
   instance found, in the filings read, of the deciding part being the low-cost operator; the evidence read points the
   other way, and "commodity businesses have risk unless you’re the low-cost producer" **[M1997-010]**.
5. **Would the customer still choose it over the low bid** **[M2017-009]**. For plan-and-specification work let through a
   general contractor, the filing gives no reason to think so: the product is the client's design installed to the
   client's specification by one of many bidders. Design-build work and maintenance contracts (18% of the segment's 2025
   revenue) are stickier; the filing gives no split of installation revenue between design-build and plan-and-spec, so the
   share that escapes the low bid cannot be read.
6. **Widening or narrowing** **[M1999-108]**. The segment's gross margin rose two points from 2023 to 2025, in the
   strongest demand the field has recorded; in the second quarter of 2026 consolidated gross margin fell to 17.4% from 21.5%
   a year earlier, "primarily due to a revenue mix shift towards Installation & Maintenance" (press release of 2026-08-13,
   `0001193125-26-347873`, Exhibit 99.1). Not evidence of a widening moat.
7. **What could destroy or reduce it** **[M1995-038]**: a fall in data-center and technology spending (42.7% of 2025
   revenue; "Any period of economic recession affecting a market or industry in which we transact business, such as data
   centers, is likely to adversely impact our business", 10-K Item 1A), losses on fixed-price jobs, and the craft labor
   that 44 bargaining agreements price.
8. **Ask the competitors.** No interviews; the rivals' filings are the record read (the competitor row below).

**The field's returns over the last full cycle, judged in words** (CONVENTION of Q2: "ordinary" carries no figure). The
last full construction cycle visible in the filings runs from 2009 through the trough of 2010 to 2012 and the recovery to
2014. Across it the named contracting rival earned EBITA margins of 1.4% to 5.3% and returns on equity of minus 12.6% to
10.1%; the engineering rival has earned net margins of 1.1% to 5.9% in every year filed. Those are ordinary returns, and
"you can have only two competitors and they’re still terrible businesses" **[M2013-052]**. Since 2015 the contracting
rival has done better, and since 2023 very much better, in a demand boom led by data centers; that is a change for the
better (the railroads "were a terrible business for decades and decades and decades and then they got good"
**[M2017-026]**), and it has not yet been through a year in which the field's volume fell. Legence's own figures do not
show the change: EBITA margins of 5.3% to 8.1% and a net loss in every year from 2022 to 2025 and in the first half of
2026, inside the same boom.

**Contrary evidence, written down as found [M1997-127]:** backlog and awarded contracts of the deciding part rose from
$0.99bn (2023) to $2.68bn (2025), book-to-bill 1.0x / 1.5x / 1.7x (10-K Item 7); the filer reports no liquidated damages
claim over $100,000 in eleven years; Bowers shows a mechanical contractor can earn 9% operating margins in this market.
None is a reason the deciding part's returns are protected: backlog measures demand, which every contractor in the field
is enjoying, and Bowers' margins are Bowers', bought in 2026 for about $325M in cash, 2,551,672 Class A shares and $50M
deferred.

**The reading considered and rejected.** The change-for-the-better paragraph would send the file to TOO HARD (NATURE)
until a downturn shows whether the field's improved pricing holds. I do not rest the box on it. The OUT below rests on
tests that do not wait for a downturn: the money test answers yes on the filer's own history, the filer states it cannot
count on passing costs through, and the deciding part is not shown to be the low-cost operator against the named rival in
any year compared. The field's full-cycle record agrees with them. A castle shown open on the evidence closes OUT
**[M2011-015]**; this is not a castle whose future cannot be judged, the case that "We don’t know how to valuate"
**[M2000-019]** sends to TOO HARD.

- **VERDICT: OUT.** The deciding part, Installation & Maintenance (53.8% of three-year segment gross profit), is a
  fixed-price bid contractor in a field that includes "many small, owner-operated entities", whose position was assembled
  with money in five years **[M2011-015]**, **[M2002-050]**, which cannot count on price increases to pass on its costs
  **[M2005-020]**, which is not shown to be the low-cost operator in a commodity-like field **[L2000-017]**,
  **[M1997-010]**, and whose field protected only ordinary returns over the last full cycle **[L2007-004]**,
  **[M2013-052]**. The file closes here. Q3 to Q12 are NOT REACHED.
