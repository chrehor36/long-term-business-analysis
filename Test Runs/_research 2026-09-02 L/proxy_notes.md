### 1c. Is Loews a "controlled company"? NO, and there is no voting agreement

**Searched the full text of both the 2026 and the 2025 proxy for the phrase "controlled
company": zero hits in each.** Loews does not claim the NYSE controlled-company exemption,
and on these numbers it could not: the family is at 18.85%, far below the more-than-50% of
voting power that the NYSE standard requires.

**Searched for "voting agreement", "shareholders' agreement", and any group-acting-together
language: none disclosed anywhere in the proxy.** No Schedule 13D group is referenced. The
Tisches appear in the principal-shareholders table as separate individual holders, each with
his own footnote, not as a group.

**This is the material distinction, and it cuts both ways.** The Tisch family's control of
Loews is *de facto*, not *de jure*. It rests on (i) an 18.85% block, by a wide margin the
largest; (ii) three of the twelve board seats plus the Chairmanship plus a director emeritus;
(iii) the CEO's office; and (iv) the absence of any other holder above 9.4%, and the two
above 6% are index managers. There is no dual-class stock, no voting trust, no contractual
lock. An outside holder is not legally barred from contesting the board; the proxy's own
2027 advance-notice provision contemplates exactly that, requiring a dissident to state it
intends to solicit holders of "at least 67% of the voting power."

**[E4-23] read.** The key-person exposure here is not one person but one family, and the
family has just handed the operating role from the second generation to the third. What
holds the arrangement together is a block, a board and a name, not a charter provision.

## 2. SUCCESSION DISCLOSURE: THE ABSENCE IS THE FINDING

**There is no CEO succession-planning process disclosed in the 2026 proxy statement.**

The word "succession" appears **exactly once in the entire document** (mechanical count over
the full text of `l-20260331.htm`). That one occurrence is inside the description of the
Compensation Committee's primary role, verbatim:

> The Compensation Committee assists our Board in discharging its responsibilities relating
> to compensation and succession planning for our executive officers. These responsibilities
> include:
> ▪ reviewing our general compensation philosophy for executive officers;
> ▪ overseeing the development and implementation of executive compensation programs; and

Note what follows the clause. The responsibilities enumerated after the words "and succession
planning" are **all about compensation**. Succession planning is named once in the charter
sentence, is then not enumerated, not described, given no cadence, no owner and no criteria,
and is never mentioned again in the document.

There is **no** "Board's Role in Succession Planning" section; **no** discussion of the
Nominating and Governance Committee's role in CEO succession; **no** emergency-succession
language; **no** account of how candidates were identified or evaluated. The phrases "talent
development" and "management development" do not appear at all.

**The absence is remarkable in context.** Loews changed Chief Executive Officer immediately
before the period this proxy covers. James S. Tisch retired in December 2024 and Benjamin J.
Tisch took the office, and the proxy reporting the first full year under the new CEO
contains no account whatever of how that succession was decided. A widely held company that
had just changed CEO would ordinarily devote a page to the process. Loews devotes a
subordinate clause. The plain reading is that succession at Loews is a family matter settled
inside the family, and the proxy does not pretend otherwise.

### 4c. DID THE METRICS CHANGE? NO, stated plainly

**The metrics did not change between the two years.** Annual cash incentive: performance-based
income, bonus pool 4.5% of it, in both years. Long-term: performance-based income per share,
same 100% / pro-rata / zero vesting schedule, in both years. The company-selected measure and
the two-item tabular list are identical.

**The target level rose, it did not fall:** the PRSU per-share target went from **$4.15 (2024)
to $4.55 (2025)**, a 9.6% increase. Both proxies contain the same disciplined sentence, that
the Committee "did not make any changes to the … executive compensation program or metrics
after they were established in the first quarter."

**No metric-switching. No mid-year goalpost move. No deterioration-followed-by-a-new-metric
pattern.** On the narrow question asked, this proxy is clean, and it is clean for the right
reason: the metric has been the same for years and the target was raised.

**But two things did change, and they matter more than a metric swap would have.**

**(i) A new pay element appeared: special SARs, granted only to the incoming generation.**

> Compensation Committee approved special grants of SARs to each of Benjamin Tisch,

would write than to a conventional at-the-money refresh. It is also the entire explanation
of the 56% jump in "compensation actually paid" in 2025, and it accounts for $4.09m of the
new CEO's $8.75m SCT total.

> establish other specific, quantitative criteria or performance measures.

> participant is in excess of the established target), except based on the Compensation

> paid to the pre-established target amounts.

The 4.5% pool is a **ceiling**, not a formula. Everything below the ceiling is judgment.

PRSUs being earned."

**The observation that matters.** The PRSU target was **$4.55 per share** and actual
performance-based income was **$8.96 per share**, so the result came in at **197% of target**,
and in 2024 at **204% of target** ($8.46 against $4.15). The vesting schedule caps at 100% at
target, so a target set at roughly half the achievable level is a target that cannot
plausibly fail. This is the one place where the compensation design does not survive
inspection: the metric never changed, but the bar is set so low that the long-term award is
functionally time-vesting stock with a performance label on it. The Committee's own negative
discretion, applied to cash, has no equivalent on the equity side.

For [E4-27], note the two facts sit side by side: a PRSU hurdle set at half of the likely
outcome, and a SAR grant struck 20% to 141% out of the money. The same Committee wrote both
in the same February.

## SUMMARY OF THE PROXY FOR THE RUN FILE

**What the proxy establishes.**

1. **Control is a family block, not a control block.** Five named Tisches hold 38,781,506
   shares, **18.85%** of 205,767,698 outstanding. Loews is **not** a controlled company;
   the phrase appears zero times in either proxy. And there is **no voting agreement,
   shareholders' agreement or 13D group**. Control is de facto: the largest block by far,
   the Chairmanship, the CEO's office and three board seats.
2. **Succession is undisclosed.** The word "succession" appears **once** in the whole
   document, in a Compensation Committee charter clause, and is never elaborated. A CEO
   change happened and the proxy does not describe how it was decided. Read this as the
   family reserving the decision to itself.
3. **The pay design is mostly owner-like, with one soft spot.** Salaries flat at $1m for
   everybody; CEO paid less than the hotels CEO; pay ratio 93:1 and ~50:1 ex the one-time
   grant; premium-struck SARs at $100/$150/$200 against an $83.01 stock, seven-year cliff.
   The soft spot is the PRSU target of $4.55 per share against $8.96 delivered, a hurdle at
   half the likely outcome.
4. **The metrics did not change.** Performance-based income and performance-based income per
   share in both years, same 4.5% pool, target raised from $4.15 to $4.55. No metric-switching
   after a bad result. But performance-based income exceeded reported net income in four of
   the last five years, so the metric is a management-favourable adjustment of net income by
   construction.
5. **Related-party dealings are minor; the auditor is clean.** Two disclosed transactions,
   both Tisch; Deloitte at $18.2m total with $381k of non-audit fees.

**The open question the proxy cannot answer.** Everything above describes a company run by a
family for the long term with restrained pay and light related-party dealing, and a family
that will not tell outside owners how the next CEO gets picked. That is the [E4-23] exposure
and it is unresolvable from the filings. Name it in the run file; do not pretend it is
researched away.
