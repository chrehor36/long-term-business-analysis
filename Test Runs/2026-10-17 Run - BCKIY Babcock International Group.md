## THE FOUNDATIONS (not a gate)
A share is a business. The test is the question "Would I be happy buying this stock if the market closed for five years?" **[M1997-109]**, and the question below is what the asset "itself, produces", not what the next buyer pays **[M1997-052]**. Two further foundations bear hardest on this name.

**No macro forecast enters.** The company's own case leans on defence budgets, the UK's Defence Investment Plan and a "civil nuclear renaissance" (results statement, 22 June 2026). Those are forecasts of what governments will do, and "macro conclusions are — just never enter into the discussion." **[M2000-094]**. What counts is "the average profitability of the business over time and how strong its competitive mode is" **[M2015-016]**.

**Who is paid to tell you.** The annual report and the results statement lead with "underlying" figures, and in FY26 with figures "excluding Type 31 charge". Those are management's own numbers, and the executives' pay is measured on them (Q6). The rows ask who gains by the framing: "you do not get impartial advice from Wall Street" when "there’s (an) enormous amount of fees possible from one action" **[M2020-037]**.

**Contrary evidence, written down as found** **[M1997-127]**:
1. The FY21 report found "material errors which impact the prior period", and the FY22 report restated FY21 a second time (AR2021 p.174; AR2022 p.159 and note 3).
2. The Financial Reporting Council sanctioned the former auditor over the FY17 and FY18 audits (Final Settlement Decision Notice of 8 March 2023, AR2023, Audit Committee report). A press report found by web search (July 2026; Bloomberg Tax, City A.M. and others; not a filing, flagged) says it fined the same firm again over the FY19 and FY20 audits.
3. The FY22 report names "the past practice of period-end working capital management (principally creditor deferrals and debt factoring)" (AR2022 p.31). The cash flows of the years before FY22 were therefore dressed at the year-end.
4. In FY26 a second loss charge of £140.0m was taken on the Type 31 frigate contract, after an earlier charge in FY24.
5. The submarine-support contract (FMSP) in the deciding part ended on 31 March 2026. It is on a six-month bridge while its replacement, "Gateway", is negotiated with the only customer. The AGM update of 16 September 2026 still describes Gateway as being "finalised".
6. A subsidiary was fined €51m by the Italian Competition Authority in February 2019 over a helicopter association's "tariff list" (AR2019 note 32). The fine was later annulled and recalculated, and about £15m was paid in FY22 (AR2022 p.31).

**What the business is, from the filings.** Revenue in FY26 was £5,177.7m in four sectors (AR2026 note 3):

**The test applied to the deciding part.**
- *Where will it be in ten years?* Understanding means "a reasonable fix on about what the earning power and competitive position will look like in five or 10 years" **[M2012-065]**. The competitive position in ten years can be pictured. The UK nuclear submarine fleet must be maintained where the licensed docks are, and Babcock owns or operates them (Devonport, Clyde, Rosyth). The MOD announced in August 2026 a ten-year, £26bn naval infrastructure programme on those sites (AGM update).
- *The key variables and how predictable they are* **[M1998-044]**. There are three. (1) The volume of UK nuclear support work: tied to a standing fleet and the deterrent, and predictable within a band. (2) The price: set in contracts negotiated with one customer. Some are subject to the Single Source Contract Regulations, under which "the SSRO sets the baseline profit rate for single source contracts let by the MOD on an annual basis" (AR2025 principal risk 2). The rate is not foreseeable to the decimal, but its regime is. (3) Cost-to-complete on long contracts, which drives the reported figures.
- *Do the past statements tell me the future ones?* "do I understand enough about this business so that the financial statements will tell me the information that’s useful to me in making a judgment about what the future financial statements are going to look like" **[M2008-033]**. This is the weakest leg. The FY21 review found material prior-period errors. Among the matters in the former auditor's FY17 and FY18 audits that the regulator sanctioned were the Vanguard refit, the MSDF naval-base contract and the RD57 Rosyth settlement, all in the submarine and dockyard business (AR2023, Audit Committee report). And about £50m a year of revenue comes from re-estimating "performance obligations satisfied or partially satisfied in previous periods" (£50.1m in FY26 excluding Type 31, "broadly in line" with FY25; AR2026 note 3). The current statements since FY22 are read on a consistent basis and say what they are doing. I can read them; I cannot read the decade before FY21 from its own statements, and that weighs at Q4.
- *Important and knowable?* **[M2006-076]** The deciding variable, Babcock's place as the UK's nuclear submarine support provider, is knowable from the filings and the customer's own published plans. The Gateway margin is important and will be knowable when signed.
- *Would the insiders write it down?* **[M2000-105]** Yes. The MOD and Babcock sign multi-year contracts on this work, and the company publishes medium-term guidance. This is not a field whose insiders call the ten-year forecast "too hard".
- *Is the forecast about technology?* The submarine support forecast is about a customer's standing need, not about a technology that could change the economics fast. The company's talk of autonomy and AI (results statement p.5) is about new programmes, not about the deciding part.
- *Doubt.* "if you have doubts about something being into your circle of competence, it isn’t." **[M2002-092]**. My doubt is real but narrow. It concerns the level of margin a monopsony customer will allow, which Q2 and Q7 measure. It does not concern whether the economics can be pictured. The parts that do not decide carry more doubt: Marine's fixed-price shipbuilding has produced two loss charges on one contract in three years. Those parts are carried, not decided on.

**VERDICT: IN, narrowly**, on the deciding part. What matters is "that I understand the economic dynamics of the industry" **[M2011-014]**: one customer, owned and licensed infrastructure, prices set or bounded by that customer. The filing facts: Nuclear's sole-provider position (AGM update, 16 September 2026), its 45.2% share of five-year profit (note 3), and its 99.9% UK revenue. Carried forward: the unreliability of the pre-FY21 statements (to Q4), and the margin set by the customer (to Q2 and Q3).

## Q2 — WHY IS THE CASTLE STILL STANDING, and what keeps it standing ten to twenty years? STOP.
The question: "why is that castle still standing? And what’s going to keep it standing or cause it not to be standing five, 10, 20 years from now." **[M1995-038]**. The file closes on the deciding part, Nuclear (Q1). The other parts are read so that what does not decide is known.

**The castle tests, on Nuclear, each with its filing fact.**
1. *The key factors and how permanent they are* **[M1995-038]**. The factors are the ownership of Devonport Royal Dockyard and Rosyth, the nuclear site licences that let submarines be docked, defuelled and refitted there, and the incumbent operation of the two naval bases. The company calls itself the "sole through-life support provider for the UK's nuclear submarine fleet (including the deterrent)" (AGM update, 16 September 2026). The facilities are being extended at the customer's expense: Devonport's 9 Dock was completed and 15 Dock re-opened in FY26; the Major Infrastructure Programme brought £438m of revenue in FY26 (results statement pp.21 to 22); and the MOD's "Royal Oak" programme of August 2026 puts £7bn into Devonport and £15bn into HMNB Clyde over ten years (AGM update). These factors last as long as the UK keeps a nuclear submarine fleet. They do not depend on "the genius of the lord in the castle".
2. *The money test* **[M2011-015]**. A rival with billions could not build a licensed nuclear submarine dock and its regulated workforce in any span that matters; the customer is the only buyer and would not pay for a second one. The answer is "no", which is the See's answer. **The one attacker that matters is the customer**, who can re-let the operating contracts, take work in-house, or split them. The Gateway replacement for FMSP was, at 16 September 2026, still being "finalised", on a six-month bridge from 1 April 2026 (results statement p.22; AGM update). "one competitor is frequently enough to ruin a business" **[M2012-108]**; here the competitor that could ruin it is the buyer itself.
3. *Pricing power and the agony before a rise* **[M2005-020]**. There is none in the ordinary sense. The price is negotiated with one customer. Single-source MOD contracts sit under the SSRO's annually set "baseline profit rate", and the regulations "are subject to review by the UK Government, which could lead to lower returns for industry" (AR2025, principal risk 2). FMSP carried "agreed savings targets" (AR2023, Audit Committee report). **Through the last inflation:** about two-thirds of group revenue had "some measure of protection for inflation"; the rest were firm fixed-price contracts, and labour is about half their cost base (AR2023 strategic report). A UK pay deal cost "c.£25 million" above what contracts recovered in FY23. Nuclear's margin fell to 5.4% in FY23 "reflecting the impact of future inflation", and rose to 9.5% by FY26 (AR2023 Nuclear review; results statement p.22). The price behaviour shows a business that absorbs a shock and recovers it at the next negotiation, not one that sets its price. "If you name some business that has incredible pricing power, you’re talking about a business that’s a monopoly or a near monopoly." **[M2010-092]**: this is a near monopoly whose customer is also a monopoly, so the power runs both ways.
4. *Volume.* Nuclear revenue grew from £1,009.7m in FY22 to £2,070.4m in FY26 (note 3 of AR2023 and AR2026), on submarine support, Cavendish civil nuclear (+18% in FY26) and the infrastructure programme. Contract backlog in Nuclear fell from £1,983.9m to £1,792.7m in FY26, because FMSP ran down to a bridge.
5. *Would the customer still choose it over the low bid?* **[M2017-009]**. For submarine docking and nuclear refit there is no low bid on the public record; no other UK facility is licensed for it. In the other parts the low bid is real: Marine lost the Swedish frigate competition in May 2026; Land and Aviation win and lose re-competed contracts.
6. *Widening or narrowing* **[M1999-108]**. In physical position it is widening: new docks, the HII partnership on US Virginia-class work, and AUKUS. In terms it is under negotiation: the replacement contract is unsigned at the price date. On the evidence, the position is widening and the margin is not shown.
7. *What could destroy, modify or reduce it, five to fifteen years out* **[M2000-014]**. Three things: a customer decision to change who operates the bases; a re-basing of the single-source profit rate; and the company's own contract execution, which in the FY21 review produced write-offs on Vanguard and the dockyard contracts (AR2023, Audit Committee report).
8. *Ask the competitors.* Not possible from the public record. The report names no competitor (below).
