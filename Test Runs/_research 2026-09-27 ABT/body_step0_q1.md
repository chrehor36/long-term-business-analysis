## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**
- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language.** Abbott is four businesses under one balance sheet, and in each of them it sells something that is used up and bought again. **(i) Devices, about half of sales:** a disposable glucose sensor worn for two weeks and replaced (Libre, $7.6bn), and implants and single-use catheters for the heart and nerves (pacemakers, defibrillators, ablation catheters and the mapping systems they plug into, stents, valve-repair clips, heart pumps, spinal-cord stimulators), bought by hospitals per procedure and paid for by Medicare, insurers and national health systems at rates they set per procedure or per diagnosis (*"DRG and PPS entitle a healthcare facility to a fixed reimbursement based on the diagnosis and/or procedure rather than actual costs incurred"*); sensors are paid through pharmacy and durable-equipment channels with rebates. **(ii) Diagnostics, a fifth:** analyzers placed in hospital and commercial laboratories (Alinity, Architect), often with the instrument supplied against a long reagent contract, after which the laboratory buys Abbott's test cartridges and reagents for every sample run; rapid tests (BinaxNOW, ID NOW) sold in volume; from 2026, Exact Sciences' Cologuard and Oncotype DX, billed per test to payers. **(iii) Nutrition, a fifth:** infant formula (Similac) and adult and pediatric drinks (Ensure, Glucerna, PediaSure, Pedialyte), sold through retailers and, in the US, to state WIC programs that *"through competitive bidding ... obtain rebates from manufacturers of infant formula"*. **(iv) Established Pharmaceuticals, an eighth:** off-patent medicines sold under Abbott brands (Creon, Duphaston, Influvac, Brufen) in emerging markets only, to pharmacies, wholesalers and governments. Gross margin 52.6% (2025) after cost of goods but before $1,682M of amortization; segment operating margin from 18.4% (Nutrition) to 33.7% (Devices). **44% of gross revenues carry rebates** (*"Most of these rebates and allowances in 2025 are in the Nutritional Products and Diabetes Care businesses"*), $4.8bn in 2025, 21.1% of the gross sales subject to them.
- **The scarce input this business controls.** No single one; the filer says *"no single patent, license, or trademark is material in relation to Abbott’s business as a whole."* Segment by segment: **the regulatory approvals and clinical data** that gate each device and test (Class III PMAs for implants, BLAs for blood screening), **the installed base of analyzers** that ties laboratories to Abbott's reagents for the contract life, **the Libre manufacturing scale** that lets a sensor be sold at a price that won access in pharmacy and national tenders, **the brands and WIC contracts** in nutrition, and **the emerging-market brands and distribution** in branded generics. Whether any of these is a franchise in [E3-03]'s sense is Q2's question.
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** **The mechanism, yes**: in 2036 Abbott will still sell sensors, implants, catheters, reagents, formula and branded generics that are used up and reordered, paid for largely by governments and insurers. **The products, not on the filer's own words**: *"Some products in this segment can be subject to rapid product obsolescence or regulatory changes. Although Abbott has benefited from technological advantages of certain of its current products, these advantages may be reduced or eliminated as competitors introduce new products"* (the same two sentences for Diagnostics and for Devices, 10-K 2025, Item 1); *"To remain competitive, Abbott must continue to launch new products and technologies"* (Item 1A). The record already shows it: the electrophysiology business is moving to pulsed field ablation (Volt, approved 2025), rhythm management to leadless pacemakers (Aveir), rapid diagnostics fell from the COVID peak to $2,454M, and the largest growth line (CGM, $7.6bn in 2025) is absent from the 10-K for 2013 (*"In 2014, Abbott received CE Mark in Europe for the FreeStyle Libre System"*, 10-K 2015). That is [E4-04]'s question, carried to Q2 under the 2026-09-20 ruling that applies it there as a competence limit.
- **Relatively simple and stable in character [E3-31]? Understood, with the technology limit named.** The economic mechanism in each segment (a consumable reordered, paid by a third party at a rate the payer influences, defended by approval, contract and brand) can be stated in five minutes **[E4-46]**, and the filer reports each segment's sales, margin, price and volume. What is not simple is judging which device or test generation will win; the framework places that at Q2, not Q1 (*"So [E4-04] is applied as a competence limit, never as a fourth franchise criterion"*), and the unit of the test is the company **[E4-08]**. The same reading gave Q1 IN at Medtronic (2026-09-26), whose products overlap Abbott's device segment. **IN at Q1 is not a finding that the durability of any segment can be judged.**
- **VERDICT: [x] IN**: the money is made visibly, as consumables and implants sold to hospitals, laboratories, pharmacies, retailers and governments and paid largely by third parties, measured by segment, business line, price and volume in the 10-K. **IN is not a finding on the franchise; the technology-durability question is Q2's.**
