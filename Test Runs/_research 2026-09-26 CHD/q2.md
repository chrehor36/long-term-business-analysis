## Q2 — IS IT A FRANCHISE? **[E3-03]**

*Every CHD figure below is read from the filed 10-Ks for 2003-2025 (Items 1 and 1A, the MD&A net-sales component tables and paragraphs, the segment notes and the statements), the 10-Q to 2026-06-30 and the release of 2026-07-31, or from SEC companyfacts checked against the filed statements (Step 0). Extractions in `Test Runs/_research 2026-09-26 CHD/`: `pv.py` (`pv_out.txt`, `pv_out_early.txt`, the consolidated component tables), `pv_cd.py` (`pv_cd_out.txt`, Consumer Domestic), `peers/chdrow.py` (`peers/chdrow_out.txt`, the return series by the CL run's construction plus a goodwill-inclusive denominator; `peers/peers_gw_out.txt` and `peers/row_gw_avg.txt` for the peers).*

**The hypotheses I formed while reading, both stated so they can be attacked [E4-26].** *(a) CHD is a value-laundry and baking-soda company that has bought a portfolio of niche brands; its own 10-K says it meets cost increases "primarily" by cutting costs, it priced below inflation for most of 2010-2017, and the margin step-ups were bought; so it is [E3-43]'s "a business" with a good acquisition habit, not a franchise.* *(b) Over the last decade CHD has raised price about as much as Procter & Gamble while selling more units every year but one, on almost no tangible capital; on the corpus's own separating tests that is a franchise, narrow, like the two this project has already admitted in the same row.* The file does not get to pick. Each is tested on the filings below, the strongest evidence against the verdict reached is set out and answered, and the analyst's own pull is stated.

**[E3-03], criterion by criterion.** *"An economic franchise arises from a product or service that: (1) is needed or desired; (2) is thought by its customers to have no close substitute and; (3) is not subject to price regulation."*

- **(1) Needed or desired [x]: passes.** Detergent, litter, toothpaste, condoms and pregnancy tests are bought every week by households; *"a vast majority of our products are consumer staples"* (2025 10-K MD&A).
- **(3) Not subject to price regulation [x]: passes.** Prices are set by the company, its competitors and its retail customers; the regulation the filer names (FDA for condoms, pregnancy tests and OTC products, EPA for some cleaners) governs what may be sold, not its price.
- **(2) Thought by its customers to have no close substitute: SPLIT, and the filer draws the line itself.**
  - *For the value tier it fails in the registrant's own words.* *"Our global product portfolio consists of both premium (66% of total worldwide consumer revenue in 2025) and value (34% of total worldwide consumer revenue in 2025) brands"*. The laundry detergents, *"our largest consumer business, measured by net sales"*, were for fifteen years described as *"value products, priced at a discount from products identified by the Company as market leaders"* (2002-2016 10-Ks): their proposition to the customer is that they are the cheaper substitute. Item 1, 2025: *"Consumer products, particularly laundry, are subject to significant price competition. As a result, we, from time to time, may need to reduce the prices for some of our products to respond to competitive and customer pressures and to maintain market share."* The 2017 MD&A: *"There continues to be significant product and price competition in the laundry detergent category. For example, P&G markets a lower-priced line of laundry detergents, Simply Tide, which competes directly with our core value laundry detergents."*
  - *For the premium tier it is claimed by rank and not measured.* The 10-K ranks the brands (*"the number one condom brand in the U.S."*, *"the world's number one dry shampoo brand"*, *"the number one alcohol free mouthwash in the U.S."*, *"the number one water flosser brand in the U.S."*, and so on) *"based on dollar share rankings from Information Resources, Inc."*, and files no share figure. Where the filer does say what customers did, it says some found substitutes: private label *"(primarily in the stain fighters, diagnostic kits and oral analgesics categories)"* (OxiClean, First Response, Orajel); VitaFusion *"primarily due to significant product competition coming from new category entrants, including private label"*; Waterpik, *"a growing number of water flosser consumers switched to competitors' value-branded products"*; and *"There has been a decrease in demand for some of our products in recent years, including condoms, as a result of demographic and other changes."*
  - Criterion (2) is therefore decided at the company level by [E3-03]'s own demonstration, below.

- **Two decades, two regimes.** Through 2010-2017 CHD grew units by 41% while its realised price and mix fell by 8%: the units were bought with trade promotion, against *"competitive pricing activity"* and P&G's discounted Simply Tide. Since 2018 it has taken price (+22.7%) and still grown units (+14.0%), with one year, 2022, in which the price cost volume. **Over the last ten years the record is price +20.0% with units +23.4%.**
- **The filer's own account of how it meets cost inflation, printed in every 10-K from 2012 to 2025:** *"Historically, we have been able to mitigate the effects of cost increases including tariffs primarily by implementing cost reduction programs and, to a lesser extent, by passing along cost increases to customers."* (the 2025 wording; 2012-2024 read *"passing along some of these cost increases"*). **That is not [E2-44]'s price-setter describing itself**; it is a low-cost operator's sentence, and it is recorded as the single strongest piece of evidence for hypothesis (a).
- **[E2-44]'s first characteristic, tested directly: *"an ability to increase prices rather easily (even when product demand is flat and capacity is not fully utilized) without fear of significant loss of either market share or unit volume"*.** Mixed, and time-dependent. It failed in 2006 (the filer's own sentence) and in 2022 (−5.1 units for +6.5 of price, a worse ratio than Procter & Gamble's or Colgate's that year); it passed in 2019 (+3.4 / +1.0), 2021 (+3.3 / +1.0), 2023 (+4.4 / +0.9) and 2024 (+1.3 / +3.3), and across the decade price and units rose together. It was not tried at all in 2010-2017.
- **[E2-44]'s second characteristic: *"an ability to accommodate large dollar volume increases in business ... with only minor additional investment of capital"*. Passes at its hardest on tangible capital.** Net sales rose from $2,521M (2009) to $6,203M (2025) while net tangible operating assets went from $690M to $725M (`peers/chdrow_out.txt`). Part of the tangible thinness is payables (*"our continued program to extend payment terms with our suppliers"*, 2019 10-K; the supplier-finance programme of $84.7M at 2025). **It fails on the owners' capital**: goodwill and intangibles rose from $1,633M to $6,139M, 70% of operating capital in 2009 and **89%** in 2025, because the dollar growth was largely bought.
- **[E4-55], units.** The physical series is the best in the household row: +60.7% over 2010-2025 and +23.4% over 2016-2025, against Clorox's units below FY2019 and Kimberly-Clark's negative three straight years (CLX and CL runs). **Recorded as the strongest evidence for hypothesis (b).**
- **[E4-47], nominal earning power.** Operating income ex-items $413M (2009), $724M (2016), $936M (2020), $981M (2021), $1,123M (2025) (charges added back: the Flawless impairment of $411.0M in 2022, the VitaFusion impairment of $357.1M in 2024 and the business-exit charges of $45.6M in 2025; gains taken out: the Flawless earnout reversals, *"acquisition-related business acquisition liability adjustments of $94.0 of additional income"* in 2020 and $98.0M in 2021, which sat inside SG&A). It rose through the 2021-2023 inflation, which Clorox's did not; the margin ex-items drifted from 20.7% (2016) to 18.9-19.1% (2020-2021) and 18.1% (2025).
- **Returns (`peers/chdrow_out.txt`; operating income as filed over the average of the year's and prior year's capital; NTOA as the CL run defined it).** **Pre-tax return on net tangible operating assets 59.8-121.0% in every year 2009-2025 (ex-charges), the top of the household row with Colgate.** **Pre-tax return on operating capital including the goodwill and intangibles bought: 17.8-20.2% in 2009-2016, 14.8-17.5% in 2017-2025 ex-items (8.8% and 12.1% GAAP in the two impairment years; 2020 and 2021 about 15.4% and 14.9% once the earnout reversals are taken out).** The written-off capital (about $0.8bn of impairments) has left the denominator, so these figures flatter the capital actually paid. Incrementally, from 2009 to 2025 operating income ex-charges rose about $710M on about $4.54bn of added operating capital, **about 15.6% pre-tax**, of which the tangible part was $34M. Net income on average equity 12.3-35.4% (14.2% and 17.6% in 2024-2025). [E2-43] names the tangible base *"the best guide to the economic attractiveness of the operation"*; on it CHD is exceptional. On the capital the owners actually supplied, which is mostly the price of brands, it is good, not exceptional, and falling as the purchased share rises.

**The bought record, because the franchise here is partly purchased.** The filer: *"Since 2001, we have acquired six of our seven “power brands.”"* Four purchases have since been written down, sold or exited:
- **VitaFusion and L'il Critters** (Avid Health, 2012, $652.3M acquisitions line): trade name fully impaired in 2024 (*"a full impairment of the $281.3 trade name"*, $357.1M of charges in all), sold 2025-12-31 for $160.3M of proceeds with a further $58.5M pre-tax loss.
- **Flawless and Finishing Touch** (2019, *"We paid $475 million at closing"* plus up to $425M contingent): the contingent liability written down in 2020 and 2021 ($93.7M and $98.0M of gains), the intangibles impaired in 2022 ($349.3M and $61.7M), the brand exited in 2025.
- **Spinbrush** (bought December 2005) and the **Waterpik showerheads** (part of the $1,024.6M Waterpik purchase of 2017): exited in 2025, with the Flawless exit *"approximately $118.0 million of annual Net Sales"* and a $45.6M charge.
- **Waterpik** itself: *"customer distribution losses and a decline in consumer demand, mainly due to lower consumer spending and more customers choosing value brands amid inflation"*; trade name carried at $644.7M with fair value *"at 117% of carrying value, down from 135% in 2024"*.
The survivors are larger than the failures: OxiClean (2006), Orajel (2008), Batiste (2011), Zicam (2020), TheraBreath (2021) and Hero (2022) remain, and the release names TheraBreath, Hero and Zicam as the growth. **The acquisition line is a cost of keeping the premium tier growing, and part of it has bought replacements for brands that faded; [E4-04]'s scope test (*"does the spending defend the same advantage, or buy its replacement?"*) answers both ways here.**

**The remaining Q2 tests.**
- **Untapped pricing power [E3-33] / [E5-28]: no.** The class requires *"a monopoly or a near monopoly"*; the filer says the opposite of untapped pricing (*"to a lesser extent, by passing along cost increases"*), and 2010-2017 shows prices held down, not held back.
- **[E4-37], the agony metric.** The 2025 Item 1A: *"We have implemented price increases and may implement additional price increases in the future, including to account for increased costs, which may slow sales growth or create volume declines in the short term as customers and consumers adjust to these price increases."* Some agony; not a prayer session.
- **[E2-53], dominance: not claimed.** No filed share figure; ranks only.
- **[E2-45], the attacker's test.** In value laundry the attacker is already in the aisle (P&G's Simply Tide, Henkel's Persil and Sun Products, private label). In the premium niches the filer states the barrier is falling: *"e-commerce significantly reducing the barriers for even small competitors to quickly introduce new brands and products directly to consumers"*, and names Starface and Peach & Lily against Hero. CHD's own record is the attacker's record: it has bought the small entrants (Hero, TheraBreath, Touchland, Miss Mouth's) rather than been displaced by them, which is a skill, and a cost.
- **[E4-32], direction.** Gross margin flat in a 41.9-45.8% band since 2009 (the widening from 29.7% in 2002 to 43.7% in 2009 was bought: the 2005 10-K says the Armkel brands *"carry a higher gross margin than the Company’s existing product lines prior to the acquisition"*); operating margin ex-items 20.7% (2016) to 18.1% (2025); return on all operating capital from 17.8-20.2% (2009-2016) to 14.8-17.5% (2017-2025). **Direction fails: the moat is not widening on the capital supplied.** Units and share (the 2026 release: *"our brands once again gained share"*) point the other way for the brands themselves.
- **[E4-36], which cause of success?** Two, in the filings: the minimisation of one variable (cost: *"primarily by implementing cost reduction programs"*, and NTOA flat while sales rose 2.5 times) and a repeated purchase of small brands with growth still ahead of them. The first is ownable and has held for decades; the second depends on the buying continuing to be done well.
- **[E4-04] and [E4-23], the rebuild and the key-person tests.** The core (Arm & Hammer, since 1846; Trojan; OxiClean; Nair; First Response) is defended, not replaced: it is not the rapid-change class, and the perimeter close is refused. **But the premium tier's growth depends on a repeated act of acquisition judgment**, which has failed twice at a cost of about $0.9bn of impairments, losses and exit charges (VitaFusion $357.1M and $58.5M, Flawless $411.0M, the 2025 exits $45.6M) inside fourteen years. That is recorded here as a moat defect, as [E4-23] directs for dependence on management, not as a Q3 strength.
- **The unit of the test [E4-08].** The company. The segment note gives Consumer Domestic (77% of sales) an operating margin of 19.3% in 2025, Consumer International 10.3% and SPD 13.6%; no segment assets are reported (*"Asset information and capital expenditures are not regularly provided to the CODM"*), and no value-versus-premium split of profit, so the franchise part cannot be isolated on return. The company-level demonstration therefore decides, with the value tier recorded as not shown.

**THE STRONGEST EVIDENCE AGAINST THE VERDICT BELOW, hunted hardest [E4-26, E3-41, E4-51].** The verdict below is IN, so the case for OUT is set out first, as its holders would state it:
1. **The filer describes a cost-taker, fourteen years running**: costs met *"primarily by implementing cost reduction programs and, to a lesser extent, by passing along cost increases to customers"*. Procter & Gamble's FY2026 10-K puts price first: *"our continued ability to manage these fluctuations through pricing actions, cost saving projects and sourcing decisions"* (`_research 2026-09-25 PG/tenk_FY2026.txt`).
2. **Its largest business sells on being cheaper than the leader**, against P&G's discount line, and must *"from time to time"* cut price *"to maintain market share"*.
3. **For eight years (2010-2017) its realised price fell 7.7% while it bought 41% more units with promotion**; and when it did price hard, in 2006 and 2022, it lost units.
4. **Its margin was bought, its premium tier was bought, and four of the purchases failed**, one of them (Flawless) inside three years; goodwill and intangibles are 89% of its operating capital, and the return on that capital (15-17%) sits below P&G's, Colgate's and Kimberly-Clark's and has fallen.
5. **Private label and small entrants are named in the filings as taking business** in stain fighters, pregnancy tests, oral analgesics, vitamins and water flossers; condom demand is falling.

**Answered.** (1) The sentence is the filer's, and it is recorded against the verdict; but [E3-03]'s demonstration is a record, not a self-description, and the record since 2018 is price +22.7% with units +14.0%, since 2016 price +20.0% with units +23.4%: that is P&G's ten-year shape (price +22, volume +13, PG run), which this project admitted, with more units. A company that meets cost increases mainly by cutting cost *and* raises price over a decade without losing volume is showing both of [E4-36]'s ownable causes, not neither. (2) Correct, and it is why the class is NARROW and the value tier is recorded as not a franchise (34% of consumer revenue); the CL run admitted Colgate with Personal and Home Care (33% of sales) recorded the same way. (3) The 2010-2017 promotion bought units that stayed (units kept growing after the promotion stopped, and 2018-2025 carried +22.7% of price); 2022 was followed by +0.9 and +3.3 of volume while price held, which is not Clorox's pattern of units that never came back. (4) This is the strongest point and it is not answered away: it is why the direction test fails, why the class is NARROW, and why the acquisition judgment is recorded as a moat defect; but [E2-43] makes the tangible base *"the best guide to the economic attractiveness of the operation"*, and on it CHD is at the top of the row, while even on the purchased capital 15-17% pre-tax is above the good class's number [E5-40] and far from FUL's 7-10%. (5) The named losses (VitaFusion, Flawless, Spinbrush, the showerheads) are gone from the perimeter; the brands that remain grew units in 2025 and in the first half of 2026. **[E3-47]**: a wrongly closed file on an understood business is the costliest error; that weighed against OUT here, where the filings do not show price following cost the way FUL's or Clorox's do.

**The analyst's own incentive, stated [operator rule 9, E4-27, E3-41].** Two pulls, opposite. Every wave 7 name folded in the survival-shape index's recent dated notes closed at Q2 OUT, most on #11 THE PASS-THROUGH, which pulls toward OUT by pattern; and two household peers (PG, CL) were admitted NARROW, which pulls toward IN by analogy. Neither pattern was used as evidence: the verdict rests on CHD's own sixteen years of component tables, its own sentences, and a row whose CHD cells were re-derived here. The case for OUT was written first and at full strength.
