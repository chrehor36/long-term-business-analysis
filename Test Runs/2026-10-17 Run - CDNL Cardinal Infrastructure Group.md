## Q2 — WHY IS THE CASTLE STILL STANDING, and what keeps it standing ten to twenty years? STOP.
The question: "why is that castle still standing? And what’s going to keep it standing or cause it not to be standing five, 10, 20 years from now. What are the key factors? And how permanent are they?" **[M1995-038]**. A moat is what "protects excellent returns on invested capital" **[L2007-004]**.

**What the filer says the castle is** (10-K Item 1): self-performance with in-house crews and equipment, speed (projects "three to four months faster than our competition on average", the filer's estimate), long relationships with national home builders that let it "negotiate contracts rather than compete in lowest-bidder scenarios", scale among North Carolina site contractors, vertical integration into asphalt. These are claims in a section written for an offering; each is tested against the filings below.

**The castle tests, each with its filing fact.**
- **The attacker with money** **[M2011-015]**: the filer answers it in its own Competition paragraph: "Historically, the construction business has not typically required large amounts of capital, which can result in relative ease of market entry for companies possessing acceptable qualifications", with "approximately 75 contractor members in North Carolina who we consider competitors", some "larger than we are". The speakers' failing answer is the field where "you can create another airline. [...] Very easily, and you have people that like to do it" **[M2013-054]**, and "there are some industries that are just never going to have barriers to entry" **[M2012-106]**. The filer's own description of its field is that answer. Its own history confirms it: it entered Charlotte (2023), Greensboro (2024) and Atlanta (2026) by buying or starting small local contractors, which is the entry the paragraph describes, done by the company itself.
- **Pricing power, and the agony before a rise** **[M2005-020]**: Item 1A: "For the past several years, our operating margins have been adversely impacted, and may continue to be impacted, by price increases for certain materials, including fuel, concrete, steel and lumber." Materials are locked by firm supplier quotes at bid, and 99% of revenue in 2024 and 2025 came from fixed-price contracts (Item 1). In Q2 2026, with revenue up 114% and backlog at a record $866M, adjusted gross margin fell from 21.3% to 15.9% and gross margin from 13.9% to 10.8% (8-K 2026-08-11, Ex. 99.1): the company could not price its own boom year to cover subcontracted labor and equipment rentals. The strong position does "manage to pass through increases in raw material costs" **[M2005-017]**; the filing shows the opposite through the last inflation.
- **Would the customer still choose it over the low bid?** **[M2017-009]**: the filing speaks two ways. Item 1, Types of Contracts: "We provide our services using traditional general contracting arrangements, which are predominantly fixed unit price or “lump sum” contracts awarded based on the lowest bid." Item 1, Our Markets and Customers: "we are awarded work through direct negotiation with the majority of our homebuilder customers." The customers are national home builders (Pulte alone 9.9% of 2025 revenue; top ten 45.5%) who buy site work as an input to lots they must sell; at ALGC two customers were 37% of 2025 revenue (8-K/A `0001193125-26-209128`, Ex. 99.1). Negotiation with a concentrated, price-sensitive buyer is not the customer of See's who will not take "the low bid"; it is nearer the buyer of a product "available from many suppliers" **[L2004-003]**.
- **Unit volume and share of mind; the brand**: no brand is claimed; the customer is a builder's purchasing department. The filer offers no share figure for any market; "leading" and "one of the largest" are its own words without a filed number.
- **The low-cost position** **[M1997-010]**, **[M2001-013]**: the filer claims efficiency from self-performance. No filed figure compares its costs with a rival's; its margins can be compared with rivals' (below), and they are higher in 2023 to 2025, the years in which the rivals' are also at their highest. Labor is about 1,480 non-union employees; the product cannot be shipped in from abroad, so **[M2007-116]** does not bear.

Operating margin fell from 11.9% (2023) to 11.4% (2024) to 8.9% (2025) on the filed statement of operations, partly from IPO costs and stock pay.

**The field's returns over the last full cycle, judged in words.** Over the cycle on file (2008 to 2022 for STRL and GVA), the durable listed site and civil contractors earned ordinary returns, with years of losses: Sterling lost money in six of eight years from 2011 to 2016; Granite earned under ten percent on equity in most years and lost money in four. The field's returns turned better from about 2022, which is the same stretch as Cardinal's whole filed record. "you can have only two competitors and they’re still terrible businesses, they beat each other’s brains out" **[M2013-052]**; the framework's convention sends "a field whose durable players earn ordinary returns over a full cycle, on the evidence of the filer's and the rivals' own filings" to OUT, with "ordinary" judged in words.

**Cardinal's own higher returns, and why they are not credited.** Cardinal's pre-tax operating income against the tangible capital it used was high in 2024 (about $35.9M on roughly $54M of equity plus debt and finance leases less cash and goodwill at end-2024, from the 10-K balance sheet), the strongest fact for the castle. It is not credited: (1) every filed year is an upcycle year of the field, and a high return is first read for "a cyclical peak in earnings" **[L1994-009]**; (2) the speakers "would not want to buy things on the basis that these returns would be sustained" **[M1998-016]**; (3) the capital base is small because the owners drew out the equity, which raises a return on capital without saying anything about a moat; (4) the return fell as the company grew: first-half 2026 operating income was $30.3M (8-K 2026-08-11, Ex. 99.1) on a tangible capital base, net of the offering cash, several times the 2024 one after the deals and the capital spending.

**The two readings, and the box.** A change for the better is credited only on the evidence of a full cycle and otherwise sends a file to TOO HARD (NATURE) (Q2, Change). That convention is written for a business whose own record shows the change. Here the question is not a change in this business; it is whether a young firm in a field the filer itself calls easy to enter is different from the field. The evidence on the record runs one way: the filer's own words on entry **[M2012-106]**, fixed-price low-bid and negotiated work for concentrated buyers **[L2004-003]**, materials costs it says hurt its margins **[M2005-017]**, margins narrowing as it grows **[M1999-108]**, and a field whose durable players earned ordinary returns over the cycle **[M2013-052]**, **[L2007-004]**. The castle is not shown to be unjudgeable; on the filer's and the rivals' filings it is shown to be open. "If the answer had been yes, we wouldn’t have done it." **[M2011-015]**. The other reading (TOO HARD (NATURE), re-look at the first fiscal year Southeast lot development falls) is recorded under What in the framework was wrong or unclear; it would not change what the price computation shows.

- **VERDICT: OUT**, **[M2011-015]**, **[M2012-106]**, **[M2013-052]**, **[L2007-004]**: the castle is open on the evidence; the field earns ordinary returns over a full cycle, and nothing filed shows this company exempt from it outside the upcycle.
