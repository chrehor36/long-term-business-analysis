## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**

- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language.** The company rents **1,168 boxes** in
  American malls, lifestyle centres and street locations across the U.S., Canada and Mexico —
  **7.3 million square feet**, an average of about **6,300 square feet** per American Eagle store
  and **5,000** per stand-alone Aerie — designs jeans, tops, bras and leggings, has them cut and
  sewn by **third-party factories, "substantially all" outside North America, primarily in Asia**,
  and sells them at a **36.5-cent gross margin on the dollar** struck after the cost of the goods,
  the buying department, the warehouses and the rent. Out of those 36.5 cents, **26.8 cents goes to
  selling, general and administrative expense** and **3.8 cents to depreciation**, leaving **4.1
  cents of operating income** — and 1.8 of those cents were consumed by impairment and restructuring
  in Fiscal 2025. **Thirty-nine percent of revenue never passes through a store**: it is ordered on
  ae.com, aerie.com or the apps and shipped from a distribution centre or out of store stock. It
  employs **about 45,000 people, 35,000 of them part-time or seasonal**. It owns almost nothing —
  two Pittsburgh office buildings and two distribution centres — and **rents every store**, against
  **$1.70 billion of capitalised operating lease liabilities and $2.24 billion of undiscounted
  future rent**. It carries **no funded debt** and **$238.9 million of cash**. The whole business
  is: *design a t-shirt, have someone in Asia make it, put it in a rented room in a mall, and sell
  it for more than the sum of the cotton, the freight, the tariff, the rent and the sales
  associate.*
- **The scarce input this business controls.** **Two trademarks, and only one of them is scarce.**
  The manufacturing is other people's — *"We design our merchandise, which is manufactured by
  third-party factories … we **did not source more than 10% of our merchandise from any single
  factory or supplier**"* and *"we do not maintain any exclusive commitments to purchase from any
  one vendor."* That is the *opposite* of the SHOE case (where 46% of revenue was Nike's, Skechers'
  and Crocs' brands) — **AEO owns its brands outright and rents nothing but the factories' time.**
  The real estate is rented on a **7-year weighted-average remaining term**. The customer is not
  contracted, though **Real Rewards** is a genuine repeat-purchase asset. **What the company
  controls is the AMERICAN EAGLE and AERIE trademarks — "renewable indefinitely" — plus a
  1,168-store distribution footprint and a loyalty file.** Whether that is a moat is Q2's question,
  not Q1's.
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** **Yes as to the mechanism.** Young
  people will buy jeans and bras; a meaningful share will try them on first. Nothing about the
  physics is changing. What is not stable is *which* logo they want — and that is a Q2 and Q4
  question, tested below, not an understandability one.
- **[E4-46] check:** this decision does not need five months. Two reportable segments, three
  countries, one reporting currency, **no funded debt**, an eighteen-note financial statement set,
  and a product a fifteen-year-old can identify. The filing was read end to end in a session.
  **It is inside the circle.**
