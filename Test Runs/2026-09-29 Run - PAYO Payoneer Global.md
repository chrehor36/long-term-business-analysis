## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**
- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language:** A licensed money transmitter for small businesses that sell or work across borders. A seller in Shenzhen selling on Amazon's American store, or a freelancer in Manila paid by a client in London, cannot easily hold dollars or pounds in a local bank. Payoneer gives each one an account that receives the foreign money as if the seller were local (marketplace payouts and invoices), holds it in several currencies, and lets the seller pay suppliers, spend on a card, or withdraw to a home bank. **It earns in two ways.** (a) **Fees on the money moved**, mostly when the customer takes money out, converts it or spends it (*"Transaction fee revenue principally consists of fees for withdrawals and usage"*, 10-Q), plus fixed fees: *"Revenue from contracts with customers"* **$813.4M in FY2025**, 0.93% of the **$87.5bn** of volume. (b) **Interest on the customers' money while it sits there**, which the customer does not receive: *"Interest income on customer balances"* **$231.6M in FY2025** (22% of revenue) on balances of **$7.9bn** at year-end, invested in deposits, money-market funds, *"U.S. Treasury Securities"* and term deposits, hedged by *"3 % interest rate floors"* on $2.2bn. A small book of merchant cash advances (*"Capital advance income"* $7.7M). Costs: bank and card-network fees (*"Transaction costs"* $165.2M), about 2,540 staff half in Israel, sales and marketing $235.2M, and incentives paid to marketplaces for the customers they bring. FY2025: revenue **$1,052.8M**, operating income **$124.7M (11.8%)**, net income $73.2M.
- **The scarce input this business controls:** **a set of payment licences in the jurisdictions that matter** (money-transmitter licences in *"all U.S. states in which such licenses are necessary"*, an Irish e-money licence passported across the EEA, UK, Hong Kong, Japan, Singapore, Australia, a Chinese licence bought in 2025, an Indian authorization pending), **approval as a payout provider inside the big marketplaces** (Amazon's marketplaces *"generated 21% of our revenues"* in FY2025), and **a network of *"close to 100 banking and payment service providers"*** giving local receiving accounts in many currencies, run under a compliance program built over twenty years. Whether any of this is scarce **relative to the named rivals**, who hold the same kinds of licence and the same marketplace approvals, is Q2's question.
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** **The need, yes; the providers and their economics, the filer itself does not say so.** Small sellers will still need to be paid across borders. But the filer describes its own industry as *"highly competitive, rapidly changing, highly innovative, and increasingly subject to regulatory scrutiny"*, with *"changing and disruptive technologies (including artificial intelligence and blockchain)"*, and names stablecoins and central-bank digital currencies as possible alternatives; and **its profit depends on the level of short-term interest rates**, which no filing can forecast ([E3-32] forbids trying): operating losses of $17.3M, $30.2M and $22.2M in 2020-2022 at near-zero rates, operating income of $103.6-149.0M in 2023-2025 at 4-5%. These bear on Q2's durability question ([E4-04], [E4-57], [E4-58]) and on Q4's range; they do not make the mechanics hard to understand.
- **Why this is inside the circle:** how the business earns is plain from the revenue note and one page of the MD&A: a fee on money moved across borders and the interest on the money while it waits, the second the older banking idea of [E4-60]'s *"very cheap money on the deposit side"*, invested with *"very little risk on the asset side"* (deposits and Treasuries). [E4-46]'s five-minute test is met on the mechanics. **What is not simple** is whether the fee is protected against a dozen licensed rivals and whether the float's yield survives rivals who pay customers interest; that is Q2. The perimeter is one company throughout (Step 0).

Whether either stream is protected is Q2.

### THE STRONGEST EVIDENCE FOR IN (the case against this file's verdict), stated as its holders would state it [E4-51, E4-26]
1. **A regulatory stack that takes decades to assemble.** *"The provision of cross-border payment services is complex and highly regulated, and ongoing changes in the regulatory landscape mean scale, expertise, and technology enabled processes are critical to success. Traditional financial institutions serving SMBs, and even modern fintech companies often lack experience in emerging markets and lack the ability to manage scaled customer onboarding and a global risk management program."* Licences in the United States (every state that needs one), the EEA, the UK, Hong Kong, Japan, Singapore, Australia and China, with India and Canada pending (FY2025 10-K, Item 1); *"close to 100 banking and payment service providers"*; *"over 7,000 trade corridors"*.
2. **Scale and growth.** Volume **$21.5bn (2018) to $87.5bn (2025)**, up every year; revenue **$260.1M to $1,052.8M**; *"nearly 2 million active customers"*; *"a market leader in the global e-commerce and service marketplaces payouts business"*.
3. **The fee has risen since 2020.** Fee revenue per dollar of volume (*"Revenue from contracts with customers"* over volume): **0.77% (2020), 0.83%, 0.93%, 0.90%, 0.89%, 0.93% (2025)** (`q2.py`), with the MD&A crediting *"certain monetization initiatives, continued adoption of our high value services, and ongoing growth in high value regions"* (FY2025). The second half of [E2-44] holds: the capital needed is software (capitalized internal-use software $60.9M in FY2025), not plant.
4. **Free float, and it grows.** Customers leave **$7.9bn** with the company at year-end 2025 and are paid nothing for it; the company invests it in deposits and Treasuries. Balances (*"Outstanding operating balances"*): **$1,686.8M (2019), $3,346.7M, $4,401.3M, $5,838.6M, $6,390.5M, $6,964.2M, $7,894.5M (2025)**, up every year. This is [E4-60]'s *"very cheap money on the deposit side"* with *"very little risk on the asset side"*, and [E5-56]'s *"Yet core deposits regularly increase"*.
5. **A two-sided network inside the marketplaces.** *"We operate a two-sided network, providing services to buyers and suppliers, businesses and contractors, marketplaces and marketplace sellers, and connecting them via a single platform"*; *"We benefit from a strong brand in the markets in which our customers operate, and especially in key markets such as China."* A seller whose receipts, supplier payments and card spending all run through one multi-currency account has a reason to stay. And the filer says it competes favourably: *"we compete primarily on the basis of the quality and functionality of our platform, customer service, data security, pricing and innovation. We believe we compete favorably with respect to all of these factors."* (S-4/A, FY2022 10-K).
6. **An informed buyer paid for it.** Nuvei, a payments company, bid $7.20-7.85 over five months and settled at $7.40, 44% over the unaffected price, with Advent committing to the regulatory approvals (Step 0).

### THE CASE AGAINST (the reading)
1. **Criterion (2) fails in the registrant's own words: its customers have close substitutes, and the filer names them every year.** FY2025 10-K, Item 1: *"Payoneer operates on a global scale and faces a very broad set of competitors"*, naming *"global digital payment platforms like PayPal, Ant Group, Airwallex, or Wise"*, *"SMB-focused B2B payment providers like Bill.com"*, *"merchant service providers like Adyen, Braintree and Stripe"*, and *"local companies that focus on enabling SMBs in one or more local markets to sell on digital marketplaces globally"*; Item 1A: *"In China, for example, we face a highly competitive market with a combination of global digital payment platforms such as WorldFirst and Airwallex and regional payment providers including PingPong and LianLian."* The same list, shorter, stands in the S-4/A of 2021 (*"global digital payment platforms like PayPal or Ant Group"*) and in every 10-K since. The marketplace, which brings the seller, can be its own substitute: *"In some cases, marketplaces provide their own payment capabilities to support payments to sellers"* (FY2023-FY2025; *"will provide"* in FY2022). And pricing is one of the five grounds the filer itself says it competes on (case for IN, item 5). A seller on Amazon choosing among several approved payout providers, and Amazon's own, is a customer who thinks there are close substitutes.
2. **The filer states that it competes on price and may have to cut its fees.** The risk-factor heading in the S-4/A and every 10-K FY2021-FY2025: *"Substantial and increasingly intense competition in the worldwide financial services and payments industry, including on pricing and payment alternatives"* could *"materially adversely affect our margins"* (*"adversely affect"* before FY2023). Under it: *"if competition causes us to reduce the fees we charge in order to attract or retain customers, there is no assurance we can sufficiently reduce our costs in order to maintain our profit margins"* (FY2025; the same clause, with *"successfully control our costs"*, in the S-4/A and every 10-K FY2021-FY2024); FY2022: *"these competitors may be able to offer more attractive fees to our current and prospective customers or other services that we do not provide"*; from FY2024, that rivals *"may choose to become direct participants in local payment schemes, allowing them to offer more attractive fees and therefore to more effectively compete for certain segments or corridors compared to us"*. The marketplace terms are negotiated, not set: *"Some agreements have fee structures that are defined only partly through the contract’s term while the remaining fee structure is subject to market competitive rates and good faith negotiation with the marketplace"* (FY2023 10-K), and Payoneer pays the marketplace for the customers it brings (*"in a few instances, we compensate the marketplace with structured incentives tied to the overall economics of the relationship"*; sales and marketing includes *"customer acquisition costs paid to customers, marketplaces and third parties"*, FY2025 MD&A).
3. **The float's price is contested too, in the filer's own words.** FY2025, new that year: *"the competitive landscape is evolving with both regional and global players broadening their offerings beyond core payments to include financial services like yield and non-financial solutions such as financial management software."* Keeping the interest on a customer's balance is a price the customer pays; a rival that pays the customer yield is cutting it.
4. **[E3-43]'s demonstration is absent: the priced product has never earned its costs in any filed year.** Operating income less *"Revenue from other sources"* (the interest on customer balances plus the small capital-advance income), `q2.py` → `q2_out.txt`, $M: **−9.8 (2018), −13.6, −23.3, −32.9, −77.5, −136.7, −118.4, −114.7 (2025)**, and **−60.5 in the first half of 2026**; as a share of fee revenue −3.9% to −23.1%. **In every year 2018-2025 the whole operating profit, and more, is interest on the customers' money.** [E3-03]'s own passage says the three conditions are *"demonstrated by a company's ability to regularly price its product or service aggressively and thereby to earn high rates of return on capital"* ([E3-43]); eight years of fees that do not cover the cost of earning them is the opposite demonstration.
