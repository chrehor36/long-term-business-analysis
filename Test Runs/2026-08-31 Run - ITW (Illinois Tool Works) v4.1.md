## (c) Is the stated yield a boom-window artifact? — **PASS, and the answer is unusual: the cash is not inflated, but the margin is the thing to watch [E4-41]**

The five-year window is FY2021 to FY2025. Four exogenous or one-time breaks sit inside it and
each is named and handled:

1. **The LIFO-to-FIFO change, Q1 2024.** A **$117M pre-tax cumulative-effect credit to cost of
   revenue**, worth **+70bp of operating margin, +$0.30 of EPS and +90bp of after-tax ROIC**.
   It is **non-cash and was added back on the 2024 cash-flow statement**, so it does not touch
   owner earnings. It does inflate the reported margin and ROIC series. **No figure anywhere in
   this run uses 2024's reported 26.8% margin or 31.2% ROIC.**
2. **The Wilsonart sale, Q3 2024.** A **$363M pre-tax gain, +$1.26 of EPS**, plus a $107M
   discrete tax benefit. Non-operating, added back on the cash-flow statement, **does not touch
   owner earnings.**
3. **Discrete tax benefits** of $20M (2023), $121M (2024) and $48M (2025). These do run through
   cash. The test is the cash-tax series: taxes paid as a share of pre-tax income ran
   **22.0% · 25.8% · 26.8% · 26.7% · 26.5%** (2021 to 2025), rising then flat, and consistently
   **above** the book effective rate. No downward drift, so no distortion to strip.
4. **The 2021–2022 inflation surge.** Organic revenue grew **+12.3% and +12.1%** in those two
   years, almost entirely price recovery, and the margin was then rebuilt with **+210bp of
   favourable price/cost in 2023**. **This is the real [E4-41] item, and it lives in the margin,
   not in the cash.** The 26.3% operating margin now standing rests in part on inflation-era
   pricing that has not been given back.

**And the direction of the window distortion is the opposite of the usual one.** For ITW,
FY2021 and FY2022 are the **two worst owner-earnings years of the seven** ($2,208M and $1,873M),
because roughly $1.6bn of working capital was absorbed into inventory and receivables during the
supply-chain build. So **the five-year mean is depressed, not inflated**: the three-year mean
($2,812M) sits **12.3% above** the five-year mean ($2,503M), and the trailing twelve months to
June 2026 sits higher still at $2,847M. There is no cash boom to strip out.

**Leg (c) passes with a named caveat.** The owner-earnings level is not a boom artifact. **The
operating margin is the exposed variable, and every 100bp of it is 4.9% of five-year mean owner
earnings.** That caveat is carried to Q4's named deaths and to Q5's bottom boundary rather than
buried here.

**STAGE 0 VERDICT: all three legs pass. The framework opens.** The finding carried forward is
that **the dividend record is real and long, roughly half of its growth was levers, and the
cash behind it has grown at under 2% a share for six years.**

## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? [E3-31]

- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language.** ITW makes small, engineered,
  physical things that go inside other people's products and processes: the plastic clip that
  holds a car's headliner, the arc welder in a fabrication shop, the dishwasher in a hotel
  kitchen, the nail gun on a framing crew, the machine that shrink-wraps a six-pack, the
  adhesive in a windscreen, the machine that tests whether a metal part will fail. It sells
  them through 88 separate businesses in 49 countries, mostly direct to industrial
  manufacturers and partly through distributors, and it employs 43,000 people to do it. It buys
  steel, resins and chemicals, which it says are "available from numerous commercial sources,"
  and converts them at a **44.1% gross margin**. Out of that it spends 17.3 points on selling,
  administration and $302M of research, and 0.5 points amortising acquired intangibles, leaving
  **26.3 cents of operating income on every dollar of revenue**, which is far more than any of
  its four closest listed peers earns. Interest takes 1.8 points and tax 5.6, so **19.1 cents of
  every dollar becomes net income**. It employs strikingly little to do this: $2,230M of net
  plant and $1,659M of inventory against $16,044M of revenue, and it spends $419M a year on
  capital. It therefore turns roughly **$3.1bn of operating cash a year**, spends $0.4bn of it
  on plant, and posts almost all of the rest out of the door: $1.8bn of dividends and $1.5bn of
  buybacks in 2025. There is no float, no annuity, no subscription and no deferred-revenue pool
  of consequence. **Backlog is explicitly not a factor.** Every dollar has to be sold again next
  year, in seven cyclical end markets, in 49 countries.
- **The scarce input this business controls: the position of a small, specified, low-cost part
  inside a customer's design or process, multiplied 88 times over.** Not the raw material, which
  the filing itself says is commonly available. Not brand in a consumer sense. What is scarce is
  that ITW's part is drawn into an automaker's assembly sequence, or its welder is what the shop
  bought and trained on, or its oven is what the kitchen was built around, and that the part is
  a trivial fraction of the customer's cost while a failure of it is expensive. That produces
  the specification lock and the price tolerance. **The second scarce input is a management
  practice, and the company says so in its own filing:** "The powerful and highly differentiated
  ITW Business Model is the Company's core source of value creation. **It is the Company's
  competitive advantage.**" That claim is examined at Q2 and it is where the run's central
  question lives.
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** Yes, with one named change.
  Cars, buildings, factories, restaurants and packaging lines will still exist, still need
  fastening, joining, testing, cooking and wrapping, and will still buy those things from
  specialists rather than make them. The format has been essentially the same since 1912 and the
  business survived the 2008–09 collapse, the 2020 shutdown and two inflation shocks with
  positive net income in every year on record. The named change is **which** cars: ITW's
  Automotive OEM segment (20.5% of revenue) is growing with Chinese manufacturers while shrinking
  in North America and Europe, which is a change in customer, not in the nature of the business.
  Carried to Q4 as a quantified death.
- **[E4-46] check:** this decision does not need five months. Seven segments, one business
  model, a short 10-K with unusually granular segment disclosure. It is inside the circle.
- **VERDICT: [x] IN**

## Q2 — IS IT A FRANCHISE? [E3-03]

**The question as posed by the operator: is ITW's 80/20-plus-decentralised-niche-leadership
claim a franchise, or a well-run conglomerate of ordinary businesses?** The answer this run
reaches, and it is not the answer either bias would have produced: **it is a franchise, and the
evidence for it is not the margin.** The margin is the weakest part of the case, because a
margin is a practice and practices can lapse. The strong evidence is the **return on total
capital including the price paid for it**, where ITW is not marginally ahead of its peer set but
two to five times ahead. Class **WIDE**, direction **MIXED: widening on profitability,
stationary on position.** Worked below.

**[E3-03], the three criteria:**
- **Needed or desired [x].** Fasteners, welders, commercial ovens and test machines are bought
  because a production process cannot run without them. Roughly half the revenue is consumables
  and service (Welding consumables, Food Equipment service at +2.8% organic in a flat year,
  Polymers & Fluids, Specialty Products consumables), which is repeat, non-deferrable demand.
- **No close substitute [~] — PARTIAL, and stated as partial.** ITW's own Item 1 says the
  opposite of a monopoly: "With operations in 49 countries, the Company offers a wide range of
  products in a myriad of markets, **many of which are fragmented**, and the Company encounters a
  variety of competitors that vary by product line, end market and geographic area. **Each of the
  Company's segments generally has several main competitors and numerous smaller ones.**"
  It names exactly two competitors in the whole filing (Lincoln Electric and ESAB, in Welding).
  **At the enterprise level there is no "no close substitute" claim to make. At the individual
  part level there very often is, because the part is specified into a design and requalifying
  it costs more than the part.** That is the honest shape of the moat: **not one wide moat but
  88 narrow ones**, and the diversification is what makes the aggregate durable.
- **Not price-regulated [x].**

**Must the moat be continuously rebuilt? [E4-04], scoped as the corpus scopes it.**
The test is whether a lapse in spending destroys the structure or merely narrows it, and whether
the spending defends the same advantage or buys its replacement. ITW's spending defends the same
advantage: $419M of capex on a $2,230M plant base, $302M of research, and 21,800 granted and
pending patents accumulated over a century. **Nothing here is a depleting asset being
periodically replaced.** The Munger competitive-destruction class does not apply: fasteners,
welding consumables and commercial dishwashers are not a rapid-change industry.
**But the second half of [E4-04] must be answered honestly and it is the uncomfortable half:**
"this criterion eliminates the business whose success depends on having a great manager." ITW's
own filing says its advantage is a management practice executed daily: "ITW will continue to
drive 80/20 Front-to-Back practice excellence in every division in the Company, **every day**,"
and "**Great ITW Leaders who have expertise in the ITW Business Model are the critical factor**
in translating the potential of the ITW Business Model into full performance."
**Ruling: this is not the [E4-23] surgeon case and it is recorded here rather than at Q3, per
the guardrail.** No individual is the moat: the practice was built under Nichols in the 1980s,
industrialised under Speer from 2012, extended under Santi, and is being extended again under
O'Herlihy since 2024, and the operating margin rose through all three transitions. It is an
institutionalised system, closer to a manufacturing religion than to a person. **But it is
still a practice, not a position, and that is a real moat defect: it is recorded here at Q2, it
is what makes the [E2-53] dominance class fail below, and it is the mechanism of the primary
named death at Q4.**

  back in the third. **That is the [E2-44] characteristic, demonstrated across a full inflation

- **[E4-37], the agony test, applied to what the filing actually shows.** There is no
  prayer-session narrative in ITW's disclosure; price is discussed as a routine, quantified,
  segment-level variable in every year's margin bridge. That is the yawn end rather than the
  agony end. **But the honest reading is one notch below that:** ITW's own vocabulary is
  "**price/cost**", defined in its own glossary as "the estimated net impact of increases or
  decreases in the cost of materials … versus changes in the selling price." That is price
  *responding to* cost with a two-year lag, not price taken because it can be. Its 2021 release
  says pricing actions "more than offset raw material cost increases **on a dollar-for-dollar
  basis**," which dilutes margin by construction and is an admission, not a boast. **A business
  with untapped pricing power does not recover margin two years late.**
- **[E3-33] and [E5-28], untapped pricing power: NOT CLAIMED, and the scope rule is why.**

  describes many of its markets as fragmented and says each segment has several main competitors
  and numerous smaller ones. The competitor row would not support a near-monopoly claim.
  **No part of the Q5 case rests on unexercised pricing power.**
- **[E2-53], the dominance class: FAILS, and this is the moat's ceiling.** The test is whether
  position rather than execution sets the economics, "good or bad, it will prosper." ITW's own
  filing answers no: its stated competitive advantage is the ITW Business Model, a practice
  driven "in every division in the Company, every day," whose full performance depends on
  "Great ITW Leaders who have expertise in the ITW Business Model." A newspaper monopoly earns
  its returns while badly run. **ITW earns 26.3% because 88 divisions execute 80/20 every day;
  it earned 19.5% in 2012 doing the same things less well.** That is the difference between a
  wide moat and dominance, and it is why [E5-18]'s "capacity to stand a little mismanagement"
  is only partly present here.
- **[E2-45], the attacker's test, the one forward-looking moat test on the shelf.** With ample
  capital and skilled personnel, how would I compete with ITW? **Not at the enterprise level:
  there is nothing to attack.** There is no single product line worth more than about 8% of
  revenue, no shared platform to undercut, no distribution chokepoint to seize. An attacker has
  to fight 88 separate niche businesses, each with a specified position in someone's design,
  each too small to justify a dedicated assault, each defended by a hundred years of application
  engineering. **The diversification is the defence, and it is genuinely hard to attack.**
  The real attack vector is different and it is already running: **not to attack ITW's
  businesses but to outgrow them**, which is what Parker-Hannifin, Emerson and Danaher have done
  by buying growth. The row shows the price they paid for it.
- **[E4-32], direction, which outranks existence. MIXED, and both halves are stated.**

- **[E4-36], which of the four causes of extreme success?** Not extreme maximisation of one
