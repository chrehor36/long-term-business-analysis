## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**
- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language.** FUL buys petrochemical raw materials (*"tackifying resins, polymers, synthetic rubbers, vinyl acetate monomer and plasticizers"*, *"approximately 75 percent of our cost of sales"*, about $1.7bn a year), mixes them to a recipe in regional plants (*"The vast majority of the products sold within any region are produced within the region"*), and sells the glue to manufacturers who put it inside their own products: diapers and wipes, food and drink packaging, cartons, labels, shoes, appliances, cars and trucks, electronics, solar panels, windows, roofing, and medical devices. No customer is more than 10% of revenue. The money is the spread between what the glue sells for and what the chemicals cost (gross margin 25.3-31.1% across 2005-2025), less selling, research and administration of about 20% of revenue, which leaves an operating margin of roughly 8-10%. The glue is a small part of the customer's product cost and, once a formula is qualified in a customer's line, changing it takes testing (*"The substitution of key raw materials requires us to identify new supply sources, reformulate and re-test and may require seeking re-approval from our customers"*). Segment adjusted EBITDA margins, the filer's measure, 2025: Hygiene, Health and Consumable 15.7% ($244.4M on $1,551.8M), Engineering 22.2% ($236.0M on $1,061.8M), Building Adhesive Solutions 15.6% ($134.0M on $860.0M).
- **The scarce input this business controls.** **A qualified formula in a customer's process, and the technical service that keeps it there; not a technology.** The filer: *"Much of the technology we use in our products and manufacturing processes is available in the public domain. For technology not available in the public domain, we rely on trade secrets and patents when appropriate"*, and *"Our business is not materially dependent upon licenses or similar rights or on any single patent or group of related patents."* The raw materials are bought *"on the open market from a variety of producers"*. What is scarce is the qualification and the relationship, and Q2 tests whether it prices.
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** **The mechanism, yes; the mix, no.** The 2005 10-K describes the same trade (industrial adhesives formulated regionally for manufacturers, bought with petrochemical inputs, priced against them); what has changed is the portfolio, by twenty years of purchases (Royal Adhesives in 2017, eleven companies since 2023, AMS pending) toward medical, engineering and building adhesives, and away from paints and flooring. Manufacturers will still glue things in ten years; the named uncertainties are raw-material prices, the customer's own volumes, and the shift to *"sustainable adhesives"*.
- **Relatively simple and stable in character [E3-31]?** Yes: buy chemicals, formulate, sell a small-ticket input to manufacturers, price against the chemicals. Thirty market segments do not make the mechanism complex; they make it broad. Not a business that would take months to understand [E4-46].
- **VERDICT: [x] IN**: the money is made visibly, as a formulator's spread over petrochemical raw materials on an input the customer has qualified. **IN is not a finding on the franchise.**
