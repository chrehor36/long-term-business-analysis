## (c) Boom/bust-window artifact [E4-41] — **FOUR ARTIFACTS FOUND, ALL POINTING THE SAME WAY, ALL QUANTIFIED**
*"Favourable exogenous breaks in the window are named and removed before the mean is trusted."*

1. **THE SPECTRUM TROUGH — the decisive one.** Verizon paid **$47,596M** for C-Band licences in
   2021 and then essentially stopped: $3,653M · $5,796M · **$900M** · **$450M**. Any window
   ending in 2025 therefore sits in a spectrum-spending *trough*, and the three-year window the
   screen used to set its bottom boundary contains almost none at all. [E4-38] names the disease

   either initial or terminal dates"* — and its remedy is to publish **every** window, which
   this run does at Q4. The trough is real, it is not Verizon's doing, and it is about to end:
   H1 2026 spectrum cash is already **$1,155M**, 2.6× the whole of 2025, with **$1.0bn** more
   committed for the UScellular licences. **AT&T has agreed to pay approximately $23 billion**
   to EchoStar for 600 MHz and 3.45 GHz, closing early 2026 (AT&T 10-K, acc.
   0000732717-26-000120). This is not a spend that has gone away.
2. **The 2024 Vertical Bridge $2.0 billion, sitting inside operating cash flow.** The 10-K says
   it: *"Other, net cash flow from operating activities during 2024 included **$2.0 billion of
   proceeds related to the transaction with Vertical Bridge**."* Verizon sold Vertical Bridge
   the exclusive right to lease and operate **over 6,000 towers for ~30 years** for $2.8bn, and
   recorded $2.0bn of it as prepaid rent **in operating activities**. It is the sale of thirty
   years of tower economics, booked in the year's cash from operations. **Stripped.**
3. **The 2025 cash-tax break.** Cash income taxes paid fell to **$3,581M from $5,632M** — from

   exactly the [E4-41] category. Carried as a named uplift rather than removed, because it is
   a rate change with legs, not a one-off — but it is why the 2025 OCF beat its own guidance.

## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? [E3-31]

- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language.** Verizon rents radio waves it
  bought from the government, pipes them through 4G and 5G equipment bolted to towers it mostly
  no longer owns, and bills 147 million American devices about $50 a month each for the right to
  connect. It also runs fibre and copper into about 17 million homes and businesses and bills
  them for internet. That is 94% of the revenue: **$83.7bn of wireless service revenue plus
  ~$13bn of fibre and broadband**, both of them monthly recurring, both of them extremely
  predictable one year out. Around that annuity sits a **handset shop it runs at a deliberate
  loss** — $25.5bn of wireless equipment revenue against $29.0bn of equipment cost, a **$3.5bn
  annual loss**, which is the price of acquiring and keeping the subscriber — financed by a
  **$31.3bn book of interest-free instalment loans to its own customers**, which Verizon in turn
  funds by securitizing them into **$28.8bn of asset-backed debt**. Out of the service revenue it
  pays $27.8bn to run the network, $33.8bn of selling and administration, and charges $18.3bn of
  depreciation, leaving **$29.3bn of operating income, 21.2% of revenue**. Interest takes $6.7bn
  and tax $5.1bn. What is left, $17.2bn, is not the owner's number: **$17.0bn goes straight back
  into the network and, on a cycle, another $6–12bn goes to the government for more spectrum.**
- **The scarce input this business controls: FCC-licensed radio spectrum**, carried at
  **$157.0 billion** — larger than the entire net property, plant and equipment account
  ($109.5bn). Nothing else is scarce. The towers are leased. The handsets are Apple's and
  Samsung's. The fibre is a commodity. The brand is worth something but the filing itself
  concedes the customer can buy the identical service from four other sellers, two of whom
  resell Verizon's own network. **The scarce asset is a government licence, and the government
  issues it to three other people.**
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** The demand side, yes with high
  confidence: Americans will carry connected devices and pay monthly for the connection, and
  three national networks will carry them. **The cost side, no** — and that is the honest
  answer. The last ten years contained one generational spectrum purchase of $47.6bn; the next
  ten will contain the 6G equivalent, and Verizon's own Item 1 says so: *"We anticipate that
  demand for spectrum will continue to increase over time … as well as AI driven data demands
  and future transition to 6G."*
- **What is NOT simple, recorded honestly.** A $31.3bn consumer-finance book; $28.8bn of
  bankruptcy-remote securitizations across ABS Notes, two ABS Financing Facilities and a master
  repurchase agreement selling residual interests; ~$12bn of junior subordinated hybrid notes
  with reset and deferral features issued in eighteen months; $23.5bn of operating leases plus a
  30-year tower sale-leaseback with a $3.2bn future minimum obligation; and a maintenance-capex
  question the framework itself says must be a guess. **The revenue model is simple. The capital
  account is not.** None of it is unintelligible — it is all disclosed and it all reconciles —
  but it is recorded here rather than at Q3, because it is a feature of the business, not a
  disclosure choice.
- **[E4-46] check:** this decision does not need five months. The business was understood inside
  a day. Where the run needed time was the (c) judgment, which is a judgment, not a fetch.
- **VERDICT: [x] IN.** How Verizon makes money is intelligible and the demand side is stable in
  character. What it costs to keep making it is the open question, and that is Q4's.

## Q2 — IS IT A FRANCHISE? [E3-03]

**This is where the run stops, and it stops for the same underlying fact that kills the screen's
number: the moat is a spectrum position, and a spectrum position must be bought again every
generation.** Worked below, criterion by criterion.

**[E3-03], the three criteria:**
- **Needed or desired — [x] YES.** 147 million connections; mobile connectivity is closer to a
  utility than to a discretionary purchase.
- **No close substitute — [ ] FAILS, and it fails in the sharpest possible form.** Verizon's own
  Item 1: *"we compete against other national wireless service providers, including **AT&T Inc.
  and T-Mobile US, Inc.**, as well as various regional wireless service providers. **We also
  compete for retail activations with resellers that buy bulk wholesale service from wireless
  service providers, including Verizon, and resell it to their customers. Resellers include
  cable companies, such as Comcast Corporation and Charter Communications, Inc.**"* Read that
  sentence again. The substitute is not merely close. **For roughly 21 million lines the
  substitute is the identical radio signal, from the identical tower, sold by somebody else at a
  lower price — and Verizon supplies it.** Charter's own 10-K: *"leveraging the cellular network
  of Verizon Communications Inc."* Comcast's: *"Our domestic wireless services are offered over
  Verizon's wireless network."* There is no franchise reading of that fact pattern.
- **Not subject to price regulation — [x] PASSES**, and honestly so. *"The Communications Act
  generally preempts regulation by state and local governments of the entry of, or the rates
  charged by, wireless carriers."* The FCC licenses and allocates the **input**; it does not set
  the **output** price. Criterion 3 is met.
  **But read the criterion-3 pass through [E2-59], which is what it actually is.** Regulation
  *caps* a franchise and *floors* a commodity business. The FCC's spectrum policy is what limits
  the field to three national networks — it is an **administered scarcity of the input**, and
  *"the moat belongs to the **regime**"*, not to Verizon. The corpus's warning about how that
  ends is *"That day is gone."* Here the erosion is already visible and the regulator authored
  it: the same FCC licensed CBRS to Charter (210 Priority Access Licences), permits unlicensed
  offload, and lets the cable operators buy wholesale capacity from the incumbents.

**Must the moat be continuously rebuilt? [E4-04] — YES, and this is the excluded class.**
The framework's own scope test is *"does a lapse in spending destroy the structure, or merely
narrow it — and does the spending **defend the same advantage, or buy its replacement**?"*
(Mitsui's Rhodes Ridge buys a replacement deposit; Coca-Cola's advertising defends the same
trademark.) Verizon's C-Band programme **bought a replacement.** In 2019 Verizon's mid-band
position was inadequate against T-Mobile's newly-acquired 2.5 GHz from Sprint — T-Mobile today
*"controlled an average of 394 MHz of combined low- and mid-band spectrum nationwide … 2.5 GHz:
185"* (TMUS 10-K). Verizon's answer was **$47.6 billion of new licences plus $7.5 billion of
clearing costs**, and it did not defend an existing advantage; it purchased the ability to
compete in the next generation. The next generation after that is named in Verizon's own filing:
**6G.** Depleting-asset logic applies without strain — each band is consumed by a technology
cycle and must be replaced. **[E4-04]: "A moat that must be continuously rebuilt will eventually
be no moat at all."**

**Does success depend on a great manager? [E4-23], the Mayo test.** No — recorded here where it
belongs and not as a compliment at Q3. Verizon's economics are set by its spectrum position and
by three-player conduct, not by a surgeon. The new CEO arrived eleven months ago.

1. **The $157 billion asset that generates no depreciation at all.** Note 4: wireless licences

### Name the specific ways THIS business dies [E2-27, E3-24] — exposure, not experience [E4-40]
**The bull case is stated first, in the form its holders would accept, because a bear case that

> **The case for Verizon, fairly stated.** Verizon is the largest US wireless carrier by
> postpaid phone subscribers, in a three-player oligopoly that no fourth entrant can join — the

> at **10× 2026 guided adjusted EPS** of $4.99–5.04, and the market is pricing a melting ice
> cube that is visibly not melting.

**That case is coherent, most of its facts are correct, and the last one is the crux.** What
follows answers it, mechanism by mechanism, quantified from filed figures.

1. **The spectrum bill is not paid; it is deferred, and the deferral is the whole of the
   valuation. — The base case, not the tail.**
   *Mechanism:* every generation of mobile technology has required a new band, and Verizon's own
   filing names the next one. Over eleven realised years spectrum cost **$6,880M a year**; over
   the last five, **$11,679M**. If the next decade costs the eleven-year average, owner earnings
   are **$8,985M**, not $19,220M — a **53% reduction** to the number the screen priced. If it
   costs nothing at all, they are $15,865M, and the screen is still **19% too high** on capex
   and stock compensation alone. **Likelihood: likely.** The bull's answer — that C-Band bought
   a decade of headroom — may be right for five years and cannot be right for twenty, and the
   corpus's window is *"five years out, or more"* [E5-34].
2. **The cable MVNO takes the growth, permanently, using Verizon's own network. — The
   structural death.**
   *Mechanism:* Charter and Comcast added **3,387,000 mobile lines in FY2025** against Verizon's
   362,000, and 1,657,000 in H1 2026 against Verizon's 239,000. They hold **21.1 million lines**
   at 2025 year end and **22.7 million** at mid-2026. Charter's residential mobile service
   revenue is **~$30 per line per month**; Verizon's postpaid ARPA is $168 per *account*.
   Verizon earns a wholesale fee — non-retail service revenue grew $673M in 2025 — but the
   wholesale fee is a fraction of the retail bill it replaces. Quantified: if the cable
   operators continue to take ~3 million lines a year and half are Verizon retail conversions,
   that is ~1.5 million lines a year off a ~94 million base, **~1.6% of the retail base annually,
   converted from a retail margin to a wholesale margin**. **Likelihood: a real possibility**,
   and Verizon just signed the renewal that guarantees it. *(The counterweight, stated: Charter's
   net adds are decelerating — 2,474 → 2,117 → 1,908, and H1 2026 down 22% year over year — and
   both cable operators are moving their **business** segments to T-Mobile in 2026, which is
   Verizon's loss but also evidence the cable operators are not captive buyers.)*
3. **The 2026 inflection is bought with price and with deferred handset spend, and both run out.
   — The near-term death of the bull case, not of the company.**
   *Mechanism:* the units returned in exactly the quarters that ARPA fell 1.4–1.6% and the
   upgrade rate fell from 3.6% to 2.6%. Quantified: **a 1.6% ARPA decline on $83.7bn of wireless
   service revenue is ~$1.34bn of annual revenue at close to 100% incremental margin** — call it
   **9% of owner earnings at the generous end and 15% at the central case**. And the handset
   deferral reverses: an
   upgrade rate held 100bp below normal for a year pulls forward roughly a full quarter of
   equipment cost, which comes back. **Likelihood: likely.** Q2 2026's revenue was **down 0.7%**
   and its net income **down 22.9%** in the quarter management called *"exceptional."*
4. **The dividend is frozen or the balance sheet is. — The mandate's death.**
   *Mechanism:* forward calls at the generous owner-earnings figure ($15,865M): dividend
   $11,467M, buyback up to $4,500M, leaving **−$100M before a dollar of spectrum or
   deleveraging**. At the central figure ($8,985M) the dividend alone is **128% of owner
   earnings**. Something gives, and it will not be the nineteen-year streak first; it will be
   the buyback, then the balance sheet. **Likelihood of a dividend cut: a low-level
   possibility** — the streak is institutionally load-bearing and the coverage on VZ's own free
   cash flow is 1.75×. **Likelihood of dividend growth staying at or below 2%: likely.**
   **Likelihood of the buyback being suspended if any of 1–3 lands: a real possibility.**
5. **A wireless licence impairment. — The tail, and the filing already flags its neighbour.**
   *Mechanism:* $157.0bn of licences are carried at auction cost and tested on a Greenfield
   valuation. Verizon moved from a **quantitative** test in 2024 to a **qualitative** one in
   2025. Separately, on goodwill, the filing says out loud: *"management believes there is a
   **continued risk that our Business reporting unit may be required to recognize an impairment
   charge in the future**"* — after a $5.8bn impairment in 2023 and a 2024 fair value
   *"marginally in excess of the carrying value."* A licence write-down is non-cash and does not
   touch owner earnings, but with **negative $95.9bn of tangible common equity** it is the item
   that would reprice the credit. **Likelihood: a low-level possibility.**
6. **Refinancing drag — arithmetic, not a scenario.** $39.9bn matures through 2028 at an
   embedded blended rate well below the 5.0% effective rate and far below the 5.7%–6.7% Verizon
   paid on its 2025–26 hybrid issues. Verizon's 2025 issuance ran **5.25%–6.00%** on senior and
   **3.996%–7.166%** on hybrids. Each point of refinancing spread on $39.9bn is **$399M a
   year — 4.4% of central-case owner earnings.** **Likelihood: likely.**
7. **The shareholder's death that is not the company's.** At $50.02 the stock is priced on the
   screen's number. If the market comes to price it on a spectrum-charged number, there is no
   floor at these multiples: on central-case owner earnings of $8,985M capitalised at the bare
