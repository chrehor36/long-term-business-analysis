## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**
- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language:** PFG buys cases of food, drink, paper goods, candy and cigarettes from manufacturers, holds them for three or four weeks in about 154 warehouses (*"Our inventory turns, on average, every three to four weeks"*), and delivers them on its own trucks to about 350,000 restaurants, convenience stores, vending operators, schools, hospitals and cinemas, none of which is more than 10% of sales (*"no customers that comprised more than 10% of consolidated net sales"*). It is paid the gap between what it pays the maker (after the makers' volume rebates) and what it charges the customer, and the price is mostly cost plus a mark-up: *"If the price is by contract, it is either based on a percentage markup over cost or a fixed markup per unit"*; otherwise it is set at order, *"typically based on a product cost that fluctuates weekly or more frequently"*. **FY2026: net sales $67,839.5M; gross profit $8,090.6M (11.9 cents on the dollar); operating expenses $7,203.1M (10.6 cents, mostly warehouse and delivery wages, fuel, trucks and buildings); operating profit $887.5M (1.3 cents)**; interest $413.7M; net income $359.3M. The three segments by the filer's segment profit measure (Adjusted EBITDA, before depreciation, amortization and stock pay): **Foodservice** (broadline distribution to restaurants and institutions from 90 distribution centres) $36,560.3M of sales, $1,293.0M, 3.5%; **Convenience** (Core-Mark: cigarettes, nicotine, candy, snacks and food to convenience stores, 38 centres) $25,965.2M, $475.4M, 1.8%, with cigarette excise tax inside both sales and cost (*"Net sales is equal to gross sales, plus excise taxes"*); **Specialty** (Vistar: candy, snacks and drinks to vending operators, offices, retailers and theatres, 26 centres) $5,056.6M, $360.4M, 7.1%. The mix matters inside Foodservice: independent restaurants pay more per case for more service and buy more of the company's own-label goods (*"independent customers generate higher gross profit per case that more than offsets the generally higher supply chain costs"*); independents were *"42.1%"* of Foodservice sales in FY2026.
- **The scarce input this business controls:** **density and scale**, and little else. The filer names it: *"We believe we have a competitive advantage through economies of scale in purchasing and procurement"*, *"supply chain efficiency, including a growing inbound logistics backhaul network"*, and the own-label *"Performance Brands"* (*"our higher margin products"*); no patents or licences (*"We do not have any material patents or licenses"*). The warehouses, trucks and sales force can be bought or leased by any rival with capital; the filer says rivals have *"greater scale and greater financial and other resources than we do in certain markets"*. Whether scale lets it set the price is Q2's question.
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** **Yes for the business model, on the record.** The old registrant's 2002 10-K describes the same broadline business to the same customers, and its operating margin was 1.2-1.6% in 2003-2007 (*"Operating profit | 87,168 | 75,110 | 70,478 | 63,680 | 72,325"* on *"Net sales | 6,304,892 | 5,826,732 | 5,721,372 | 5,173,078 | 4,602,792"*, $ thousands); the present registrant's was 0.8-1.1% in FY2011-FY2015 (IPO prospectus: *"Operating profit | 160.1 | 115.6 | 114.7 | 110.9 | 102.5"* on *"Net sales | 15,270.0 | 13,685.7 | 12,826.5 | 11,505.9 | 10,594.1"*) and 1.3% in FY2026. Food away from home is not a fast-changing field. **The known drift is in Convenience**: cigarettes, *"sales of which are generally declining"* (Item 1A), are being replaced by other nicotine products (*"a mix shift from cigarettes to alternative nicotine products"*, FY2026 MD&A). That is a mix question inside an understood segment, not a change of business.

The difficulty lies in whether anything protects the spread (Q2) and in the debt and leases carried against it (Q4), not in how it earns.
