## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**
- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language.** Caterpillar designs and builds heavy machines and the engines that go in them and in other things: excavators, loaders, dozers and backhoes for builders and road crews; haul trucks, draglines, shovels and underground loaders for mines; diesel and gas engines, generator sets, industrial gas turbines (Solar) and locomotives for power plants, data centres, oil and gas fields, ships and railways. It sells almost all of them to about 150 independent dealers (*"41 located in the United States and 109 located outside the United States, serving 190 countries"*), who sell, rent, finance and repair them in a fixed territory, and it sells the dealers the replacement parts for the whole installed fleet for the life of each machine (*"MP&E Services Revenues ... totaled $24 billion in 2025"*, about 37% of the $63,980M of MP&E sales). It buys steel, castings, forgings and components, pays a large workforce and runs plants that swing between full and idle with the construction, mining and energy cycles. It keeps the difference between the price the dealer pays and the cost of the machine, and a financing arm lends to the dealers and their customers against the machines, earning a spread on borrowed money. **The money is made in three places with different cycles**: construction machines (volume set by building and road work, dealers' own stocking decisions and the filer's *"price discounting"*), mining machines (set by commodity prices and miners' capital budgets; *"highly cyclical and significantly impacted by commodity prices"*), and engines and turbines (set by power, oil and gas and rail investment; the 2025 growth leg).
- **The scarce input this business controls.** The candidates, stated before Q2 tests them: (a) **the dealer network and the installed base it services**: the largest fleet of its kind in the world, maintained with Caterpillar parts through dealers whose principal business is Caterpillar (*"in most cases sales and servicing of our products are the dealers’ principal business"*); (b) **design control of key components** (*"We believe our ability to control the integration and design of key machine components and innovative technologies represents a competitive advantage"*); (c) **scale in engines and turbines** sold into long-lived installations. Against all three, the filer's own sentences, placed before Q2: *"In all markets, we compete on the basis of product performance, customer service, quality and price. From time to time, the intensity of competition results in price discounting in a particular industry or region."* Whether any of them lets Caterpillar set its price is Q2's question.
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** Yes in kind. The 2000 10-K describes the same three businesses (machines, engines, financial products) sold through the same dealer system; the segment names have changed four times and the products are recognisably the same. What moves is mix (engines and turbines for data centres were the 2025 growth leg, *"backlog believed to be firm was approximately $51.2 billion at December 31, 2025 and $30.0 billion at December 31, 2024"*, with *"the largest increase in Power & Energy"*), emissions rules, electrification of smaller machines, and trade (the 2025 tariffs). None requires predicting which technology wins; whether the margin is protected through the cycle is Q2's question.

A decision in five minutes [E4-46]?** Yes, as a business: machines and parts sold through dealers, a finance arm lending against them, the three segments' sales, profits, assets and price and volume effects filed every year, and the finance arm separated by the filer itself. The difficulty is not understanding how the money is made; it is that the amount swings with three cycles, which is Q2's and Q4's question, not Q1's.
