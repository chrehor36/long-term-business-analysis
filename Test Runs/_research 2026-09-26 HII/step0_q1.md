## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**
- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language.** HII owns the two largest private naval shipyards in the United States: Newport News in Virginia (nuclear aircraft carriers, a share of every nuclear submarine, and the four-year mid-life refuelling of carriers) and Ingalls in Mississippi (amphibious ships and destroyers); plus a services arm, Mission Technologies (engineering, training, IT and cyber work, unmanned vehicles, and running Department of Energy sites). The Navy orders a ship or a block of ships; the contract pays HII its **allowable costs** as it builds, and HII's profit is a **fee** on those costs, negotiated up front and moved up or down by how the actual cost compares with a target (a *"cost-share limit"*) and by customer-scored award fees. In 2025, *"Approximately 46% of our revenues in 2025 were generated under fixed-price incentive contracts, approximately 50% were generated under cost-type contracts, approximately 1% were generated under time and material contracts, and approximately 3% were generated under firm fixed-price contracts."* Which costs count, how overhead is spread and what the pension costs the customer are set by federal rules: *"costs billed to contracts with the U.S. Government are treated as allowable and allocable costs under the FAR and CAS regulations"*, audited by the DCAA. So the money is revenue roughly equal to cost, plus a negotiated margin in the mid-single digits, plus or minus what the yard's own productivity does to that margin over a build of five to ten years. 2025: revenue $12,484M, operating income $657M (5.3%), segment operating income $717M (Ingalls 7.6%, Newport News 5.1%, Mission Technologies 5.0% of segment sales).
- **The scarce input this business controls.** **The only yard able to build and refuel nuclear aircraft carriers, one of two able to build nuclear submarines, the only builder of the Navy's large amphibious ships, and the trained nuclear workforce inside those yards.** Item 1: *"We also are the only company currently capable of building, refueling, and inactivating the U.S. Navy's nuclear-powered aircraft carriers"*; *"We are one of only two companies currently capable of designing and building nuclear-powered submarines for the U.S. Navy"*; *"We are the sole builder of amphibious assault ships and one of two builders of surface combatants for the U.S. Navy"*; about 44,000 employees, *"1,096 Master Shipbuilders at Newport News"*, two apprentice schools. What it does not control is the price (contract rules and the customer's negotiators set it), the volume (Congress appropriates it, a ship at a time), or its labour market (the risk factors name hiring and retention as a live cost problem). Whether that scarcity makes a franchise is Q2's question.
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** **Yes, in mechanism and in customer; the filings describe the same business in 2011 and 2025.** The FY2011 10-K describes the same two yards, the same programs (Ford-class carriers, Virginia-class submarines in the Electric Boat teaming arrangement, LPD and LHA amphibious ships, DDG-51 destroyers, carrier refuelling) and the same contract forms; the FY2025 10-K adds Mission Technologies (bought in pieces 2014-2025) and the Columbia-class submarine subcontract. The programs run for decades (*"Aircraft carriers have a lifespan of approximately 50 years"*; each refuelling *"takes nearly four years to complete"*; the Columbia program *"currently anticipates 12 new ballistic missile submarines"*). Item 1A names what could change: *"future strategy reassessments by the Department may result in decreased demand for our shipbuilding programs, including our aircraft carrier programs"*, and a government yard could take refuelling work. Those are statements about volume, weighed at Q2 and Q4; the way a ship earns is not in doubt.
- **Relatively simple and stable in character [E3-31]?** The earning mechanism is simple (cost plus a negotiated fee on work the customer must have) and stable over fifteen filed years. The difficulty is in the estimates: profit is booked by estimating the cost at completion of ships *"not completely designed"*, and the auditor's critical audit matter is that estimate. That is an accounting judgment the filings disclose year by year (the cumulative catch-up adjustments), not a business that would take months to understand [E4-46]. Not a technology business.
- **VERDICT: [x] IN**: the money is made visibly, as a regulated fee on allowable cost, earned building and servicing ships that only this company (or this company and one other) can build, for one customer. **IN is not a finding on the franchise.**
