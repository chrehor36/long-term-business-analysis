## Q2 — IS IT A FRANCHISE? **[E3-03]**

*Every HII figure below is read from the filed 10-Ks for FY2011 to FY2025 and the 10-Q to 2026-06-30 (Items 1 and 1A, the MD&A segment and reconciliation tables, the statements, and the revenue, segment and retirement notes), or from SEC XBRL companyfacts checked against the filed FY2025 statements (Step 0). Extractions in `Test Runs/_research 2026-09-26 HII/`: `cfparse.py` (`cfparse_out.txt`), `bsparse.py` (`bs_parsed.json`), `roc.py` (`roc_out.txt`), `peers/gdms.py`, `peers/pm.py` (`peers/pm_out.txt`). The segment operating income series was read from the reconciliation table in each 10-K's MD&A (segment operating income = operating income before the FAS/CAS pension adjustment and non-current state taxes); 2011-2015 from the FY2012, FY2013 and FY2015 10-Ks, 2016-2025 from the FY2018 to FY2025 10-Ks.*

**The hypothesis I formed while reading, stated so it can be attacked [E4-26]:** *HII owns the scarcest industrial assets in the United States, the only carrier yard and one of two submarine yards, and sells to one buyer who cannot go elsewhere; but the price of what it sells is not set by HII or by a market. It is set by federal regulation of which costs may be charged, how overhead is spread, what cost data must be certified, and what fee may be earned, and the buyer audits it and can take money back. A near monopoly with a regulated price is [E5-60]'s utility, not [E3-03]'s franchise, and the record should show a regulated margin that the buyer, not HII, moves.* The evidence against it is hunted and answered before the verdict.

**[E3-03], criterion by criterion.** *"An economic franchise arises from a product or service that: (1) is needed or desired; (2) is thought by its customers to have no close substitute and; (3) is not subject to price regulation. The existence of all three conditions will be demonstrated by a company's ability to regularly price its product or service aggressively and thereby to earn high rates of return on capital."*

- **(1) Needed or desired [x]: passes.** The Navy's fleet plan and Congress's appropriations buy carriers, submarines, amphibious ships and destroyers every year; the FY2026 defense appropriations bill funds *"Enterprise (CVN 80) and Doris Miller (CVN 81), along with advance procurement for William J. Clinton (CVN 82)"* (FY2025 MD&A).
- **(2) No close substitute [x]: passes for the nuclear carrier and the amphibious ships; passes narrowly for submarines and destroyers; fails for Mission Technologies.** *"We also are the only company currently capable of building, refueling, and inactivating the U.S. Navy's nuclear-powered aircraft carriers"*; *"We are the sole builder of amphibious assault ships"*. Submarines are built jointly with the one other capable yard under a teaming agreement (*"Work on the reactor plant and the final assembly, test, outfit, and delivery of the submarines to the U.S. Navy alternates between Electric Boat and us"*), and destroyers are split with General Dynamics' Bath yard (*"one of only two companies that constructs, Arleigh Burke class guided missile destroyers"*). The filer's own qualification: *"If a U.S. Government shipyard were to become capable and engaged in the refueling of nuclear-powered aircraft carriers, our market position would likely be significantly adversely affected"*, and *"smaller shipyards have entered the market for surface combatants"*. Mission Technologies, 24% of 2025 segment revenue, competes *"against midsized to large traditional aerospace and defense ("A&D") companies and non-traditional defense technology companies"*, with *"competitive rates"* named as a key factor. For the two shipyards, which are three quarters of the business, criterion (2) is met as well as any product the project has run.
- **(3) Not subject to price regulation [ ]: FAILS, in the registrant's own description of how its price is made.**
  - *The cost that goes into the price is defined by regulation.* FY2025 MD&A, Contracts: *"Unless otherwise specified in a contract, costs billed to contracts with the U.S. Government are treated as allowable and allocable costs under the FAR and CAS regulations. Examples of costs incurred by us that are not allowable under the FAR and CAS regulations include certain legal costs, lobbying costs, charitable donations, interest expense, organizational costs, including certain merger and acquisition costs, and advertising costs."* Item 1: *"U.S. Government regulations determine contractor costs that are allowable and therefore recoverable from the government, and certain costs are not allowable and therefore not recoverable. The U.S. Government also regulates the methods by which allowable costs, including overhead, are allocated to government contracts."* Even the pension enters the price by regulation: *"CAS prescribes the determination, allocation, and recovery of retirement related benefit plan costs on U.S. Government contracts through the pricing of products and services"*, and executive pay is capped for recovery (*"we may be limited in the amount and terms of compensation we are able to offer our executive officers or other employees as a U.S. defense contractor under certain circumstances"*).
  - *The price is audited and can be taken back.* Item 1: *"We are overseen and audited by the U.S. Government and its agencies, including the U.S. Navy's Supervisor of Shipbuilding, the Defense Contract Audit Agency ("DCAA"), and the Defense Contract Management Agency ("DCMA")"*; costs *"determined to be unallowable or improperly allocated to a specific contract are not recoverable or must be refunded if already reimbursed"*; *"The U.S. Government has the ability to decrease or withhold contract payments if it determines significant deficiencies exist in one or more of our business systems."* The statutes named include *"the Truth in Negotiations Act"*, the certified-cost-data law under which a negotiated price is built up from the contractor's own costs.
  - *The profit is a negotiated fee on those costs, not a price the company chooses.* 96% of 2025 revenue was under fixed-price incentive (46%) or cost-type (50%) contracts, both of which *"provide for reimbursement of the contractor's allowable costs"*; *"Negotiated contract fee structures include: fixed fee amounts, cost sharing arrangements to reward or penalize contractors for under- or over-cost target performance, respectively, positive award fees, and negative penalty arrangements"*; award fees *"are determined and earned based on an evaluation by the customer of our performance"*. Under cost-type contracts a cost saving lowers the price; under incentive contracts the saving is shared, and an overrun past the share limit is HII's alone (*"Fixed-price incentive contracts effectively become firm fixed-price contracts once the cost-share limit is reached"*). The shape is the same in FY2011 (*"Flexibly Priced Contracts"*, the same share-limit sentence), FY2016 and FY2020 (55% fixed-price incentive, 38% cost-type); by 2025 the cost-type share had risen to 50%.
  - This is price regulation in the fullest sense criterion (3) names: not a regulator setting a tariff for many buyers, but the one buyer writing, through statute and regulation, which costs count, how they are spread, what data must be certified, what fee may be earned, and what may be clawed back. The project's precedents read it the same way: ICFI (2026-09-25) failed criterion (3) *"FAILS IN PART, on the federal half"* on the same FAR, DCAA and cost-data language; OTTR (2026-09-19) failed it *"by definition"* on regulated tariffs.

- **High returns on capital: yes on the operating assets, and falling.** Pre-tax segment income on net tangible operating assets ran 16-25% in 2012-2023 and 14.2% in 2024. [E3-46]'s *"very high returns on capital employed over time"* is closer to met here than in any Q2 failure this wave; this is the strongest single piece of evidence against the verdict, and it is answered below. The return is high because the capital is small: the customer pays as costs are incurred (the critical audit matter describes revenue recognised *"as the construction of the ship progresses because transfer of control to the customer is continuous"*, with the customer able to *"take control of any work in process"*), shares the cost of new facilities through capital grants ($94M in 2019, $61M in 2018) and state-financed facilities (Ingalls leases the west bank of its yard *"from the State of Mississippi pursuant to a 99-year lease"*), and pays the pension through CAS.
- **Pricing aggressively: no, and the regulation is why.** A fee on allowable cost cannot be raised by the seller; the margin moves when the customer negotiates the next contract's fee and share line, and when the yard's productivity moves the cost against the target. The record shows the second: segment margin **10.1% in 2016 and 5.0% in 2024**, the fall attributed in the filer's own risk factor to costs it could not pass on: *"inflation, changes in trade policy (including tariffs), technical challenges, manufacturing difficulties, delays, workforce-related issues, including labor shortages and reduced productivity"* have *"in the past resulted, and may in the future result, in contract prices becoming less favorable or even unprofitable to us over time"*, and *"the inability to recover increased cost growth have in the past had, and may in the future have, a material adverse effect"*. Net cumulative catch-up adjustments: +$118M (2023), **−$126M (2024)**, −$28M (2025); the FY2025 MD&A: *"The Company’s Newport News segment continues to experience performance challenges in the construction of aircraft carriers and the Virginia class (SSN 774) submarine program"*. A business with [E3-03]'s pricing ability passes inflation on; [E4-47]'s immune class *"retains its earning power in real dollars"*. HII's margin fell by half while revenue rose 77% (2016-2025).

**[E5-60], [E2-59] and [E2-58], the regulated-return doctrine, applied as the framework states it.** [E5-60] is the closest passage on the shelf: utilities *"were usually the sole supplier of a needed product and were allowed to price at a level that gave them a prescribed return upon the capital they employed."* HII is the sole or dual supplier of a needed product, allowed to price at a level that gives it a negotiated fee upon the **cost** it incurs. The difference cuts against HII, not for it: the utility's prescribed return is on capital, so growth in capital raises its earnings; HII's fee is on cost, so the upside is capped by the negotiated fee while the incentive contracts leave the overrun past the share line with HII. [E2-59] states where the benefit of an administered price belongs: *"insulated at least partially from normal market forces"* by *"government intervention"*, the moat belongs to the regime; the framework's own reading is that *"Regulation caps a franchise ([E3-03] criterion 3) and floors a commodity business; neither creates the class."* A shipyard is not a commodity business, so [E2-58] does not describe it; the regulation here does the capping, not the flooring.

- **What the row shows.** The two yards that build the Navy's nuclear ships, one the sole carrier yard and the other the lead submarine yard, earn **the same single-digit margin and it falls in step**: HII 8.4% to 6.3%, GD Marine 9.3% to 7.5%, between 2012-2019 and 2020-2025. **The remaining Q2 tests.**
- **[E2-44], the two characteristics.** *"an ability to increase prices rather easily (even when product demand is flat and capacity is not fully utilized) without fear of significant loss of either market share or unit volume"*: prices are not increased by the seller at all; they are negotiated from certified cost, and the 2021-2024 inflation was not recovered (above). *"only minor additional investment of capital"*: partly met in the past (capital expenditure 2-3% of revenue for most years), no longer: for 2026 the filer expects *"capital expenditures for maintenance and sustainment to be approximately 1.0% to 1.5% of annual revenues and our discretionary capital expenditures to be approximately 3.0% to 3.5% of annual revenues"*, *"due to investments to expand our shipbuilding capacity"*. Fails the first, weakening on the second.
- **[E3-33] / [E5-28], untapped pricing power.** HII is a near monopoly in [E5-28]'s sense for carriers and amphibious ships. But [E3-33]'s class is a business where *"any manager could raise the return enormously just by raising prices"*; here no manager may, because the price is built from certified cost plus a negotiated fee and audited. **Monopoly without the right to price is [E5-60]'s case, not [E3-33]'s.**
- **[E4-37], the agony metric.** There is no price increase to agonise over; there is a negotiation of the next contract's fee and share line with the only buyer, and a request for equitable adjustment when costs outrun the contract (*"large REAs are not uncommon at the conclusion of both new construction and RCOH activities"*).
- **[E2-53], the dominance test.** *"Once dominant, the newspaper itself, not the marketplace, determines just how good or how bad the paper will be."* HII is dominant in its products, and the customer, not HII, determines its economics: the customer writes the cost rules, scores the award fees, sets the fee at each contract, and decides how many ships to buy.
- **[E2-45], the attacker's test.** Nobody with ample capital can build a nuclear carrier yard; this is the reason criterion (2) passes. The attacker that matters is the customer: *"the U.S. Government periodically revisits whether refueling of nuclear-powered aircraft carriers should be performed in private or public facilities"*, and *"future strategy reassessments by the Department may result in decreased demand for our shipbuilding programs, including our aircraft carrier programs."*
- **[E4-55], the physical series.** Units, not dollars: the Virginia-class Block IV contract *"contemplated production of two submarines per year"*; its first boat was delivered in 2020 and *"seven more submarines have been delivered through 2025"*, eight in six years, and the FY2025 MD&A names *"performance challenges"* at Newport News. The physical rate has run below the contracted rate while revenue grew.
- **[E4-04] and [E4-23].** [E4-04] is applied as a competence limit (ruling of 2026-09-20) and is not the ground of this verdict. No single key person is essential; the moat such as it is lives in the yards, the workforce and the customer's need, which endure.
- **[E3-43]'s other clause, recorded:** *"a business, unlike a franchise, can be killed by poor management."* The 2016-2024 margin fall was, in the filer's words, workforce, productivity and supplier-driven; the business stands or falls on execution against a regulated price, which is the "business" side of [E3-43]'s line.
- **The unit of the test [E4-08].** Two shipyards and a services segment sharing one customer and one pricing regime; the shipyards are three quarters of revenue and all of the scarce input.

**THE STRONGEST EVIDENCE AGAINST THIS VERDICT, hunted hardest [E4-26, E3-41, E4-51].**
1. **Criterion (2) is met as strongly as the project has seen.** The customer has no substitute for a nuclear carrier and no second amphibious-ship yard, and cannot build one; the scarcity is permanent on any horizon a run can see.
2. **The returns on operating capital are high**: 14-25% pre-tax on net tangible operating assets every year 2012-2025, and 12-51% on equity. [E3-03] says the three conditions *"will be demonstrated by"* high returns on capital, and [E3-46] asks the return question about the business before the manager.
3. **The same regime lets BWXT earn 17-22%.** If regulation capped the margin, BWXT could not earn three times HII's; so HII's single digits might be HII's execution, and a better-run HII inside the same rules might earn more.
4. **The shelf itself holds a naval shipbuilder.** Berkshire bought 14% of General Dynamics in 1992 (1992 letter, `Shareholder Letters/1992 Letter.txt`, lines 666-686: *"we were able to purchase 14% of the General Dynamics shares that remained outstanding after the tender was completed"*) and held it through 1993.
5. **[E3-47]**: a wrongly closed file on an understood business is the costliest error, and this business is understood.

**Answered.** (1) Criterion (2) passes, and I record it as passing; [E3-03] needs all three conditions, and criterion (3) fails in the registrant's own words on allowable cost, allocation, audit, refund and fee. A product the customer cannot replace, sold at a price the customer regulates, is [E5-60]'s sole supplier *"allowed to price at a level that gave them a prescribed return"*: the scarcity is real and it belongs to the buyer as much as to the seller, because the buyer is equally without an alternative and writes the pricing rules. (2) The returns are high on operating capital because the customer finances the capital (progress payments, capital grants, state-owned yard land, CAS pension recovery) and the fee is set on cost, not on capital. That is the arithmetic of a regulated fee on a thin capital base, not [E3-03]'s demonstration, which is *"to regularly price its product or service aggressively and thereby to earn high rates of return"*: the high return must come **from** pricing, and here the pricing cannot be moved by the seller, the return fell from 25.4% (2016) to 14.2% (2024) when inflation arrived, and the equity return is inflated by pension deficits and buybacks in the denominator [E2-47]. (3) BWXT proves the regime permits a higher fee for a different product and contract form (reactor components, largely cost-type); it does not show that anyone in the regime sets their own price, and it confirms the point that the margin a contractor keeps is decided inside the negotiation with the one buyer. If HII's single digits are execution, then HII is [E3-43]'s "business", whose results depend on management, and a better manager is not a franchise [E2-37, E3-39]. (4) The 1992 letter gives its own reason, and it is the manager and the tender, not the product: *"I began studying the company and the accomplishments of Bill Anders ... Bill had a clearly articulated and rational strategy"*, *"I dumped my arbitrage thoughts and decided that Berkshire should become a long-term investor with Bill."* No ledger row carries that passage and it cannot license a verdict here; read on its own terms, it is a purchase made on the manager inside a price-regulated business, which the guardrail [E2-37, E3-39] says cannot repair Q2. (5) I weighed [E3-47]. The file closes on the pricing mechanism in the registrant's own filing and on a fourteen-year margin record that moved with the customer's rules and against HII when costs rose, not on the manager or the price of the shares; **[E5-42] keeps the judgments apart.**

**Why OUT and not the perimeter close (UNKNOWABLE at Q2).** The 2026-09-20 ruling reserves UNKNOWABLE for *"A name that passes [E3-03] and whose durability cannot be judged from filings"*. HII does not pass [E3-03]: criterion (3) fails in its own Item 1 and MD&A. The durability of the Navy's demand is uncertain in the ordinary way, but that question does not arise when a franchise criterion itself fails. The verdict is about the class of the business, not its quality: nothing here says HII is badly run, or that its yards are not scarce, or that its customer will stop buying ships.
