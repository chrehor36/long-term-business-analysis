- Must the moat be continuously rebuilt? Does success depend on a great manager? **[E4-04]** **Neither is the ground here.** The advantage the filer claims (local stock, trained staff, supplier authorizations, customer relationships) is defended, not replaced each generation, so this is not the rebuilt-from-zero class; and the [E4-04] competence branch (UNKNOWABLE at Q2) does not arise, because nothing about the durability of a repair-parts distributor needs a forecast of technology: the filings answer the question directly. **Key-person dependence [E4-23], recorded as a moat defect of the minor kind:** the relationships sit partly with branch staff, in the filer's words *"key people may leave and compete against us"* and *"we greatly benefit from having employees who are familiar with the products and services we sell, and their applications, as well as with our customer and supplier relationships."* No single manager is the business.
- Primary moat metric, filing-sourced, and its trend: **operating margin and gross margin over twenty-nine years (the [E3-43] pricing half), EBIT on net tangible operating assets beside the other SEC-filing industrial distributors (the [E3-46] return half)**, and the filer's own words on how it competes, read across every 10-K from FY2000 to FY2026.

**WHAT THE FILER SAYS ABOUT ITS OWN MARKET, EVERY YEAR [E3-03] criterion (2).** Read in all twenty-seven annual reports on EDGAR with a business section (FY2000-FY2026; the 10-K/A of 2024 carries none):
- *"highly competitive"*: **in every one of the twenty-seven.** FY2000: *"Competition. We consider our business to be highly competitive. In addition, our markets present few economic or technological barriers to entry, although longstanding supplier and customer relationships may operate as barriers. Competition is based generally on product and service offerings, product availability, price, and having a local presence."* FY2026: *"An increase in competition could decrease sales or earnings. We operate in a highly competitive, fragmented industry."* ... *"Competition is largely focused in the local service area and is generally based on product line breadth, product availability, service capabilities, and price."*
- **"few economic or technological barriers to entry": in all twenty 10-Ks FY2000-FY2019**, in FY2019's words *"our markets present few economic or technological barriers to entry, contributing to a high fragmentation of market share. Longstanding supplier and customer relationships, geographic coverage, name recognition, and our employees' knowledge and experience do, however, support our competitive position."* The sentence left the 10-K when the business section was rewritten for FY2020; **no filing says the barriers rose** (the replacement text lists twelve *"relative advantages"* and keeps *"highly competitive, fragmented"* and price).
- **"non-exclusive distributor": in every one of the twenty-seven.** The goods are other companies' brands: *"Products we distribute are generally supplied to us by manufacturers whom we serve as a non-exclusive distributor."* The authorization can go: *"Many of our relationships with key product suppliers are longstanding, but are terminable by either party"*, and the maker can change the terms or come round the distributor: *"the prices we must pay for their products relative to other distributors or relative to competing brands; the geographic or product line breadth of distributor authorizations; the number of distributor authorizations; supplier purchasing incentive or other support programs; product purchase or stocking expectations; or the extent to which the suppliers seek to serve end users directly."*
- **Stronger rivals, named as a class:** *"Existing competitors have, and future competitors may have, greater financial or other resources than we do, broader or more appealing product or service offerings, greater market presence, stronger relationships with key suppliers or customers, or better name recognition."* And price pressure named: *"We can experience downward pressure on sales prices because of deflation, pressure from customers to reduce costs, shifts in customer preference to less costly products, or increased competition."*
- **The substitute names Applied.** Genuine Parts' 10-K for 2025 (accession `0000040987-26-000003`, read by the GPC run of 2026-09-26 and re-read here from that run's saved text): *"In industrial distribution, our main competitor is Applied Industrial Technologies, Inc., but we also compete against Fastenal Company, and W."* (the sentence continues to W.W. Grainger). The same bearing, belt or hydraulic pump is on the shelf of the other network.
- **The prayer session, in the filer's words [E4-37]:** the 10-Ks for FY2012 through FY2017 carried, in each of the six, *"Raising our prices could result in decreased sales volume, which could significantly reduce our profitability."* (FY2012, the sentence before it: *"Our ability to pass along to customers the increases in our costs depends on market conditions."*) Munger's metric is *"the agony they go through in determining whether a price increase can be sustained"*; this is a company telling its owners it cannot raise price without losing units.
- **Growth by purchase, in the filer's words:** *"Many industries we serve are mature. As a result, acquisitions have been, and will continue to be, important to our growth."* and *"existing and future competitors, as well as private equity firms, increasingly compete with us for acquisitions, which can increase the cost of potential acquisitions and reduce the number of suitable opportunities."*

**[E3-03] criterion by criterion.**
- **(1) needed or desired: passes.** Factories need replacement bearings and hydraulic repairs; *"no single customer accounts for more than 5% of our 2026 sales."*
- **(2) no close substitute: fails, in the filer's own words**, above: a non-exclusive reseller of other makers' brands in a *"highly competitive, fragmented"* trade with *"few economic or technological barriers to entry"* (twenty years of 10-Ks), competing on availability, service and *"price"*, whose largest rival calls it its main competitor. What the customer cannot substitute is speed on a breakdown at a plant near a well-stocked branch; the filer's own paragraph says that is contested *"in the local service area"* by *"local, regional, national, and multinational operations"*, including *"catalog and e-commerce companies"*.
- **(3) not subject to price regulation: passes.** No filing read describes a rate regime.

- **For twenty-four years, FY1998-FY2021, the operating margin never exceeded 7.3%** (FY2008 and FY2018) and averaged **5.2%** as reported (5.5% with the FY2016 and FY2020 impairments of $64.8M and $131.0M added back; FY2009 carried a $36.6M goodwill impairment). **FY2022-FY2026 it ran 9.4-11.1%.**
- **What made the step, from the filer's own MD&A:** gross margin rose only **1.4 points** from FY2021 to FY2026 (28.9% to 30.3%), explained year by year as *"countermeasures in response to ongoing inflation and supply chain dynamics"* (FY2022 and FY2023), LIFO swings (*"a $21.2 million decrease in LIFO expense over the prior year, which positively impacted gross margins by 47 basis points"*, FY2024), *"recent acquisitions"* (23 basis points, FY2025) and *"price contribution and channel execution, as well as favorable mix impacts from the growth in revenues in the Engineered Solutions segment"* (FY2026). The rest of the step, about **2.4 points, is cost spread over more dollars**: SD&A and other operating cost fell from 22.6% of sales (FY2021) to 19.2% (FY2026) as sales rose 53%, of which the inflation years supplied much of the dollar growth. That is operating leverage on a pass-through, the [E3-43] class of *"a business"* earning more because *"supply of its product or service is tight"*, which *"usually does not last long"*, not the demonstrated ability to *"regularly price ... aggressively"*. The FY2026 volume split in the Service Center segment: *"higher volumes of approximately $80.1 million ... and the remainder from positive price contribution"*, i.e. about $68.0M of price on $3.0bn, **about 2.3%**.
- **[E2-44], both halves.** (1) *"an ability to increase prices rather easily (even when product demand is flat and capacity is not fully utilized) without fear of significant loss of either market share or unit volume"*: the gross margin held in the demand falls (27.0% in FY2009, 28.1% in FY2016, 28.9% in FY2020), which is a markup held on suppliers' list prices, but the filer said for six years that raising price *"could result in decreased sales volume"*, and the operating margin fell from 7.3% (FY2008) to 5.7% in FY2009 before that year's $36.6M goodwill impairment (3.8% after it): **not shown**. (2) *"an ability to accommodate large dollar volume increases ... with only minor additional investment of capital"*: each dollar of added sales carries about 20 cents of receivables and inventory net of payables (FY2026: $831.2M + $509.0M - $341.1M on $4,966.7M), and the growth has been bought (**$2,150.9M of acquisitions FY1998-FY2026, 47% of all operating cash in those years**): **partly fails.**

### THE STRONGEST EVIDENCE AGAINST THIS VERDICT, stated as its holders would state it [E4-51, E4-26, E3-47]
1. *"The margin has gone from 7% to 11% in five years and held there through FY2024-FY2026 when volume was flat; that is pricing power arriving, and [E4-32] says direction outranks level."* **Answer:** the step is real and it is the best point for IN. The MD&A attributes it to inflation *"countermeasures"*, LIFO, mix and acquisitions, and the arithmetic puts about 1.4 points in gross margin and about 2.4 points in cost leverage. FY2024-FY2026 are three years at a plateau, not a demonstration through a demand fall; the demand-fall years show the old level holding, not a higher one: before impairments, 5.7% (FY2009), 6.1% (FY2016) and 6.8% (FY2020), and in each of those years acquired goodwill was written off ($36.6M, $64.8M, $131.0M), which is the price of the purchases, not of the shelf. [E3-43]'s *"a business"* earns exceptional profits when *"supply of its product or service is tight"* and *"Tightness in supply usually does not last long"*; three years after the 2021-2023 supply-chain shortage is too soon to tell the two apart, and the question at Q2 is whether the franchise is demonstrated, not whether one could emerge. **If it is emerging, the reversal condition below is how it would show.**
2. *"Return on tangible capital of 29-46% is [E3-46]'s high return on capital employed; this is a good business."* **Answer:** it is a good return on tangible capital, and the row shows the whole trade earns it (Global Industrial 50%, DXP 32%, GPC 28%), because suppliers finance the stock; [E3-43] ties the return to price, and on the capital the owners supplied, goodwill included, it was 12.6-13.5% for four years after the largest purchase. Being a satisfactory business is not the question Q2 asks.
3. *"Technical service, engineered systems and same-day availability near the plant are real differentiation; customers do not shop a breakdown on price."* **Answer:** the filer lists exactly these as its *"relative advantages"* and in the same filing says competition is *"generally based on product line breadth, product availability, service capabilities, and price"* in *"the local service area"*; its named rival offers the same, and the margin leader (Fastenal) earns twice as much by a different model. Differentiation that shows no price premium over twenty-nine years is service, not a franchise.
4. *"The supplier authorizations are the moat: SKF, Timken or Parker will not authorize a new distributor in a territory."* **Answer:** the filer calls itself a *"non-exclusive distributor"* in every 10-K, says the relationships are *"terminable by either party"*, and lists the number of authorizations and direct sales to end users as things the supplier may change. An authorization the maker grants, shares and can withdraw is the maker's moat, not the distributor's.
5. *"Scale buys better purchasing terms and the consolidation of a fragmented trade is a long runway."* **Answer:** the runway is growth, and [E4-20] and [E4-43] price growth at Q4 and Q5; at Q2 the question is price-setting, and the row shows the larger distributors earn more, but Applied, at $5bn, is not the one setting the price. The filer itself warns that *"continued consolidation among suppliers could reduce our ability to negotiate favorable pricing"*.

None of the five turns the filer's own words or the twenty-four-year margin record. Reading (b), OUT on the business, survives its own hardest test.

**The analyst's own incentives [E4-27], stated (operator rule 9).** An unattended cycle with a budget has an incentive to close at Q2, the cheapest outcome, and the last three names (WFCF, III, and GPC the day before) closed there, which is a pull toward pattern. Against it: the recent margin step and the FY2026 return of 46% on tangible capital pull toward IN. I tried hardest against the OUT [E4-26]: I read the competition paragraph in all twenty-seven annual reports rather than the newest one, built the margin series back to FY1998 rather than trusting the five-year window, split the FY2021-FY2026 step into gross margin and cost leverage from the filer's own MD&A, measured the row on two windows so the recent improvement is shown, and stated the rise up the row as the best evidence for IN. What survived is an OUT on the business with a reversal condition written in words.

**THE REVERSAL CONDITION, in words (a Q2 OUT arms no price band; the QLYS ruling of 2026-09-07).** Q2 would be re-opened only on filed evidence of price-setting, not on a lower price: (1) a **consolidated operating margin held at or above about 10% through a year of falling organic volume** (a year like FY2009, FY2016 or FY2020), with the MD&A attributing the hold to price rather than cost cuts; **and** (2) the gap to the margin leaders in the row closing on the gross margin line, not on SD&A leverage (Applied's EBIT margin within about 3 points of Grainger's for three consecutive years, same construction); **and** (3) the filer's own Item 1 and 1A no longer describing competition as based on *"price"* in a *"highly competitive, fragmented"* trade, or naming an exclusive right it holds. Any one alone is not enough.
