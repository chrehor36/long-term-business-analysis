### VERDICT ON THE STREAK: **22 consecutive years, first increase in FY2004, streak intact through FY2025.**

The filed data supports the claim exactly and to the year. FY2000 through FY2003 were flat at
$0.085 — **four flat years, not increases.** The first increase is FY2004 ($0.089 vs $0.085). Every
fiscal year from FY2004 to FY2025 inclusive shows a higher declared dividend per share than the
prior year. That is 22 increases (2004…2025). There is no year in the series where the dividend
was cut, held flat, or paid in stock. **The claim is TRUE and is not a marketing artifact.**

Source for the FY2000–FY2003 flat run: five-year selected financial data, Ex-13 to the FY2004
10-K (acc `0001193125-05-038623`): *"Dividends declared per common share | $.089 | $.085 | $.085 |
$.085 | $.085"* for 2004/2003/2002/2001/2000.

### Where TI makes the claim — and where it does not

**It is NOT in any 10-K.** The word "consecutive" appears exactly once in each 10-K from FY2014 to
FY2025, and in every one of those years it is used in an unrelated context. **No 10-K in the last
thirteen years asserts a dividend-increase streak.**

**It IS in the September dividend 8-Ks**, Item 8.01, Exhibit 99. The streak count in TI's own
words, year by year:

| 8-K filed | Accession | Headline / claim, verbatim |
|---|---|---|
| 2018-09-20 | 0001564590-18-023374 | "Today's announcement marks **15** consecutive years of dividend increases." |
| 2020-09-17 | 0000097476-20-000030 | "Today's announcement marks **17** consecutive years of dividend increases." |
| 2021-09-15 | 0000097476-21-000025 | "Today's announcement marks **18** consecutive years of dividend increases." |
| 2022-09-15 | 0000097476-22-000043 | "…marks **19** consecutive years…" |
| 2023-09-21 | 0000950103-23-013859 | "TI increases dividend 5%, marking **20** consecutive years of increases" |
| 2024-09-19 | 0000097476-24-000036 | "TI increases dividend 5%, marking **21** consecutive years of increases" |
| 2025-09-18 | 0000097476-25-000048 | "TI increases dividend 4% to $1.42 per share, marking **22** consecutive years of increases" |

TI's own count back-solves to a first increase in 2004 (2018 − 15 + 1 = 2004), which is exactly
where the filed per-share series puts it. **TI's public count and its filed financial statements
agree.** This is a clean, verified claim — rare enough to note.

## B.2 The quality of the streak is deteriorating even though the streak survives

The streak is intact; the *rate* is decaying, and the decay is monotonic and steep.

| Increase announced | Raise % |
|---|---|
| Sept 2018 | **24%** |
| Sept 2020 | 13% |
| Sept 2021 | 13% |
| Sept 2022 | 8% |
| Sept 2023 | 5% |
| Sept 2024 | 5% |
| Sept 2025 | **4%** |

And on declared DPS, year-over-year growth: FY2017 +29.3%, FY2018 +24.1%, FY2019 +22.1%,
FY2020 +15.9%, FY2021 +13.2%, FY2022 +11.4%, FY2023 +7.0%, FY2024 +4.8%, **FY2025 +4.6%.**

A streak preserved by shrinking the increment is a streak being defended for its own sake. The
prospective question for Q6 is what happens in the first year where a 3–4% raise cannot be funded
without further borrowing. TI has never faced that test, and management has a 22-year public
commitment that creates an incentive to pass it by any available means.

## B.3 Dividend coverage disclosure went dark

TI used to state the dividend as a share of free cash flow in the 10-K MD&A. It stopped.

| 10-K | Verbatim |
|---|---|
| FY2016 | "…dividends represented **40 percent** of free cash flow…" |
| FY2017 | "Our dividends represented **45 percent** of free cash flow, underscoring their sustainability." |
| FY2018 | "Our dividends represented **42 percent** of free cash flow, underscoring their sustainability." |
| FY2019 | "Our dividends represented **52%** of free cash flow, underscoring their sustainability." |
| FY2020 | "…our dividend represented **62%** of free cash flow, underscoring its sustainability." |
| FY2021 | "…our dividend represented **62%** of free cash flow, underscoring its sustainability." |
| **FY2022** | **sentence removed** |
| FY2023, FY2024, FY2025 | **absent** |

The same ratio disappeared from the dividend 8-Ks after 2021 (Sept 2018: "TI has paid 41 percent
of its free cash flow"; Sept 2020 and Sept 2021: "56%"; Sept 2022 onward: no figure).

**Computed for the years TI stopped disclosing** — dividends paid ÷ (OCF − capex):
FY2022 **73%** · FY2023 **338%** · FY2024 **320%** · FY2025 **192%**.
Against TI's own FY2025 redefined free cash flow of $2,938M, FY2025 dividends alone are **170%**.

The disclosure stopped in the first year the ratio would have exceeded roughly two-thirds, and has
stayed stopped through three years in which the dividend alone exceeded free cash flow. Nothing
false was said. A number that was volunteered while it flattered was withdrawn when it stopped
flattering. That is the pattern Q3 exists to catch.

# PART C — THE CAPITAL-RETURN PHILOSOPHY, VERBATIM, AND HOW IT CHANGED

## C.1 The current statement — FY2025 10-K, Item 7, Overview (acc 0000097476-26-000059)

> We design and manufacture semiconductors that we sell to electronics designers and manufacturers
> all over the world. Technology is the foundation of our company, but ultimately, **our objective
> and the best metric for owners to measure our progress is through the growth of free cash flow
> per share over the long term.**
>
> Our strategy to maximize long-term free cash flow per share growth has three elements:
> 1. A great business model … built around four sustainable competitive advantages …
> 2. **Discipline in allocating capital to the best opportunities.** This spans how we select R&D
>    projects, develop new capabilities, invest in manufacturing capacity or how we think about
>    acquisitions and returning cash to our owners.
> 3. Efficiency, which means constantly striving for more output for every dollar spent.
>
> … Finally, we will remain focused on the belief that **long-term growth of free cash flow per
> share is the ultimate measure to generate value.**

And on the mechanism (FY2025 10-K, Item 1, Business):

> Over a 10-year period from 2016 to 2025, we allocated **$109 billion**, which reinforces the
> importance of discipline in capital allocation. The largest allocation over this period was to
> drive organic growth, which includes investments in R&D, sales and marketing, capital
> expenditures and working capital for inventory. In this period, we allocated about **$24 billion
> to capital expenditures** to support future revenue growth, which will be a greater component of
> free cash flow per share growth going forward, as we are near completion of our six-year
> elevated capital expenditures cycle. Beyond that, we also allocated capital to dividends and
> share repurchases. **Dividends are designed to appeal to a broad set of investors, and share
> repurchases are made with the goal of the accretive capture of future free cash flow for
> long-term investors.** Lastly, for inorganic growth, we consider acquisitions that meet our
> financial and strategic objectives.

Note the buyback rationale: *"the accretive capture of future free cash flow for long-term
investors."* That is a value statement in form — it says repurchases should capture value — but it
names **no price test, no intrinsic-value estimate, and no discipline that could ever fail.** It
is a statement of intent, not a rule. Contrast with the FY2004–FY2025 record in Part D, in which
TI bought most heavily at the highest prices it ever paid.

## C.2 THE LANGUAGE DRIFT — the finding

TI's flagship promise, **"return all free cash flow to shareholders,"** entered the 10-K in FY2017,
survived three years, and was **removed from the 10-K in FY2020 — the first year of the capex
ramp.** It was never restored. Meanwhile it *survives* in the September dividend press releases,
where it was actually strengthened in 2022, with the hedge **"over time"** attached.

So the strongest form of the promise ("all free cash flow") is made **only in the channel that is
not the annual report**, and only with the escape hatch **"over time"** attached. Note also the

**Assessment.** Nothing here is a misstatement. But the promise now lives where it costs least: a
one-page press release, hedged by "over time," while the periodic report that carries the auditor's
signature and officer certifications no longer contains it. A reader who reads only the 10-K would
not know TI claims to return all its free cash flow. A reader who reads only the press releases
would not know the 10-K stopped saying so. Both documents are TI's.

## C.3 The non-GAAP free cash flow DEFINITION was changed in FY2025

| 10-K | Definition, verbatim |
|---|---|
| FY2016–FY2020 | "Free cash flow is a non-GAAP measure derived by subtracting capital expenditures from cash flows from operating activities." |
| FY2021–FY2024 | "Free cash flow was calculated by subtracting capital expenditures from the most directly comparable GAAP measure, cash flows from operating activities…" |
| **FY2025** | "Free cash flow is calculated as cash flows from operating activities (also referred to as cash flow from operations) less capital expenditures, **plus proceeds from CHIPS Act incentives.**" |

Effect on the reported FY2025 number: OCF $7,153 − capex $4,550 = **$2,603M** on the old
definition; **$2,938M** on the new one, a **+12.9%** lift. FY2025 free cash flow margin is
reported as 16.6% of revenue; on the pre-2025 definition it is 14.7%.

The prior-year comparative is unaffected only because CHIPS proceeds were zero in 2024 — so the
change passes visually while lifting the current year. The FY2025 10-K discloses the new
definition plainly in the reconciliation table and does not hide it. But **the definition of the
company's single self-declared headline metric was widened in the year that metric was under
pressure**, and it was widened by adding a government subsidy receipt to a measure whose whole
purpose is to show what the business generates on its own.

Separately, note that **operating cash flow itself is being flattered by CHIPS**: the FY2025 10-K
footnotes cash flow from operations as including "cash benefits of $335 million and $588 million
from the CHIPS Act ITC used to reduce income taxes payable for 2025 and 2024." So the CHIPS Act
appears in the free cash flow calculation **twice** — once inside OCF as a tax reduction and once
as an added-back proceeds line. Both are disclosed. For owner earnings, both must come out.

## D.2 What the price series says

**The average price paid rose in every single year from FY2013 to FY2021 — from $36.98 to
$181.72, a 4.9x increase — with no year of pause.** That is the signature of a program executed on
a calendar, not on a price.

The two years TI spent the *least* were FY2021 ($527M) and FY2023 ($293M) — and FY2021 carries the
**highest average price in the entire series, $181.72.** The year with the largest single spend,
FY2018 at $5,100M, was executed at $103.07, roughly the midpoint of that year's range, in the same
month TI's board authorized an extra $12 billion.

Against **[E5-08] condition 2** — that a repurchase must be made at a price below a conservatively
estimated intrinsic value — the filings offer nothing to test the condition against. Searched
across every 10-K from FY2004 to FY2025:

- **No filing states a price limit, a valuation ceiling, or a repurchase discipline of any kind.**
- The only stated rationale is *"share repurchases are made with the goal of the accretive capture
  of future free cash flow for long-term investors"* (FY2020–FY2025 10-Ks), which asserts the
  outcome rather than specifying the test.
- The authorizations are open-ended in time: *"No expiration date has been specified for these
  authorizations."* As of 2025-12-31, **$18.79 billion remains authorized** under the September
  2018 ($12.0B) and September 2022 ($15.0B) board authorizations.

**Condition 2 of [E5-08] is NOT MET on the disclosed record.** Not because TI is shown to have
overpaid — that is a judgment requiring an intrinsic-value estimate this file does not make — but
because **TI has never published a price discipline that could be checked, and the realized price
series is monotonically increasing.** Under the framework's four verdicts this is not UNRESEARCHED
(there is no document that would resolve it — TI does not disclose one), and it is not IN. It is
the mechanical-execution pattern the ledger warns about.

## D.3 The share count, and what $55 billion of buybacks bought

TI has spent **$54.98 billion** repurchasing **1.238 billion** shares since FY2004. Shares
outstanding went from roughly 1.74 billion (2004) to **907 million** (2025-12-31) — a **~48%**
reduction, which matches TI's own claim in the September 2020 8-K that "as of second quarter 2020,
the company has reduced its outstanding shares by 47%."

But look at the recent end of the series:

| Year-end | Shares out (M) | Change |
|---|---|---|
| 2020 | 919.4 | |
| 2021 | 924.0 | **+4.6** |
| 2022 | 906.0 | −18.0 |
| 2023 | 909.0 | **+3.0** |
| 2024 | 911.0 | **+2.0** |
| 2025 | 907.0 | −4.0 |
| 2026-06-30 | 913.2 | **+6.2** |

**The share count has been flat-to-rising for five years.** In FY2021, FY2023, FY2024 and the first
half of FY2026, share issuance from options and RSUs **exceeded** repurchases. Since 2020 the net
reduction is 12.4 million shares, 1.3%, at a cost of $6.84 billion in repurchases — because most of
that spend went to offsetting dilution rather than retiring float. On the company's own headline
metric, **free cash flow per share, the "per share" denominator has stopped working.**

# PART E — WHAT THE SERIES SHOWS AS A WHOLE

1. **The dividend streak is real and verified: 22 consecutive years of increases in declared DPS,
   FY2004 through FY2025.** TI's public count and its filed statements agree exactly. But the
   increase rate has fallen from 24% (2018) to 4% (2025) in an unbroken decay.

2. **TI has returned more than it earned in 17 of the 22 years.** Cumulative FY2004–FY2025:
   $98.3 billion returned against $86.1 billion of net income (1.14x) and $75.8 billion of
   OCF-less-capex (1.30x). The gap has been financed by the balance sheet — long-term debt went
   from **zero** at the end of FY2008 and FY2009 to **$12.9 billion** at 2026-06-30.

3. **FY2023–FY2025 is the sharpest version of that.** Cash returned exceeded OCF-less-capex by
   3.60x, 3.82x and 2.49x. In FY2025, TI generated $2.6 billion after capex and paid out $6.5
   billion — a $3.9 billion shortfall funded by $1.2 billion of net new debt and a $2.7 billion
   drawdown of total cash. **The dividend alone ($5.0 billion) was 1.9x cash flow after capex.**

4. **Three disclosures moved in the direction of the reader learning less**, each at the moment the
   underlying number turned: the "return all free cash flow" commitment left the 10-K in FY2020 as
   the capex cycle began; the dividend-as-%-of-FCF ratio left the 10-K in FY2022 as it passed
   two-thirds; the definition of free cash flow itself was widened in FY2025 to include CHIPS Act
   proceeds. All three are individually defensible and none is a misstatement. Together they are a
   pattern, and the pattern runs one way.

5. **The buyback has no disclosed price discipline and has been executed at monotonically rising
   prices**, and since FY2020 has not meaningfully reduced the share count.

6. **A $7.5 billion all-cash, debt-funded acquisition of Silicon Labs** was announced 2026-02-04,
   expected to close H1 2027, on top of all of the above.

**Carry forward to Q3 (honest and rational) and Q4 (survival):** the honesty question turns on the
three disclosure withdrawals in §C, not on any false statement. The rationality question turns on
whether a 22-year streak defended at 4% increments, funded past free cash flow by borrowing, while
simultaneously buying back stock at record prices and signing a $7.5 billion debt-funded
acquisition, is capital allocation or is a commitment being honoured because it was made.
