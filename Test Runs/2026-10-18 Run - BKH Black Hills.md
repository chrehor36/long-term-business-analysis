## Q1: CAN I UNDERSTAND IT? Where its economics will be in ten years, inside my perimeter. STOP.
- **The test.** Understanding means "a reasonable fix on about what the earning power and competitive position will look like in five or 10 years" **[M2012-065]**. The key variables **[M1998-044]** of a rate-regulated utility are stated in its own filing: rate base (built by capital spending), the authorized return on equity and the equity share of the authorized capital structure (Item 1 tables: allowed equity returns 9.30% to 10.80%, equity shares 46% to 57%, authorized rate base by jurisdiction), the lag between spending and new rates (Note 2: four gas rate reviews settled and one electric review decided in 2024 and 2025), and customer growth (gas customers 1,116,393 at 2023 to 1,138,152 at 2025; electric 222,340 to 227,100). Fuel, purchased power and gas costs pass through to customers (Item 1, "cost recovery mechanisms that allow us to pass the prudently-incurred cost of natural gas, fuel, and purchased power to customers"), so the commodity price is not a key variable of earnings. Do the past statements tell me the future ones **[M2008-033]**? For the regulated parts, yes: earnings follow rate base and the allowed return, and the record 2019 to 2025 shows return on equity held between 8.0% and 9.2% (Q2).
- **The parts** (Q2's business of parts, which defines "matters" here). Segment operating income 2021 to 2025, from the 10-K tables (FY2023 10-K for 2021 and 2022 on the current segment basis; FY2025 10-K for 2023 to 2025): Gas Utilities 211.2, 244.2, 228.8, 271.3, 320.8 = **$1,276.3M (53.2%)**; Electric Utilities 202.7, 214.3, 248.8, 233.0, 222.5 = **$1,121.3M (46.8%)**. Gas decides. Both are franchised, rate-regulated distribution businesses read the same way. The Google data-center generation (8-K 2026-10-06) is to be owned "through a non-regulated affiliate" under a contract to 2048 and earns nothing before 2027; it is no part of the record and does not matter by the threshold.
- **Where the forecast is hard.** The electric side is changing (coal output down from 2,683 GWh in 2023 to 2,075 GWh in 2025; new data-center loads in Wyoming; Lange II under construction), but the change runs through regulated capital spending that earns the allowed return, which is the economics a ten-year forecast needs; gas faces electrification initiatives (Item 1, Competition: they "could negatively impact demand for natural gas and decrease future growth"; "To date, these initiatives have not had a material impact"). Would the insiders write the forecast down **[M2000-105]**? Utility commissions and the company do exactly that in every rate review: rate base, return and capital structure for years ahead. This is not a field of rapid technological change in the Q1 sense.
- **Doubt test** **[M2002-092]**: none about what the business is and how it earns; the doubts are about the size of the return on added capital and about the regulators, which are Q2, Q3 and Q9 questions, not Q1.
- **VERDICT: IN.** The economics of a franchised, rate-regulated gas and electric distributor are knowable and important **[M2006-076]**, and their ten-year shape can be assessed **[M2000-037]**: rate base times an allowed return, less the lag.

## Q2: WHY IS THE CASTLE STILL STANDING, and what keeps it standing ten to twenty years? STOP.
- **The castle questions** **[M1995-038]**. The key factor is the state franchise: an exclusive right, granted and overseen by eight state commissions and FERC, to distribute gas or electricity in a territory, with "limited competition for the retail distribution of natural gas" and for "the retail generation and distribution of electricity in our service areas" (10-K Item 1, Competition). How permanent: as permanent as the commissions and the municipalities allow (see what could destroy it). How much does it depend on the lord: little; the wires and mains do the work, and the deciding test is the regulator, not the manager.
- **The attacker with money** **[M2011-015]**. Could a rival with billions take a gas distribution territory in Nebraska or Arkansas? Not by building a second set of mains: entry requires a franchise and a commission certificate. The attacks the filing names are of a different kind: independent marketers selling the gas commodity to industrial and commercial customers, and when they win, "we still collect fees for transporting the gas through our distribution network" (Item 1); IPPs bidding for new generation in Colorado and Wyoming under competitive-bidding rules (Item 1); customer self-generation (Item 1A); and municipal condemnation (Item 1A: municipalities "possess the power of condemnation and could establish a municipal utility"; "We have from time to time faced challenges or ballot initiatives on franchise renewals").

## WHAT IN THE FRAMEWORK WAS WRONG OR UNCLEAR
(1) **The regulated-utility paragraph and specific (e) do not say how they combine.** The paragraph credits debt "against the capital spending it funded"; (e) makes the maintenance case central and charges only the growth spending against the growth. When both apply, it is unclear whether the debt credit is set against all capital spending or only against the growth spending, and therefore which product the allowed debt share caps. This run set the credit against the growth spending and tested the cap both ways (not binding either way here); a utility whose debt raised exceeds half its growth spending would close differently on the two readings (NorthWestern, beside, is such a case: its cap binds against growth spending, not against all spending). (2) **"The long-term debt raised over the window"** does not say gross or net of cash; BKH held $183M at the end of 2025 to repay notes due in January 2026, so the gross figure ($930.5M) overstates the debt that funded the window's assets. This run used net debt ($754.1M) and showed the gross beside. (3) **Aggregating the allowed debt share.** The 10-K states it by jurisdiction (43% to 54%) and not at all for three black-box settlements; the text gives no rule for combining them. This run weighted by authorized rate base where both are stated (50.0%). (4) **Specific (a) is written for cycles, not one-off events.** Winter Storm Uri is a one-month event with a four-year recovery; "the last full cycle, peak to peak or trough to trough" does not fit it. This run treated the event and its recovery as the cycle, which made the base the literal window. (5) **Growth between successive cycles is unstable here and (a) meets (c).** The earlier cycle contains the 2016 SourceGas acquisition year; counting it gives 6.42%, leaving it out gives 2.95%, and starting at 2018 gives -0.50%. The text does not say how an acquisition inside the prior cycle bears on the growth credited; this run left the acquisition year out under (c)'s rule against crediting bought growth without charging its price, and showed the literal figure beside. The box does not turn on it (no case earns the floor). (6) **Whose tax, for a utility.** The carryforward convention names a statutory rate; a regulated utility's low book rate is partly excess deferred taxes returned to customers through rates, so the statutory rate would charge them twice. This run charged the book provision and added the carryforward shelter, confessed as a CONVENTION of this run; the framework should say whether deferred taxes from accelerated depreciation count as the company's own tax. (7) **Q6's narrower STOP needs Q7's range, which comes after Q6.** Part VII's write-back rule names only the buyback test; this run extended the same write-back to the all-stock STOP and to the issuance test. The STOP also asks only whether the acquirer's stock is below value; the rows' companion test of relative overvaluation **[L1997-031]** has no place in the text, though here it was the more telling one. (8) **Competitors.** The 10-K names no rival; the brief's fallback was used and confessed. (9) **Tooling.** `tools/run.py` prints `LiabilitiesOtherThanLongtermDebtNoncurrent` as a debt line (d3), which for this filer is the total of deferred credits and other liabilities ($1,523M at 2025), so the tool's debt sum overstates debt; it should be dropped from the debt table.
