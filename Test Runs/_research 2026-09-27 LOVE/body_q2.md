## Q2 — IS IT A FRANCHISE? **[E3-03]**

*Every Lovesac figure below is read from its filed 10-Ks for fiscal 2019 to fiscal 2026, the 2018 prospectus (fiscal 2017 and 2018), and the 10-Q to 2026-08-02 (accessions in Step 0), or from SEC companyfacts checked against those statements. Scripts and outputs, all in `Test Runs/_research 2026-09-27 LOVE/`: `xb.py` (`xb_out.txt`), `q2_series.py` (`q2_series.txt`), `pricegrep.py` (`price_sentences.txt`, every MD&A sentence on pricing, promotion and comparable sales, fiscal 2019 to fiscal 2026), `peers/row.py` (`peers/row_all.txt`), `peers/summary.py` (`peers/row_summary.txt`).*

**The question this file has to answer: does Lovesac set its prices, or buy its demand?** It owns a patented product no one else may copy until the 2030s, a compatibility promise that ties an installed base to its parts and covers, and a gross margin above most of its trade. **The two hypotheses, stated so they can be attacked [E4-26].** *(a) A franchise of design and installed base: the patented modular couch has no close substitute, owners come back for seats, sides and covers, and the gross margin shows the pricing.* *(b) A furniture retailer with a clever product, whose wide gross margin is spent every year on showrooms and advertising to find the next buyer, and whose prices move with promotion and with its costs, as a retailer's do.* Each is tested on the filings below.

**How the filer describes its competition and its customer.** *"Our business is rapidly evolving and intensely competitive. Retailers compete based on a variety of factors, including design, quality, price and customer service. Levels of competition and the ability of our competitors to attract customers through competitive pricing or other factors may impact our results of operations. Our competition includes furniture stores, big box retailers, department stores, specialty retailers and online furniture retailers and marketplaces."* Of its own main product: *"We believe our Sactionals platform is unlike competing products in its adaptability yet is comparable aesthetically to similarly priced premium couches and sectionals."* Risk factors: *"competitors with whom we compete, or who can obtain better pricing, more favorable contractual terms and conditions, or more favorable allocations of products during periods of limited supply may be able to offer lower prices than we are able to offer. Our operating results and financial condition may be adversely affected by these and other industry-wide pricing pressures."* And: *"In recessionary periods and other periods where disposable income is adversely affected, we may have to increase the number of promotional sales or otherwise dispose of inventory we have previously paid to manufacture"* (10-K fiscal 2026, Items 1 and 1A). The patents: *"We believe that our patent portfolio, combined with our innovative design approach may deter others from attempting to imitate or replicate our products."* **The customer is a household choosing a couch; the filer's own sentence sets the product beside "similarly priced premium couches and sectionals."**

**Read against [E3-43]'s demonstration** (*"The existence of all three conditions will be demonstrated by a company's ability to regularly price its product or service aggressively and thereby to earn high rates of return on capital"*): the pricing half is not shown. **The margin rises when discounting falls and falls when discounting rises**, and discounting fell only in the two pandemic years when every furniture seller's demand ran ahead of supply. **In four consecutive years of slack demand, fiscal 2023 to fiscal 2026, the filer reports higher promotional discounting every year**, and it bought comparable sales with *"strong promotional campaigns"* in two of them. **When costs rose, price did not follow in the year it mattered**: the 25% China tariffs of fiscal 2020 took 480 basis points of gross margin; the price increases of fiscal 2026 and 2027 arrived with the second tariff shock and were *"partially offset by higher promotional discounting"* while new customers fell 5.6% and then 2.6%. The 10-Ks for fiscal 2023, 2024 and 2025 said the brand *"commands premium pricing, as we feel lowering prices may negatively affect the perception of our products"*; **the fiscal 2026 10-K drops the sentence**, and a putative class action filed 2024-03-21 (*Nguyen*, pending on a second motion to dismiss) *"generally alleges that the Company falsely advertised discounts on certain products"* (Note 7). The list price holds; the price actually paid moves with promotion. That is [E4-37]'s agony case read from the other side: not a prayer session before a price rise, but a promotional calendar the filer calls its peak season (*"Black Friday ... the holiday season and our related promotional and marketing campaigns"*, 35.7% of fiscal 2026 sales in the fourth quarter).

**[E2-44], the two characteristics.** (1) *"an ability to increase prices rather easily (even when product demand is flat and capacity is not fully utilized) without fear of significant loss of either market share or unit volume"*: **not met.** In the flat-demand years the filer raised promotion, not price; when it did raise price (fiscal 2026 to 2027, under tariffs) it paired it with deeper promotion and lost new customers. (2) *"an ability to accommodate large dollar volume increases in business ... with only minor additional investment of capital"*: **not met on the cash record.** Sales grew about nine-fold, $76.3M to $697.1M, over fiscal 2017-2026; over the same ten years operating cash was $189.1M, stock compensation $48.2M and capital spending $157.6M, so **owner earnings at the capex end summed to minus $16.6M** (plus $60.5M at the D&A end), while shareholders put in paid-in capital of $194.8M against accumulated earnings of $23.9M at 2026-02-01, and the company signed $238.2M of showroom and office lease obligations. The growth was paid for from outside.

- **Peers taken: eleven SEC filers**, retailers and makers of furniture and home furnishings, including the direct-to-consumer sellers with showrooms (RH, Arhaus, Ethan Allen, Haverty's, Bassett) and online (Wayfair, Purple). **Not taken:** Sleep Number and Kirkland's (not found under those tickers in the SEC ticker file on 2026-09-27; not pursued), and the private competitors the filer's categories cover (it names none). The class below **does not rest on the row**: it rests on the filer's own statements of how its prices and margins moved and on ten years of its own returns, which the row supplements; it is not held PROVISIONAL (the IPAR, MEDP and JJSF precedent).
- **What the row shows.** **Lovesac's gross margin is in the top third and its operating margin in the bottom third.** The wide gross margin is not distinctive among sellers who own their showrooms (Ethan Allen 61.2%, Haverty's 60.7%, Bassett 56.3%), and it is spent: Lovesac's cumulative operating margin, 2.8%, sits with Hooker and Bassett, below every profitable showroom retailer in the row, and its latest return on non-cash assets (1.2%) is the fourth-lowest of twelve. Williams-Sonoma held 16-19% margins and 30-35% on non-cash assets through the same slump in which Lovesac went from 8.1% to 0.8%. **Its limit [E3-61]:** the row shows position, not conduct.

**The remaining Q2 tests.**
- **[E4-32], direction: narrowing.** Operating margin 8.1% (fiscal 2022) to 0.8% (fiscal 2026) and an operating loss in the first half of fiscal 2027 even after $20.0M of tariff refunds (operating loss $6.4M; about $26M without the refund); promotional discounting higher in each of four years; new customers down 5.6% in fiscal 2026 and 2.6% in the first half of fiscal 2027. **[E4-55], units:** the filer publishes no unit series in its recent 10-Ks; the nearest physical series is customers, and new customers are falling while the showroom count rises (257 to 278 to 284).
- **[E3-33] untapped pricing power / [E5-28]: not claimed**, and the record refutes it: a near monopoly does not raise promotion four years running; the filer says competitors *"may be able to offer lower prices than we are able to offer."*
- **[E2-53], dominance: not shown.** Lovesac's profits track its execution and the promotional calendar, not a position that prospers *"Good or bad"*.
- **[E2-45], the attacker's test.** The patents stop a copy of the coupling geometry until 2035-2043; they do not stop an attacker who sells a comfortable sectional, which is the substitute the filer itself names. The installed base is the one thing an attacker cannot take quickly, and it is weighed in the case for IN below.
- **[E3-74], the class.** A showroom retailer is Buffett's named have-to-stay-smart case (*"retailing is a good case of a business where you have to stay smart ... you cannot coast in retailing"*); Lovesac's 278 leased mall showrooms and 12.7% of sales in advertising put it in that class.
- **[E4-04], in the ruling's form.** Not the ground of this verdict: the product has been maintained since 1995 and nothing must be rebuilt from zero.
- **[E4-23], key-person dependence: a prompt, recorded here as the rule requires.** Item 1A: *"We believe our success has depended, and continues to depend, on the efforts and talents of Shawn Nelson, our founder ..."*, with the President and the (since departed) CFO. The product and brand are the founder's; the filing does not show the franchise outlasting him, and the record is too short to test it. Not the ground of the verdict.
- **[E4-36] and [E3-51], which cause of success.** The one extreme result in the record, fiscal 2021-2022, is wave-riding: the pandemic surge in home furnishing, which lifted the whole row. A surfing run is not a moat.
- **[E3-62], who keeps the gains.** The tariff refunds of fiscal 2027 are an example on the record: *"In the event the Company receives a refund of tariffs previously paid, it may elect to return a portion of such amounts to vendors that provided the Company tariff relief and/or price concessions"* (10-K fiscal 2026, Note 11). The gains are shared along the chain, not kept by the brand.

### THE STRONGEST EVIDENCE AGAINST THIS VERDICT, stated as its holders would state it [E4-51, E4-26, E3-47]
The verdict below is OUT, so the case for IN is set out first, at full strength:
1. **No one else may sell this product.** 55 US utility patents to 2035-2043 protect the modular geometry and coupling; a Sactional owner who wants more seats, a new cover or the StealthTech sides must buy them from Lovesac, and the compatibility is guaranteed. Repeat customers rose to 50.4% of transactions in fiscal 2026; their number grew 15.6% and 8.8% in fiscal 2025 and 2026 while new customers rose 1.4% and then fell 5.6%.
2. **The gross margin is a pricing fact.** 56-58% in fiscal 2024-2026, through a housing slump and two tariff shocks, is above RH, Williams-Sonoma, La-Z-Boy and Arhaus; a commodity couch does not carry it.
3. **The operating margin is investment, not weakness.** Lovesac opened 28 and 39 showrooms in fiscal 2026 and 2025, built a new head office, is onshoring Sactional production, and carried legal and professional fees in fiscal 2025 that fell by $12.9M in fiscal 2026 as the restatement's aftermath passed; a young brand in its growth phase should not be judged on its trough margin.
4. **The cycle is aberrational [E3-30].** Every furniture seller's margins fell after 2022 on housing turnover and rates; the first half of fiscal 2027 shows price increases going through (product margin +280 basis points).
5. **The returns on capital are high when the business is at scale**: 85% pre-tax on operating capital in fiscal 2022 and 34% in fiscal 2023, on a capital-light model with outsourced manufacturing.

**Answered.** (1) is accepted as the best asset in the file and weighed where it shows up: the installed base has not lifted total sales, which have been flat at $651-700M for four years, and it has not stopped the discounting; the parts are compatible but the buyer of the first couch, who must be found every year with 12.7% of sales in advertising and a promotional calendar, is choosing among sectionals the filer calls comparable. (2) is weighed against the row and the filer's own warning that *"Certain competitors and other retailers may report gross profit differently than we do"*: showroom retailers that make or buy their own goods (Ethan Allen 61.2%, Haverty's 60.7%, Bassett 56.3%) book the same gross margin, and it moved at Lovesac with discounts (fiscal 2021-2025) and with costs it did not pass on (fiscal 2020). (3) is the argument [E4-18] warns against: the demonstration [E3-43] asks for is a record of pricing and returns, and a verdict that holds only if the trough is set aside and the growth years are counted as investment has narrowed its assumptions to reach the answer; ten years of cumulative operating margin at 2.6% include the growth years and the peak. (4) is tested against the row: in the same slump Williams-Sonoma held 18% margins and a third on its non-cash assets; the cycle hit everyone, and the franchise-like seller in the row stood it while Lovesac went to 0.8%. The price rises of fiscal 2027 are real and partly offset by deeper promotion, with new customers falling; one half-year is not [E2-44]'s test. (5) is [E4-41]: the high returns sit in the pandemic years that lifted the whole row, and the operating capital denominator is small because suppliers and landlords finance it.

**The analyst's own incentive, stated [operator rule 9, E4-27, E3-41].** OUT ends the run early, a pull toward OUT; the JJSF, IPAR and MEDP files closed on [E3-43]'s "a business", a pull toward OUT by analogy; a patented product with a devoted installed base is a pull toward IN by story. The verdict rests on the filer's own sentences about its competition, its promotions and its costs in every MD&A from fiscal 2019 to the latest 10-Q, ten years of its own returns and cash, and a row of eleven filers; the case for IN was written first and at full strength.
