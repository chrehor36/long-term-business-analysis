## Q2 — WHY IS THE CASTLE STILL STANDING, and what keeps it standing ten to twenty years? STOP.
The question: "why is that castle still standing? And what’s going to keep it standing or cause it not to be standing five, 10, 20 years from now. What are the key factors? And how permanent are they?" **[M1995-038]**. The moat is what "protects excellent returns on invested capital" **[L2007-004]**.

**The rivals (the brief's rule).** The 10-K's competition text names no rival; it says only that "Some of our competitors have greater financial and other resources" and that "numerous smaller companies" compete regionally "and adopt more aggressive pricing policies" (Item 1). The only compression businesses the 10-K names anywhere are three members of its pay peer group (Item 11): **Archrock, Inc.** (10-K filer; FY2025 10-K accession 0001389050-26-000009, FY2021 0001389050-22-000007, FY2018 0001389050-19-000021, and its XBRL facts), **Kodiak Gas Services, Inc.** (10-K filer from FY2023; FY2025 10-K accession 0001767042-26-000012) and **Enerflex Ltd.** (files a 40-F with the SEC under IFRS, a Canadian company with international operations; **flagged and not used**, because its U.S. contract compression is not reported on a basis comparable to the other three in the filings read). The "numerous smaller companies" are unnamed and file nothing.

**The tests, each with its filing fact.**
1. **What keeps it standing, and how permanent** **[M1995-038]**. The filer's case (Item 1): units sized to the customer's site, a "modern and standardized fleet", run-time guarantees of 95% to 98%, and the cost and down-time a customer would bear to swap a working unit. The record supports some stickiness: "we typically continue to provide compression services at a specific location beyond the initial contract term", and in 2025 the month-to-month 19% of compression revenue carried rates that "did not differ significantly" from contracted rates (Item 1A, Item 7). That is the incumbent's convenience. It did not hold the price through the last downturn (test 4).
2. **Would it stand without the lord** **[M1996-037]**? Yes. Nothing in the filings ties the economics to one person; the executive team turned over in 2025 (severance "related to the departure of certain senior management", Item 7) with no change in rates or utilization. This test passes and decides nothing alone.
3. **The money test: could a well-funded attacker take it** **[M2011-015]**? The filer buys its units "primarily from third-party fabricators who build the units to our specifications", from engines and frames made by Caterpillar and Ariel (Item 1); every rival buys the same components, and the customer can too: "The historical availability of attractive financing terms from financial institutions and equipment manufacturers has made the purchase of individual compression units more affordable to our customers" (Item 1), and customers "may choose to vertically integrate their operations by purchasing and operating their own compression fleets" (Item 1A). A competitor's own words: "The natural gas compression services business is highly competitive with low barriers to entry" (Archrock FY2025 10-K, Item 1). The rows name this kind of field: "there are some industries that are just never going to have barriers to entry. And in those industries, you better be running very fast" **[M2012-106]**. The attacker's only obstacle in 2023 to 2025 was the queue at the suppliers ("lead-times for such engines and frames have extended beyond one year, and in some cases with certain engine classes, are in excess of two years", Item 1), which is a shortage, not a castle; a shortage ends when the suppliers catch up.
4. **Pricing power, and the agony before a rise** **[M2005-020]**. USAC's average revenue per revenue-generating horsepower per month, from each year's 10-K: $13.39 (2012), $14.15 (2013), $15.57 (2014), $15.90 (2015), $15.41 (2016), $15.07 (2017) for USAC before the combination; then $16.09 (2018), $16.65 (2019), $16.71 (2020), $16.60 (2021), $17.35 (2022), $18.86 (2023), $20.43 (2024), $21.38 (2025) and $22.84 (second quarter of 2026, including the higher-rate J-W fleet). The rate fell in 2016 and 2017 and was $16.60 in 2021 against $15.57 in 2014: seven years with almost no nominal rise, while its own costs rose. The filer says why: "During periods of low crude oil prices, we typically experience pressure on service rates and utilization from our customers in gas lift applications" (Item 1A), and the rises it reports come "as market conditions permit" (Item 7). The rows' measure is the price behaviour over time **[M2005-020]**; this price follows the industry's spare capacity in both directions.
5. **Unit volume** (the horsepower on rent). Average utilization, from each 10-K: 94.0% (2014), 90.5% (2015), 87.4% (2016), 92.0% (2017) for USAC; 91.9% (2018), 94.1% (2019), 86.8% (2020), 82.7% (2021), 88.6% (2022), 93.4% (2023), 94.6% (2024), 94.3% (2025). Revenue-generating horsepower at year-end: 3,310,024 (2019), 2,997,262 (2020), 2,964,206 (2021), 3,585,452 (2025). Volume fell 10% from 2019 to 2021 and recovered; the volume is the customers' production cycle, not a preference for this fleet.
6. **The low-cost position** **[L2004-007]**, **[M1997-010]**. On the same year and the filers' own definitions, adjusted gross margin over revenue in 2025: USAC 67.1% (its whole revenue); Kodiak's contract services 68.4% (65.7% in 2024); Archrock's contract operations 73% (67% in 2024), of which 2.8 points come from "a net benefit of $35.0 million as a result of certain sales and use tax audit settlements and credits", so about 70% without it. Revenue per horsepower in 2025: USAC $21.38; Archrock about $23.59 (contract operations revenue $1,272.1M over 4,494 thousand average operating horsepower, per month); Kodiak about $22.88 (contract services revenue $1,181.3M over the average of its year-end revenue-generating horsepower, 4,302.6 thousand, per month; an approximation, its average is not filed). USAC has the lowest rate and the lowest margin of the three; the fleets differ in mix (USAC's average revenue-generating unit was 847 HP in 2025, Kodiak's 970 HP), and the filings read do not separate how much of the gap mix explains. No filing shows USAC, or any of the three, as the low-cost producer that sets the others' price.
7. **The brand in the customer's mind** **[M2017-009]**. Not claimed by any filer; the filer competes "on the basis of price, equipment availability, customer service, flexibility in meeting customer needs, quality and reliability" (Item 1), the same list Archrock and Kodiak print.
8. **Would the customer still choose it over the low bid** **[M2017-009]**? The filer lists price first among the bases of competition and names rivals who "adopt more aggressive pricing policies" (Item 1); its customers "could seek to preserve capital or reduce expenses by using lower-cost providers of compression services" (Item 1A). The customer is a producer or a gatherer buying a service by specification; the rows' commodity mark is the customer who does not "care from whom they buy" **[L2004-003]**, and the filings describe that customer.
9. **Ask the competitors** **[M1999-130]**. Their filings are the only answer on the public record: Archrock, "low barriers to entry"; Kodiak, "competition from numerous companies that may be able to adapt to changes more quickly" and "adopt more aggressive pricing policies" (FY2025 10-K, Item 1).
10. **Widening or narrowing** **[M1999-108]**. Fleet horsepower at year-end 2025: Archrock 4,788 thousand available, Kodiak 4,456 thousand, USAC 3,894 thousand (4,952 thousand after J-W, June 2026). From 2019 to 2025 USAC's fleet grew 5.7% (3,682,968 to 3,894,332 HP) on $1,263M of capital spending, cash capex plus inventory moved into the fleet (`returns_output.txt`); the two rivals grew mainly by buying other fleets (Archrock's TOPS and NGCS purchases, Kodiak's CSI Compressco purchase, their FY2025 10-Ks), as USAC now has with J-W. Consolidation among the three is the change of the last two years; no filing claims it has changed how prices are set, and the shortage of engines and frames is the reason the filer gives for the recent tightness.
11. **What could destroy, modify or reduce it** **[M2000-014]**. Customers owning their compression; electric-drive compression, which the filer offers as "dual-drive technology" and Archrock says could "increase demand for electric compressors or require us to make modifications to our existing natural gas-powered units" (FY2025 10-K); lower production in the basins served; and new units from the suppliers' catching up, which would reopen the price.

**The parts table.** Not needed: "Our assets and operations are organized into a single reportable segment" (10-K FY2025, Item 1; Note 18). The J-W fabrication shops are small beside the rental fleet (J-W's "Field equipment sales" were $83.9M of $362.4M revenue in its year to 2025-10-31, 8-K/A accession 0001522727-26-000031).

Pre-tax, before interest, on tangible assets carried at depreciated cost, the two filers with a long record earned 3% to 7% a year from 2014 to 2022, through the 2015 to 2016 and 2020 to 2021 downturns, and 9% to 13% only in 2023 to 2025, at utilization of 93% to 96% and after the rate rises above. USAC's twelve-year mean is 6.4%, Archrock's ten-year mean 6.8%. The base flatters the peak: USAC's net property of $2,162.6M is about half its gross cost of $4,363.5M (Note 5), so the 2025 figure on the cost of the fleet actually standing (operating income before impairments plus intangible amortization, $343.7M, over gross property, $4,363.5M) is about 7.9%. Judged in words, as the rule requires **[M1999-108]**: over a full cycle the durable players of this field earn ordinary returns, the kind of which the rows say "if you have a business that’s earning 5 or 6 percent on equity and you hold it for a long time, you are not going to do well in investing" **[M1998-006]**, and the field fits "you can have only two competitors and they’re still terrible businesses" **[M2013-052]** in its milder form: three large rivals and many small ones, none with a price it can hold in a downturn.

**A change for the better?** The recent years are the one argument for the castle. The rows record that change for the better happens, "the railroads were a terrible business for decades and decades and decades and then they got good." **[M2017-026]**, and the framework credits it "only on the evidence of a full cycle in which the price behaviour held" **[M2005-020]**, **[L1995-022]**. Here no filing claims a change in how the field sets prices; the filer ties the rises to "market-based rates" and to supplier lead-times; and the record holds one earlier rise of the same kind, $13.39 to $15.57 (2012 to 2014, utilization near 94%), followed by falls in 2016 and 2017 and seven flat years. What the evidence shows is a castle that stood through the last cycle and protected ordinary returns through it, not a changed castle awaiting its test; the OUT rule for a standing castle with ordinary returns governs, not the TOO HARD rule for an uncredited change. The reading the other way (TOO HARD (NATURE), re-look at the first year the field's volume falls) is recorded under "What in the framework was wrong or unclear".

**Why the box is OUT and not TOO HARD.** The returns are known from the filings, so the reason the tenuous-moat row gives for TOO HARD, "We don’t know how to valuate that" **[M2000-019]**, does not apply; the framework's rule is that "a field whose durable players earn ordinary returns over a full cycle, on the evidence of the filer's and the rivals' own filings, is OUT, not TOO HARD" (Q2, What it rules OUT). The castle is a capital-heavy rental of a machine anyone can buy, priced by spare capacity, and the moat that would protect "excellent returns" **[L2007-004]** is not shown: the money test fails on the filer's and a rival's own words **[M2012-106]**, the price fell when capacity was loose **[M2005-020]**, and no one of the three is the low-cost producer **[M1997-010]**.

- **VERDICT: OUT** at Q2: a standing castle that protects only ordinary returns over a full cycle, on the filer's and the rivals' filings **[L2007-004]**, **[M1998-006]**, **[M2012-106]**, **[M2005-020]**.
