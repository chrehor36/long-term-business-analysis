### (c)(i) THE PRICE EVENT, AND WHERE IT SITS IN THE MARGIN

**Gross margin, filed, by year — the single most important table in this leg:**

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 11.30% | 10.70% | 12.78% | 15.58% | 12.83% | 13.56% | 15.48% | **16.50%** | **16.17%** |

**THE ANSWER, PLAINLY: YES — the current margin level rests in material part on price taken during
the 2021–23 raw-material squeeze and retained after the input cost fell away, and the amount is
quantifiable. But the same window contains a housing cycle at a trough, which pushes the other
way, and a good-faith reading has to carry both.**

1. **2021–22 were the margin TROUGH, not the peak.** Gross margin fell to 42.1% in 2022 as
   petrochemical-derived resins, latex, solvents and titanium dioxide spiked. Sherwin-Williams
   raised architectural prices through 2021–2023 to recover the cost.
2. **The cost came back down and the price did not.** Gross margin is now **48.8%, the highest in
   the nine-year series** and 6.7 points above 2022. FY2025 MD&A, verbatim: *"Consolidated Gross
   profit increased $320.4 million, or 2.9%… primarily due to **favorable selling prices in the
   Paint Stores Group**… and **moderating raw material costs**."*
3. **Quantified. Each point of gross margin on 2025 revenue is $235.7M pre-tax.** Reversion to the
   2019–2020 mean of 46.1% — a fair pre-squeeze base, avoiding both the Valspar-distorted 2017–18
   and the squeezed 2021–22 — costs **2.7 points, $636.5M pre-tax, $489.5M after tax at the 23.1%
   effective rate**:

| gross margin | pre-tax hit | after tax | **bottom-boundary owner earnings** | **yield at $82,248.9M** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **48.8% — 2025 actual** | — | — | **$1,988.8M** | **2.42%** |
| 47.5% — half the give-back | $306.4M | $235.6M | $1,753.2M | 2.13% |
| **46.1% — the 2019–20 mean** | **$636.5M** | **$489.5M** | **$1,499.3M** | **1.82%** |
| 44.9% — 2019 alone | $919.2M | $706.9M | $1,281.9M | 1.56% |

4. **THE OTHER HALF OF THE WINDOW, AND IT IS GENUINELY DIFFERENT FROM THE CARLISLE CASE.** The
   housing cycle sits in the same window at its **trough**, not its peak, and Sherwin-Williams's own
   filings say so: 2025 Paint Stores Group volumes fell, new residential fell again in H1 2026
   *"as expected"*, and the Consumer Brands Group's *"core North America DIY demand remained soft."*
   **The 2025 margin was earned on depressed volume.** A normalisation that strips the price without
   also normalising the volume would be a one-sided adjustment, and this run does not make one.
5. **Third artifact, small and named: the pandemic year.** 2020 net operating cash of $3,408.6M was
   a do-it-yourself surge — net income rose 31.7% on 2.6% revenue growth. It sits in the seven-year
   window and is the reason the 2020-base decomposition reads so differently from the 2019 one.
6. **Fourth artifact, and it is a cash artifact rather than an earnings one: the One Big Beautiful
   Bill Act.** Verbatim: *"On July 4, 2025, U.S. tax reform legislation known as the One Big
   Beautiful Bill Act… Key provisions… include **immediate expensing of certain domestic capital
   expenditures and domestic research and development costs**."* **Income taxes PAID fell from
   $779.8M (2024) to $592.7M (2025) while pre-tax income fell only 3.3%; cash taxes as a share of
   pre-tax income went 26.3% → 22.6% → 17.8% against a book rate that did not move.** MD&A names the
   cause in its own cash-flow discussion: *"Net operating cash increased $298.4 million in 2025… primarily
   due to **an increase in Deferred income taxes** and lower cash requirements for working capital,
   partially offset by lower Net income."* **Roughly $150–160M of 2025 net operating cash is a tax
   deferral, not earnings. It is flagged here and carried into the Q4 range rather than adjusted out,
   because the deferral is real cash for as long as the capital programme continues.**
7. **The counter-case, stated fairly [E4-51], before the conclusion.** Not all of the margin gain is
   recovered price. Sherwin-Williams took **$111.0M of restructuring provisions in 2025** and named
   the savings; it closed 40 Latin American stores and 13 industrial branches; the Paint Stores Group
   segment margin has been **22.0%–22.5% in each of the last three years**, which is stability, not a
   spike; and **the operating margin was already 15.58% in 2020, before the price event.** Against
   2020 rather than 2019, the elevation is 0.6 points, not 3.4. **A full reversion to the 2019 level
   is not the base case.**

## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? [E3-31]

- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language.** Sherwin-Williams buys petrochemical
  resins, latex, solvents, titanium dioxide and additives, mixes them into paint in its own
  factories, and then — and this is the whole business — **sells that paint out of 4,853 shops it
  owns itself, to the man who paints houses for a living.** He drives to the store before seven in
  the morning, the counter staff know his name and his colours, the store carries his account so he
  does not pay until he is paid, it mixes to a chip he brings in, and if he needs forty more gallons
  at eleven o'clock the store puts them on a truck to the jobsite. He does not shop on price because
  a wasted hour costs him more than a gallon does. That segment — **Paint Stores Group, $13,605.9M
  of revenue, 57.7% of the total — earns a 22.5% pre-tax margin and 67.8% of all segment profit.**
  A second segment (Consumer Brands) makes the paint, sells about **63% of its output internally to
  the stores at cost**, and puts the rest through home centres and hardware chains under labels like
  Valspar and Krylon. A third (Performance Coatings, $6,795.2M) sells industrial and automotive
  refinish coatings worldwide through 317 branches and direct sales, at a 13.9% margin, and carries
  **$258.3M a year of Valspar amortisation.** Consolidated: 48.8 cents of gross profit on the sales
  dollar, 32.6 cents of selling and administration, **16.2 cents of operating income**, 2.0 cents of
  interest and 3.3 of tax, leaving **10.9 cents of net income**. It does this with **64,249 people**
  — **$366,900 of revenue per employee**, less than half Carlisle's, because the payroll is a retail
  payroll — **$4,137.4M of net plant, $2,318.2M of inventory and $2,791.2M of receivables**, and
  **$1,995.2M of right-of-use assets** because the shops are leased. It turns **$3.45 billion of
  operating cash**, spends roughly $450M keeping the operating business running, and pushes
  **$789.8M of dividends and $1,656.4M of buybacks** out of the door. There is no float; there is
  **$813.0M of sale-leaseback financing on the head office**.
- **The scarce input this business controls: store density within a few minutes' drive of a
  professional painting contractor, plus the account, the colour library and the delivery truck
  behind it.** Not the paint, which any competent chemist can make. Not the brand, though the brand
  helps. **What is scarce is 4,853 locations, built over 150 years, each one economic only because
  the contractors around it already buy there — a position that has to be assembled in the right
  order and cannot be bought.** Sherwin-Williams's own Item 1 is unusually plain about the channel:
  *"In the Paint Stores Group, competitors include other paint and wallpaper stores, **mass
  merchandisers, home centers, independent hardware stores, hardware chains** and
  manufacturer-operated direct outlets."* Every named competitor sells through someone else's floor.
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** Yes. Houses will need repainting on
  roughly a seven-to-twelve-year cycle whether or not they change hands; professional painters will
  keep buying where it is fastest; and paint is not a technology with a successor in view. The named
  change is **who does the painting** — the long shift from do-it-yourself to do-it-for-me, which
  runs in Sherwin-Williams's favour and against the home centres — and **how many houses change
  hands**, which is the cycle and is carried to Q4 as a named death.
- **[E4-46] check:** this decision does not need five months. Three segments, one plainly written
  10-K, twenty-three notes in ordinary English, and a product you can buy in a can. **It is inside
  the circle.**
- **VERDICT: [x] IN**

Class **WIDE at the Paint Stores Group (57.7% of revenue, 67.8% of segment profit); NARROW at

**[E3-03], the three criteria:**
- **Needed or desired [x] — YES.** Paint is a small fraction of the cost of a paint job (labour is
  most of it) and a large fraction of how the job looks. Residential repaint, commercial and
  protective and marine all grew in H1 2026.
- **No close substitute [x] — YES, at the level that matters, and this is the strongest single
  criterion in the file.** There is a close substitute for a gallon of paint; **there is no close
  substitute for a store four minutes from the jobsite that carries the contractor's account.**
  The three biggest possible attackers have each tried the other route and none is gaining
  (worked under the attacker's test below).
- **Not price-regulated [x].**

**Must the moat be continuously rebuilt? [E4-04], scoped as the corpus scopes it.** The test is
whether a lapse in spending destroys the structure or merely narrows it, and whether the spending
defends the same advantage or buys its replacement. **Sherwin-Williams's answer is the cleanest of
any name in this queue.** Stopping the 80-to-100 new stores a year would slow growth and change
nothing about the 4,853 that exist; the stores are decades old and the brand is 160; **the spending
defends the same advantage rather than buying a replacement.** Contrast Carlisle, where acquisitions
of +3.2% of revenue exactly offset organic decline of −2.9%. **Sherwin-Williams's organic growth is
positive without acquisitions: +2.1% consolidated in 2025 with acquisitions contributing 1.1%, and
+7.2% in H1 2026.**

**The second half of [E4-04] — does success depend on a great manager? NO, and this is the [E4-23]
Mayo Clinic case rather than the brain-surgeon case.** The moat is 4,853 leases, a colour library,
a delivery fleet and 64,249 employees' worth of accumulated contractor relationships. **A holder
does not need to know who runs Sherwin-Williams**, which is exactly the distinction [E4-23] draws.
**Recorded at Q2 as a strength, and the CEO transition is therefore read at Q3 as evidence rather
than here as a defect.**
