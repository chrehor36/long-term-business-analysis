## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**
- Unit economics in my own words, no management language: Grainger buys maintenance and repair goods (safety gear, motors, pumps, plumbing, material handling, cleaning supplies, hand tools, fasteners) from more than 5,000 makers, keeps about 1.5 million of them in stock in distribution centres and branches, and resells them to 4.6 million customers who need the part quickly and would rather not run a stockroom; no single customer is over 10% of sales and no supplier over 5% of purchases. It does this in two ways: the North American business (78% of FY2025 sales) sells to mid-size and large customers through sales staff, electronic purchasing links, on-site stockrooms and next-day delivery; the online businesses (20%: Zoro in the U.S., MonotaRO in Japan) sell a far larger list, about 13 million and 29 million items, to smaller buyers at listed prices. On each $100 of FY2025 sales it paid **$60.90** for the goods, spent **$25.20** on people, buildings, trucks, systems and marketing ($24.10 without the $196M loss on leaving the U.K.), and kept **$13.90** before interest and tax (sales $17,942M, gross profit $7,009M, operating earnings $2,495M). The two businesses earn differently: North America kept **$16.80** per $100 (41.7% gross margin, $2,354M on $13,993M), the online businesses **$9.50** (29.9%, $345M on $3,625M). The capital is stock and receivables less what suppliers finance, **$3,760M, about 21 cents per dollar of sales** (receivables $2,329M + inventories $2,394M − payables $963M), plus a distribution network carried at **$2,268M** net and growing (capital spending $684M against depreciation and amortization of $254M in FY2025). **Profit is (a markup on other makers' goods, and on its own private label, 19% of U.S. stocked-product sales, x the repair and replacement volume of North American and Japanese businesses) less a network cost that is mostly fixed in the short run.**
- The scarce input this business controls: **the scale of the network**: distribution centres that ship most orders complete next day, branches for same-day pick-up, a stocked range of about 2 million products in North America, on-site stockrooms inside customers' plants (KeepStock), and electronic purchasing links wired into large customers' procurement systems. The filer's own claim: *"In the large and fragmented MRO industry, Grainger holds an advantaged position with its supply chain infrastructure and a broad in-stock product offering ."* Whether any of this is scarce against the other national distributors is Q2's question, not Q1's.
- Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years? **Yes.** Buildings, factories and institutions will still need repair parts delivered quickly; the company was *"incorporated in the State of Illinois in 1928"* and the FY1993 and FY2025 10-Ks describe the same trade against the same rivals (*"manufacturers (including some of the Company's own suppliers) that sell directly to certain segments of the market, from wholesale distributors, and from certain retail enterprises"*, FY1993; the same list with *"internet-based businesses"* added, FY2025). The channel has already moved online inside Grainger (eCommerce was 51% of sales in FY2017, 10-K FY2017), and the filer names the change it expects: *"Grainger expects technological advancements, innovations and the increased use of eCommerce solutions within the industry to continue to evolve at a rapid pace."* That is a question about the moat's durability (Q2 and Q4), not about how the money is made.
- **Simple and stable in character [E3-31]; a decision that can be made on the filings [E4-46].** Nothing in the business requires a forecast of technology to understand how it makes money.
