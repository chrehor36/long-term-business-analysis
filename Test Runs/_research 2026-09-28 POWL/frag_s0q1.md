## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**
- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language:** Powell bids, one project at a time, to design and build the electrical rooms that take power from the grid or a generator and distribute it to motors and machines inside a large plant: medium-voltage switchgear and circuit breakers (some of its own design), motor control centres, bus duct, and whole prefabricated steel buildings holding them (*"integrated power control room substations (PCRs ® ), custom-engineered modules, electrical houses (E-Houses)"*), built in Houston, Canada and the UK and shipped to the site. The customer is the plant owner or its engineering contractor: *"Each project is specifically engineered and manufactured to meet the exact specifications and requirements of the individual customer"*, *"typically awarded in competitive bid situations"*. It is paid a fixed price agreed at the bid, in milestone payments that often arrive before the work (hence the contract liabilities), and earns the difference between that price and what the steel, copper, aluminium and bought components (*"Material costs represented 45% of revenues in Fiscal 2025, 47% of revenues in Fiscal 2024, and 49% of revenues in Fiscal 2023"*) and its roughly 3,100 engineers and fabricators cost by the time the room ships. It bears the overrun: *"we bear the risk of cost overruns and delays in most of our contracts"*. The customers' demand is their own capital spending, **cyclical by the filer's first risk factor**: *"The end markets that we serve have historically been cyclical and will continue to be vulnerable to general downturns"*. FY2025: revenue **$1,104.3M**; gross profit **$324.4M (29.4%)**; operating income **$217.9M (19.7%)**; net income $180.7M. The markets: oil and gas 37%, electric utility 25%, commercial and other industrial (data centres inside it) 16%, petrochemical 14%, light rail 4%, other 4%. No customer over 10% in FY2025 or FY2024.
- **The scarce input this business controls:** **engineering and fabrication capacity with a record of delivering large, safety-critical electrical rooms on schedule**, plus the relationships with the engineering contractors who write the specifications. The filer's own list: *"our products and services, integration capabilities, technical and project management acumen, application engineering expertise and specialty contracting experience, together with our financial strength and responsiveness"*. No patents that matter: *"we believe that the growth of our business will depend primarily upon the quality of our products and our relationships with our customers, rather than the extent of our patent protection"*. Whether the capacity and the record let it set the price is Q2's question.
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** **The product, yes; the level of demand, no one can say from the filings.** Switchgear, breakers and E-houses of 480 volts to 38 kilovolts are a mature technology; the FY2011 and FY2025 10-Ks describe the same products to the same kinds of customer (oil, gas, petrochemical, utilities, industrial). What changes is how many plants are being built: revenue fell from $717.2M (FY2012, as then reported) to $395.9M (FY2017) and rose to $1,104.3M (FY2025). That is the cycle the filer names, not a change of business.
- **Why this is inside the circle:** how the business earns is plain from one page of the 10-K (a fixed-price bid against a known bill of materials and labour, paid in milestones), and [E4-46]'s five-minute test is met. The hard questions are whether anything protects the price at the bid (Q2) and whether the last three years' margins are a cycle or a new level (Q2 and Q4), not how it earns.
