## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND IT? Where its economics will be in ten years, inside my perimeter. STOP.
- **The test.** Understanding is "a reasonable fix on about what the earning power and competitive position will look like in five or 10 years" and "where the company will stand within the industry" **[M2012-065]**; the product may stay technical if "I understand the economic dynamics of the industry. Is there — are there competitive moats? Is there ease of entry?" **[M2011-014]**; and the past statements must tell "what the future financial statements are going to look like" **[M2008-033]**.
- **The business, from Item 1.** USAC rents standardized natural-gas compression units, with its own technicians operating and maintaining them, for fixed monthly fees; it does not take title to the gas, and the fuel is the customer's. Revenue is horsepower on rent, times the monthly rate, times utilization; costs are labour, parts, lubricants and the fleet's depreciation; growth is new units bought from fabricators at a filed cost (the FY2025 10-K: "binding commitments to purchase $78.4 million of additional compression units" against 63,250 horsepower on order, about $1,240 a horsepower).
- **The key variables and whether they are foreseeable** **[M1998-044]**: (1) the volume of U.S. gas moved through gathering and processing in the basins served, which drives horsepower on rent; (2) the rate per horsepower, which the record shows moving with the industry's spare capacity (flat from 2014 to 2021, up 29% from 2021 to 2025); (3) the cost of a new unit and its life (25 years on the filer's depreciation policy, Note 5); (4) the debt and its cost. The first is a volume tied to a commodity's production; the rows put the commodity price outside any forecast, "We don’t think we can predict commodity prices." **[M2016-025]**, and judge such a business off its figures, "if you can’t make a decision on something like PetroChina off the figures, forget about going further" **[M2008-069]**. The figures here are readable: twelve years of filed horsepower, rate and utilization, and a filed unit cost.
- **Is it important and knowable** **[M2006-076]**? The economics of renting a standardized machine are knowable from the filer's and the rivals' filings; what is not knowable is the year-by-year volume, which the framework sends to Q7 as the cycle, not to Q1 (Q2, A commodity business). The technology moves slowly: the units are engines and reciprocating frames, and the filer's change is a "dual-drive" electric option (Item 1). The insiders do write the picture down: the filer, Archrock and Kodiak each describe the same business in nearly the same words, so this is not a field whose insiders would say "That’s too hard." **[M2000-105]**.
- **Doubt** **[M2002-092]**: the doubt this run has is about the castle and the price, not about what the business is or how it earns; that belongs to Q2 and Q7.
- **VERDICT: IN.** The economics (rented horsepower x rate x utilization, a filed unit cost, a stated asset life) can be followed from the filings ten years out in kind, though not in amount **[M2012-065]**, **[M2011-014]**, **[M2008-069]**.

## Q2 — WHY IS THE CASTLE STILL STANDING, and what keeps it standing ten to twenty years? STOP.
The question: "why is that castle still standing? And what’s going to keep it standing or cause it not to be standing five, 10, 20 years from now. What are the key factors? And how permanent are they?" **[M1995-038]**. The moat is what "protects excellent returns on invested capital" **[L2007-004]**.

**The rivals (the brief's rule).** The 10-K's competition text names no rival; it says only that "Some of our competitors have greater financial and other resources" and that "numerous smaller companies" compete regionally "and adopt more aggressive pricing policies" (Item 1). The only compression businesses the 10-K names anywhere are three members of its pay peer group (Item 11): **Archrock, Inc.** (10-K filer; FY2025 10-K accession 0001389050-26-000009, FY2021 0001389050-22-000007, FY2018 0001389050-19-000021, and its XBRL facts), **Kodiak Gas Services, Inc.** (10-K filer from FY2023; FY2025 10-K accession 0001767042-26-000012) and **Enerflex Ltd.** (files a 40-F with the SEC under IFRS, a Canadian company with international operations; **flagged and not used**, because its U.S. contract compression is not reported on a basis comparable to the other three in the filings read). The "numerous smaller companies" are unnamed and file nothing.

**The tests, each with its filing fact.**
1. **What keeps it standing, and how permanent** **[M1995-038]**. The filer's case (Item 1): units sized to the customer's site, a "modern and standardized fleet", run-time guarantees of 95% to 98%, and the cost and down-time a customer would bear to swap a working unit. The record supports some stickiness: "we typically continue to provide compression services at a specific location beyond the initial contract term", and in 2025 the month-to-month 19% of compression revenue carried rates that "did not differ significantly" from contracted rates (Item 1A, Item 7). That is the incumbent's convenience. It did not hold the price through the last downturn (test 4).
2. **Would it stand without the lord** **[M1996-037]**? Yes. Nothing in the filings ties the economics to one person; the executive team turned over in 2025 (severance "related to the departure of certain senior management", Item 7) with no change in rates or utilization. This test passes and decides nothing alone.
3. **The money test: could a well-funded attacker take it** **[M2011-015]**? The filer buys its units "primarily from third-party fabricators who build the units to our specifications", from engines and frames made by Caterpillar and Ariel (Item 1); every rival buys the same components, and the customer can too: "The historical availability of attractive financing terms from financial institutions and equipment manufacturers has made the purchase of individual compression units more affordable to our customers" (Item 1), and customers "may choose to vertically integrate their operations by purchasing and operating their own compression fleets" (Item 1A). A competitor's own words: "The natural gas compression services business is highly competitive with low barriers to entry" (Archrock FY2025 10-K, Item 1). The rows name this kind of field: "there are some industries that are just never going to have barriers to entry. And in those industries, you better be running very fast" **[M2012-106]**. The attacker's only obstacle in 2023 to 2025 was the queue at the suppliers ("lead-times for such engines and frames have extended beyond one year, and in some cases with certain engine classes, are in excess of two years", Item 1), which is a shortage, not a castle; a shortage ends when the suppliers catch up.
4. **Pricing power, and the agony before a rise** **[M2005-020]**. USAC's average revenue per revenue-generating horsepower per month, from each year's 10-K: $13.39 (2012), $14.15 (2013), $15.57 (2014), $15.90 (2015), $15.41 (2016), $15.07 (2017) for USAC before the combination; then $16.09 (2018), $16.65 (2019), $16.71 (2020), $16.60 (2021), $17.35 (2022), $18.86 (2023), $20.43 (2024), $21.38 (2025) and $22.84 (second quarter of 2026, including the higher-rate J-W fleet). The rate fell in 2016 and 2017 and was $16.60 in 2021 against $15.57 in 2014: seven years with almost no nominal rise, while its own costs rose. The filer says why: "During periods of low crude oil prices, we typically experience pressure on service rates and utilization from our customers in gas lift applications" (Item 1A), and the rises it reports come "as market conditions permit" (Item 7). The rows' measure is the price behaviour over time **[M2005-020]**; this price follows the industry's spare capacity in both directions.
5. **Unit volume** (the horsepower on rent). Average utilization, from each 10-K: 94.0% (2014), 90.5% (2015), 87.4% (2016), 92.0% (2017) for USAC; 91.9% (2018), 94.1% (2019), 86.8% (2020), 82.7% (2021), 88.6% (2022), 93.4% (2023), 94.6% (2024), 94.3% (2025). Revenue-generating horsepower at year-end: 3,310,024 (2019), 2,997,262 (2020), 2,964,206 (2021), 3,585,452 (2025). Volume fell 10% from 2019 to 2021 and recovered; the volume is the customers' production cycle, not a preference for this fleet.
6. **The low-cost position** **[L2004-007]**, **[M1997-010]**. On the same year and the filers' own definitions, adjusted gross margin over revenue in 2025: USAC 67.1% (its whole revenue); Kodiak's contract services 68.4% (65.7% in 2024); Archrock's contract operations 73% (67% in 2024), of which 2.8 points come from "a net benefit of $35.0 million as a result of certain sales and use tax audit settlements and credits", so about 70% without it. The filer lists price first among the bases of competition and names rivals who "adopt more aggressive pricing policies" (Item 1); its customers "could seek to preserve capital or reduce expenses by using lower-cost providers of compression services" (Item 1A). ## WHAT IN THE FRAMEWORK WAS WRONG OR UNCLEAR
Three things. (1) **Two Q2 rules meet on a cyclical field at its peak.** The OUT rule for "a field whose durable players earn ordinary returns over a full cycle" and the convention that "a change for the better is credited only on the evidence of a full cycle", TOO HARD (NATURE) until then, can both be read onto a field that earned ordinary returns through the last cycle and good ones in the latest three years. This run read the recent years as the up-leg of the same cycle (no filing claims a structural change; an earlier rise of the same kind, 2012 to 2014, was followed by falls), so the OUT rule governed. The text does not say which rule governs when the improvement is not claimed by anyone as a change, and another analyst could close the same facts TOO HARD (NATURE) with a re-look at the first year the field's volume falls; the dispatcher may want the line drawn. (2) **Whose tax, for a partnership.** "The company's own tax" is nearly nil for a publicly traded partnership because its holders pay the tax on allocated income, so the floor is applied to cash before any income tax at all, while a corporation's owner cash is after a 21% federal rate. The convention makes a partnership look about a fifth cheaper than the same business in corporate form; it did not decide anything here, but the text does not mention partnerships. (3) **The maintenance guess when the filer's own figure is implausible.** Specific (e) says the maintenance-only case is central "where the filing allows the guess" but not what to do when the filing states a maintenance figure ($39.4M) that its own fleet record contradicts; this run built the guess from filed facts (fleet, life, unit cost) and, because it came out at or above total spending, kept the all-spending case central. A sentence saying the analyst's own guess from filed facts is allowed, and the filer's label is not binding, would settle it. Also noted: `Screens/cover_shares.py` parsed no count from this filer's 10-Q cover, and `tools/run.py` does not see capital spending routed through inventory.
