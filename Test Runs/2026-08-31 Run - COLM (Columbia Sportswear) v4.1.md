## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**

- **Unit economics in my own words, without management's language:** Columbia designs
  outdoor and "active lifestyle" apparel, footwear and equipment under four owned
  trademarks — **Columbia** ($2,972.6M, 87.5% of sales), SOREL ($221.7M), prAna
  ($102.8M) and Mountain Hardwear ($100.2M) — owns **no factories at all**, and has the
  goods cut and sewn by contract manufacturers in 16 countries (apparel: Vietnam ~35%,
  Bangladesh ~30%, Indonesia ~10%, India ~10%; footwear: Vietnam ~80%, China ~10%). It
  imports the finished goods over a U.S. tariff wall and sells them two ways: **wholesale**
  ($1,780.6M, 52%) to ~1,950 U.S. and ~4,000 international retailers and distributors on
  cancellable, order-by-order terms with no long-term contracts, and **direct-to-consumer**
  ($1,616.8M, 48%) through roughly 570 leased retail and outlet stores worldwide plus
  brand e-commerce. It also collects **$21.8M of net licensing income** for letting others
  put the trademark on their goods. Consolidated gross margin ~50.5%; SG&A takes 44.2%;
  what is left is 6.1%. The whole model is: rent the manufacturing, own the trademark,
  and pay for shelf space either in someone else's store (wholesale margin) or in your own
  (retail margin, higher, but with rent and payroll attached — $186.9M of total lease cost
  in 2025).
- **The scarce input the business controls:** the **trademarks**, and nothing else. The
  factories are rented, the raw materials are bought by the contract manufacturers, the
  distribution centres are ordinary warehouses, the customer is a discretionary shopper
  and the wholesale customer is under no obligation. The business is one asset — a name —
  wrapped in working capital.
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** Yes as to *mechanism*:
  brands will still be designed here, made in Asia and sold through wholesale and DTC.
  What is genuinely open is whether the Columbia name is worth a premium to the consumer
  of 2036. That is a Q2 question about the franchise, not a Q1 question about
  comprehensibility.
- **[E4-46] check:** this is not a business requiring months of study. It is a simple,
  stable-character model with legible economics; the difficulty is in judging the brand,
  not in understanding the machine.
- **VERDICT: [x] IN.**

**The 10-K's own diagnosis, verbatim (Item 7, "Business Environment and Trends"):**
> *"**The Columbia brand in the U.S. has been under pressure due to numerous factors,
> including changes in consumer trends and competitive pressures.** While product
> functionality, quality and value remain important elements for consideration, outdoor
> consumer preferences have increasingly shifted to also incorporate versatility and style
> for everyday wear. **Athletic, athleisure, emerging outdoor, and other brands have

That is the [E3-03] criterion-2 failure written by the company. Other brands *capitalized*

### The filed evidence, in order

**The two-characteristic test [E2-44]: 0 of 2.**
1. *Can it raise prices when demand is flat and capacity is not fully utilised?* **No.**
   In FY2025 COLM absorbed **$31.0M of incremental U.S. tariffs into gross margin**
   ("prior to mitigation tactics", Q4 press release) rather than passing them through, and
   the 10-K's own forward-looking list flags *"product price elasticities resulting from
   **tariff-induced price increases**"* and warns that *"our tariff-induced price increases
   for the U.S. Spring 2026 and Fall 2026 seasons **may further impact demand**."* The risk
   factors add: *"there is **no assurance that price increases will be accepted** by our
   wholesale customers, international distributors or consumers"*, and list as a
   consequence of trade policy: *"Cause **an increase in promotional activity** in the U.S.
   marketplace to offset tariff-induced price increases."* Q2 FY2026 then reported
   *"unfavorable channel profitability resulting from **increased promotional activity**
   within DTC B&M."*
   **[E4-37] agony metric: agony-class, unambiguously.** *"it's not a great business when
   you have to have a prayer session before you raise your prices a penny."* A company that
   files three separate warnings about whether its own price increase will be accepted is
   holding the prayer session in public.
2. *Can it grow dollar volume with only minor additional investment of capital?* **No.**
   Over CY2021→CY2025, SG&A rose **$322.2M (+27.3%)** to produce **$271.0M (+8.7%)** of
   extra revenue, and operating income **fell 54%**. SG&A went from 37.8% to **44.2%** of
   sales. In FY2025 alone: demand-creation spend **+$24.1M**, omni-channel spend **+$36.9M**
   — and consolidated revenue rose 1% while DTC (the channel being invested in) **fell 1%**.

**Units vs dollars [E4-55] — and the absence is the finding.** COLM discloses **no
sales-unit series at all**: no units shipped, no pairs, no comparable-store sales, no sales
per square foot. Per the tasking, that absence is recorded as a finding in itself — the
Precision Steel lesson is that dollar revenue flattered by price is how a shrinking
franchise hides, and this filer publishes only the dollars. The one physical series it does
publish is *inventory* units, and it publishes it honestly: *"**While inventory dollars
were flat, units were down by approximately 11%** as of December 31, 2025."* Flat dollars
on 11% fewer units is an 11% rise in cost per unit carried — tariffs and price, not volume.

- **U.S. retail store fleet grew while U.S. sales fell.** 142 U.S. stores (2021-12-31) →
  156 (2022) → 172 + 28 temporary clearance (2024) → 173 + 8 (2025). U.S. segment net
  sales peaked at ≈$2,300M in 2022 and are **$1,979.0M** in 2025 — **−14% on a store
  count up 11%.**
- **Brand-level dollars:** SOREL **−7%**, Mountain Hardwear **−8%**, prAna **−1%** in
  FY2025. Three of the four brands shrank; only Columbia grew (+2%), and only because
  international offset a declining U.S.
