## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**
- Unit economics in my own words, no management language: MSA designs, certifies and makes safety equipment that laws and industry standards require workers and firefighters to use, and sells it mostly through about 1,800 distributor locations. Three product families in FY2025: **detection** $763.4M (41%: gas and flame detectors fixed in plants, refineries, refrigeration and water works, and hand-held gas monitors carried by workers), **fire service** $647.5M (34%: breathing sets for firefighters, fire helmets, protective clothing), and **industrial protective equipment** $463.9M (25%: hard hats, fall-arrest harnesses and lifelines, respirators). No customer is over 10% of sales. On each $100 of FY2025 sales ($1,874.8M) it paid **$53.50** to make the goods (materials are about two-thirds of that), spent **$22.10** on selling and overhead and **$3.50** on research, and kept **$19.80** of operating income ($371.8M, after $15.8M of currency losses, $10.5M of deal costs and $12.6M of acquired-intangible amortization). Much of the fixed-detection and breathing-set revenue is followed by years of replacement sensors, cylinders, parts and service. The capital is receivables and stock less payables, **$538.7M, about 29 cents per dollar of sales** (receivables $306.5M + inventories $343.0M − payables $110.8M), plus plant of **$283.1M** net; capital spending ran $42.8M, $54.2M and $68.4M in FY2023-FY2025 against depreciation and amortization of $60.8M, $64.3M and $71.6M. **Profit is (a price well above the cost of materials, sustained by certification, brand and the installed base) x (regulated replacement demand from industry and fire departments) less a mostly fixed selling and engineering organization.**
- The scarce input this business controls: **approved designs and the trust of buyers whose failure mode is death.** Breathing sets in North America *"must be approved by the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health, or NIOSH"* (FY2013 10-K) and meet NFPA standards; MSA's engineers *"work closely with the safety industry’s leading standards-setting groups"*; the filer names one piece of intellectual property whose loss would matter: *"no single patent, or patent application, trademark or license is, in our opinion, of such value to us that our business would be materially affected by the expiration or termination thereof, other than the “MSA” trademark."* Whether that is scarce against Dräger, 3M's Scott, Honeywell and the others is Q2's question, not Q1's.
- Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years? **Yes.** The company was making mine-safety equipment in 1914 and the FY1993 10-K describes the same trade (*"Principal products include respiratory protective equipment that is air-purifying, air-supplied and self-contained in design. The registrant also produces instruments that monitor and analyze workplace environments"*) against *"many large and small enterprises"*; purchases are, in the filer's words, *"non-discretionary"* and *"often mandated by government and industry regulations"*. What changes is the electronics and software content (connected detectors, cloud software), which the filer spends on continuously (*"frequent introductions of new products"*), and the standards themselves, which periodically re-set the fire-service replacement cycle. That is a question about the moat's durability (Q2), not about how the money is made.

### THE STRONGEST EVIDENCE AGAINST, written first (operator rule 9, [E4-26], [E4-51])
The reading below is IN, NARROW. The case for OUT is set out first, at full strength, as its holders would state it.
1. **Price is named among the grounds of competition, in the filer's own words, for twenty-seven years.** FY1999: *"For most of the registrant's products and in most markets, principal methods of competition include product features, quality and price."*; FY2000-FY2003 the same sentence with *"are"*. FY2004-FY2017 (Item 1): *"participants in this industry compete primarily on the basis of product characteristics (such as functional performance, agency approvals, design and style), price, brand name recognition and service"* (price moved to the end of the list in FY2014 and left Item 1 in FY2018). FY2025 (Item 1A): *"compete primarily on the basis of product characteristics (such as functional performance, technology, cost of ownership, comfort, design and style), price, service and delivery"* and *"our business could be adversely affected by competitors’ new product innovations, technological advances made to competing products and solutions and pricing changes made by us in response to competition from existing or new competitors"*; *"Some of our competitors have greater financial and other resources than we do"*. [E3-03] criterion (2) asks whether customers think there is **no close substitute**; a filer that has told its owners every year since FY2005 that it changes prices in response to competitors has not said that.
2. **The certification that protects MSA also makes its rivals' products substitutes by design.** Every breathing set sold to a U.S. fire department must pass the same NIOSH approval and NFPA standard; a department choosing among certified sets is choosing among products the standard has made equivalent on the tested points. The filed record shows share moving with product generations: breathing apparatus sales **−7% in FY2014** (*"breathing apparatus decreased 7%, on a local currency basis"*) and then *"Strong G1 self-contained breathing apparatus sales throughout the year drove growth in breathing apparatus, up 113% during the period"* (FY2015). A lead that must be re-won at each standard cycle is the [E3-74] have-to-stay-smart class, and the filer says so: *"If we are unable to identify emerging customer and technological trends, maintain and improve the competitiveness of our products and solutions and introduce new ones, we may lose our market position"*.
3. **For eighteen years the same brand, products and approvals earned ordinary returns.** Gross margin was 36-41% in every year FY1996-FY2010 (38.9% FY1997, 36.4% FY1999, 37.8% FY2006-FY2007, 37.0% FY2009, 37.7% FY2010), the FY1998 MD&A cited *"competitive pricing and promotions in personal protective equipment markets"*, and in FY2009 the margin fell on *"sales mix, lower production volumes, and recessionary pricing pressures"* in Europe and International. EBIT on net tangible operating assets was **18.2% in FY2009 and 14.8% in FY2010** (`q2series_out.txt`). [E2-53]'s test is whether position, not execution, sets the economics; a position that produced middling returns until management changed its pricing and mix looks like execution.
4. **The good economics are one segment's.** In FY2020-FY2025 the Americas segment earned an adjusted operating margin of 22.3-30.5% and International **12.6-16.2%**, close to Dräger's safety division (11.3-11.9%, below). If MSA had a franchise in its products, it would show wherever the products are sold; what shows is a strong North American position (fire service tied to NFPA and NIOSH, 1,800 distributor locations) and an ordinary business abroad.
5. **The fire-service customer is a public body buying on grants and budgets.** FY2025: fire service fell *"partly due to a shift in Assistance to Firefighters Grant (AFG) funding as well as the 2025 U.S. federal government shutdown"*, and International fire service declined *"due to budgetary shifts within key European markets"*. Government buyers tender; a tender is a price contest among qualified bidders.
