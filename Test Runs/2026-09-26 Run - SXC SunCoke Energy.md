## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**
- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language.** SunCoke owns five coke plants in Ohio, Illinois, Indiana and Virginia (3.7 million tons a year of nameplate capacity before the Haverhill I closure in Q1 2026). It buys metallurgical coal (5.6 million tons in 2025, about 1.4 tons of coal per ton of coke), bakes it into coke in ovens that burn off the gases and use the heat to make steam or electricity, and sells the coke to a handful of blast-furnace steelmakers under multi-year contracts. **The contract price is three pieces: the coal cost, passed through to the steelmaker as long as SunCoke hits a contractual coal-to-coke yield; an operating-cost piece, either fixed and indexed to inflation or passed through against an agreed budget; and a fixed fee per ton, set when the contract is signed and unchanged for its life.** The customer must take the contracted tons or pay for them. So the money is the fixed fee per ton times the tons, plus or minus how well SunCoke runs the ovens against the yield and cost standards, plus sales of steam and power, plus spot and export sales of any tons beyond the contracts, plus foundry coke from Jewell sold on annual contracts. A second segment handles bulk materials for a per-ton fee (coal export at the Convent terminal in Louisiana, coal mixing at the Kanawha terminal in West Virginia, a captive terminal at Indiana Harbor) and, since August 2025, removes and processes slag and scrap inside steel mills (Phoenix Global) under multi-year contracts with fixed and volume-linked fees. In 2025, Domestic Coke was $1,613.8M of $1,837.3M revenue; two customers, Cleveland-Cliffs (61.6%) and U.S. Steel (14.2%), bought 75.8% of everything (Note 4).
- **The scarce input this business controls.** **Permitted, operating heat-recovery coke capacity next to or within reach of its customers' blast furnaces, and the contracts on it.** Item 1: *"We have constructed the only greenfield cokemaking facilities in the U.S. in over 35 years"*; *"our Domestic Coke segment has accounted for approximate ly 38 percent of the U.S. blast furnace coke market capacity"* (the space is in the extracted text); three plants deliver *"by a conveyor belt leading to the customer's blast furnace"*. The permits and the location are hard to replicate; the technology is patented (102 US patents). What it does not control is its raw material (coal is bought on one-year contracts, and *"The customer can generally exercise an overriding vote on most coal procurement decisions"*) or its customers' choice of steelmaking route. Whether the scarce capacity gives the customer no close substitute is Q2's question.
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** **The mechanism, yes; the customers and the volume, not assured, and the filings say so.** The FY2011 10-K describes the same model (take-or-pay contracts with pass-through coal and a fixed fee; *"For the year ended December 31, 2011, ArcelorMittal, our largest customer, accounted for approximately 64 percent of our total revenues"*, with U.S. Steel 18% and AK Steel 14%); the FY2025 10-K describes it in nearly the same words, with Cleveland-Cliffs now owning two of the three original customers (it bought AK Steel and ArcelorMittal USA in 2020). Item 1A names the direction of the demand: *"electric arc furnace technology is a commercially proven process widely used in the U.S. As these alternative processes for production of steel become more widespread, the demand for blast furnace coke, including the coke we produce, may be significantly reduced."* Algoma Steel refused further tons in 2025 and the Haverhill I plant was closed; Granite City is on one-year extensions. That is a statement about how much coke will be bought, which Q2 and Q4 weigh; the way each ton earns is not in doubt.
- **Relatively simple and stable in character [E3-31]?** Simple: a toll processor paid a fixed fee per ton on contracted volume, with the commodity cost passed through, plus a handling business paid per ton. Stable in mechanism for the fifteen years filed. Not a technology business and not [E4-46]'s months-of-study case. The complexity is in the accounting history (the carve-out, the MLP and its unwinding, the coal-mining exit in 2016, the Indian joint venture written off), all of which the filings state.
- **VERDICT: [x] IN**: the money is made visibly, as a fixed fee per ton of coke on contracted tons, with coal passed through and operating costs fixed or budgeted, plus per-ton handling and mill-service fees. **IN is not a finding on the franchise.**

**[E2-58] and [E2-59], the commodity doctrine, applied as the framework states it.** Coke is undifferentiated *"in any customer-important way"* except quality, which the registrant claims but which the contract prices only through the fixed fee set at signing. [E2-59]: commodity businesses may escape *"if prices or costs are administered in some manner and thereby insulated at least partially from normal market forces"*. The take-or-pay contract is that administration, done privately: coal cost passed through, operating cost indexed or budgeted, a fee fixed for the term, volume guaranteed or paid for. **That is why the earnings are steady, and it is why the steadiness belongs to the contract, not to the product.** The framework's own reading (Q2, *"the moat belongs to the regime"*): the regime floors the profit while it lasts, and the renewal is where *"That day is gone"* is written; Granite City's *"significantly lower overall economics"* is that sentence in this filer's words, and the 2019 bankruptcy of a logistics customer, *"Murray American Coal, Inc.'s bankruptcy and the rejection of its take-or-pay contract"* (FY2019 10-K), shows that the contract is only as strong as the buyer. **The one exception [E2-58] allows is "a cost advantage that is both wide and sustainable".** The registrant claims quality, heat-recovery power sales, environmental performance and new plants against *"aging by-product coke batteries"*; if that were a wide cost advantage it would show in the fee at renewal and in the return on capital, and neither shows it.
