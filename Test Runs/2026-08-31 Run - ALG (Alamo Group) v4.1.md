## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? [E3-31]

- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language.** Alamo buys steel, hydraulic
  hoses, paint, tires, engines, gearboxes, drivelines, tractors and truck chassis — all of
  which its own filing says are "available from a variety of sources in adequate quantities
  and at prevailing market prices" — and welds and bolts them into large yellow and orange
  machines: boom mowers that cut the grass on a highway verge, street sweepers, vacuum trucks
  that clean a storm drain, telescopic excavators, snow blowers and plough blades, wood
  chippers, highway attenuator trucks, grapple loaders, rotary cutters for a farm tractor. It
  does this in **27 plants** with **3,800 people** under about **forty brand names** acquired
  over fifty years, and sells the output through independent dealers and directly to
  contractors and municipalities. Out of every dollar of the **$1,603.7M** of 2025 revenue,
  cost of sales takes **75.2 cents**, SG&A and intangible amortisation take **15.4 cents**,
  and **9.5 cents** is operating income — of which interest takes 0.6 and tax 2.2, leaving
  **6.5 cents of net income**. **$261.7M, 16.3% of revenue, is replacement parts** sold back
  into an installed base. The balance sheet is where the money actually lives: $383.3M of
  inventories and $276.9M of receivables against only $166.0M of net plant, so the business
  consumes about **35 cents of working capital for every dollar of revenue** and only about
  **1.9 cents a year of fixed capital expenditure**. It turns roughly **$116M of operating
  cash a year on a five-year mean**, spends $30M of it on plant, pays out $14-17M of
  dividends, and puts most of the rest into buying more small equipment companies.
- **The scarce input this business controls: I cannot name one that is genuinely scarce, and
  saying so is a Q1 answer, not an evasion.** The raw materials are commodities by the
  filing's own words. There is no patent estate of consequence disclosed, no network, no
  float, no subscription, no deferred-revenue pool, no customer above 10% of revenue. The
  three candidates are **(i)** the dealer and distributor networks built over fifty years,
  **(ii)** the parts annuity attached to an installed base of machines with 10-to-20-year
  lives, and **(iii)** approved-vendor status on municipal and state purchasing schedules.
  All three are real and all three are ordinary. **The absence of a scarce input is recorded
  here and carried to Q2, where it decides the question** — per the template's own routing,
  a warning about the business belongs at Q2, not disguised as a compliment elsewhere.
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** **Yes.** Highway verges will
  still need mowing, storm drains still need vacuuming, streets still need sweeping and snow
  still needs moving, and the entities that do it will still be highway departments,
  airports, municipalities and contractors buying purpose-built machines from specialists.
  The format has been essentially unchanged since 1969. Nothing here is a rapid-change
  industry.
- **[E4-46] check:** this decision does not need five months. Two segments, one 100-page
  10-K, a plain FIFO income statement, no financial-services arm, no float, no equity-method
  stakes, no off-balance-sheet structures. **It is inside the circle**, and that is precisely
  why the file must be closed on the business rather than on my diligence if it closes.

### THE CASE FOR ALG BEING A FRANCHISE, stated as its holders would state it [E4-51]

1. **Demand is non-discretionary and budget-funded.** Grass on a state highway right-of-way
   must be cut whether or not the economy is good. Snow must be moved. The customer is a
   public works department with an appropriated maintenance budget, not a consumer.
2. **ALG says it holds leading positions**, and the claim is specific: "the Company believes
   that within the U.S. it is a **leading supplier to governmental markets**, a leading
   supplier in the U.S. agricultural market, and one of the largest suppliers in the European
   market for its key niche product offerings."
3. **There is a real parts annuity** — $261.7M, 16.3% of revenue, sold into machines with
   long lives through a dealer network a new entrant cannot replicate cheaply.
4. **The Industrial Equipment Division is a good business and it is now 59% of revenue.** It
   grew net sales **34% in two years** ($710.6M → $949.7M) in flat end markets and lifted its
   operating margin from **10.7% to 13.5%**; its Q4 2025 adjusted operating margin was 14.9%.
5. **The 2025 consolidated margin is depressed by identifiable one-time items** — CEO
   transition, acquisition and integration costs and facility-consolidation restructuring,
   $14.8M pre-tax in total, which puts adjusted operating margin at 10.4% rather than 9.5%.
6. **The acquisition programme has been disciplined on price.** Goodwill and intangibles are
   only **22.4% of total assets**, against 41.9% at Federal Signal and 37.2% at Douglas
   Dynamics. ALG has paid less for what it bought than its peers did.
7. **No customer is 10% of revenue**, and the business survived 2009, 2020 and two inflation
   shocks with positive net income in every year on record.

**That is a fair statement of the case, and every fact in it is filed. It is not enough, and
the reasons follow.**

- **Needed or desired — [x] YES.** Conceded without argument. Roadside mowing, street

  A franchise is a product "**thought by its customers to have no close substitute**". A
