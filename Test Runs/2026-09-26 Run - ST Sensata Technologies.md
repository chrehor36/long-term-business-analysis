## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**
- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language.** Sensata makes small engineered parts that sit inside other companies' machines: sensors that turn a pressure, temperature, force or position into an electrical signal (tyre-pressure sensors, brake and transmission pressure sensors, exhaust temperature sensors), and electrical protection parts (thermal switches and motor protectors in compressors and appliances, circuit breakers, and high-voltage contactors and fuses for electric vehicles and batteries). Its customers are carmakers and their Tier 1 suppliers (the Automotive segment, 57.0% of FY2025 revenue), makers of heating and cooling equipment, appliances and industrial machinery (Industrials, 21.3%), and makers of aircraft, trucks, construction and farm machines (Aerospace, Defense, and Commercial Equipment, 21.7%). The sale is won years ahead: the part is engineered to one customer's application, tested and certified on one vehicle or machine platform, and then supplied for that platform's life (*"which in the case of the automotive industry typically lasts five to seven years"*), usually for a share of the customer's requirement rather than a fixed quantity (*"Many of our customer agreements provide for the supply of a certain share of the customer's requirements for a particular application or platform, rather than for a specific quantity of products"*). Volume therefore follows the customers' production (light-vehicle, truck and appliance builds) times the number of Sensata parts per unit. Manufacturing is in low-cost countries (net PP&E $776.5M, of which China $254.3M, Mexico $162.8M, Malaysia $120.5M, Bulgaria $99.4M, United States $99.4M). The money is a gross margin of about 29-38% on the part (29.3% in FY2025 after depreciation), less engineering and selling costs, leaving segment operating margins of about 24% (Automotive $500.8M on about $2.11bn), 29% (Industrials $226.0M) and 26% (ADCE $211.5M) before $344.1M of corporate costs, $80.2M of acquired-intangible amortisation and $225.7M of goodwill impairment. Every year the customer asks for the price to fall (*"Many of our customers, including transportation manufacturers and other industrial and commercial OEMs, demand annual price reductions"*), and Sensata earns its margin by making the part cheaper faster than the price falls.
- **The scarce input this business controls.** **Not a resource; an incumbency: the position of the certified supplier on a platform for its life.** The filing states it: *"Once our solutions are designed into an application, we are well positioned as the incumbent supplier due to the high degree of sensor customization and application/vehicle platform certification. This results in high switching costs for automotive manufacturers once a sensor is designed into a particular system or platform."* Behind it: engineering relationships (*"We have had relationships with our top ten customers for an average of 36 years"*) and manufacturing scale in low-cost locations. Nothing owned is scarce in the way a licence or a site is: *"we do not believe that our overall competitive position is dependent on patent protection or that our overall business is dependent upon any single patent or group of related patents"*. Whether that incumbency is a franchise is Q2's question; Q1 records that the scarce thing is renewed platform by platform, not held.
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** **The way it earns, yes; its product mix is already changing and it has shown it cannot yet judge the new part.** The FY2010 10-K describes the same business (Sensors and Controls segments; carmakers, Tier 1s, HVAC and appliance makers; design-in on platforms; annual price-downs), and the FY2025 10-K describes it again under new segment names. What changes is the vehicle: the internal-combustion powertrain sensors (fuel, exhaust, oil pressure) decline as electric vehicles grow, and high-voltage contactors, fuses and battery parts replace them (*"As more transportation platforms leverage a plug-in hybrid or fully electrified powertrain, the ability to protect the vehicle systems/sub-systems from high-voltage power sources becomes critical"*). The company's own bets on that change (Dynapower, Spear, and the Xirgo and SmartWitness telematics business called Insights) were written down or sold within four years of purchase (goodwill impairments $697.5M in FY2023-FY2025). The mechanism stays understandable: a component engineered into a platform, supplied for its life, repriced downward every year. Whether its economics survive the change of platform is Q2's [E4-04] question and Q4's.
- **Relatively simple and stable in character [E3-31]?** The core mechanism is simple and has been stable for twenty years of filings; the end markets are cyclical (revenue fell 20.2% in FY2009 and 11.7% in FY2020) and the powertrain content is in transition. Not a business that would take months to understand [E4-46].

*Every Sensata figure below is read from the filed 10-Ks for FY2010 to FY2025 (Items 1 and 1A, the MD&A net-revenue discussions, the statements and the segment notes; FY2008-FY2009 from the FY2010 10-K) and the 10-Q to 2026-06-30, or from SEC XBRL companyfacts checked against the filed statements (Step 0; every cash-flow line used was read off each filed statement by `cfgrep.py`, output `cfgrep_out.txt`). **The hypothesis I formed while reading, stated so it can be attacked [E4-26]:** *Sensata's parts are hard to dislodge once designed into a platform, but the choice of supplier is made in a competitive award among three or four qualified makers (two of them, Bosch and Denso, also its customers), and after the award the customer takes a price reduction every year. The customer thinks it has close substitutes at the moment it buys, and the lock-in lasts only a platform's life. That is [E3-43]'s "business", a low-cost operator whose cost gains pass to the customer as price, and the returns should show it: high on tangible capital because the parts are small and the plants cheap, but falling as the price-downs outrun the cost-downs.* The evidence against, above all the design-in switching cost and the returns, is hunted and answered before the verdict.
