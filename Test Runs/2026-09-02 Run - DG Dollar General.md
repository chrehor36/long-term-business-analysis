**Four findings, and the first destroys the headline reading of the series.**

1. **Fiscal 2022's $351M is not an earnings collapse. It is a $1,920.1M working-capital
   absorption sitting on top of a still-peak operating year.** Ex-working-capital, fiscal 2022
   was **the best year in the entire series ($2,271.4M)**. The peak-to-trough fall in owner
   earnings was $2,428.3M; the working-capital line alone swung **$2,478.5M** across the same
   two years — **more than the whole decline**. The other moving part, a $532.6M capex
   step-up, runs the same way. Nothing in the P&L broke in 2022: pretax income was $3,116.6M,
   *higher* than 2021's.
2. **The real earnings event is fiscal 2023–24 and the headline series hides it.** Ex-WC owner
   earnings fell $2,271.4M → **$941.0M** → **$1,002.6M**, **−58%**, while the *reported* series
   was busy recovering off its trough. Pretax income confirms it: $3,116.6M → $2,119.5M →
   **$1,439.8M**. This is the shrink-and-margin event, quantified at (b)(ii).
3. **Fiscal 2025's $2,301.8M is flattered.** It contains **$613.8M of working-capital release**
   — inventory drawdown plus an accrual DG attributes *"primarily to an increase in accrued
   incentive compensation"*, which pays out in fiscal 2026 — **plus $199.2M of income-tax
   timing**. Ex-WC: **$1,688.0M**. The Q2 fiscal 2026 10-Q already shows the reversal beginning:
   **26-week operating cash flow $1,496.7M against $1,814.9M a year earlier, down $318.2M**, on
   a $249.0M inventory build and a $140.9M tax use, in a half-year when net income *rose*
   $191.1M.
4. **But the working-capital increment is NOT removed, and the ex-WC column is a diagnostic,
   never the reported number.** [E2-23] is explicit that where a business requires additional
   working capital, *"the increment also should be included in (c)"*, and the [E2-23] LIFO
   carve-out does not rescue DG — it applies only *"if unit volume does not change"*, and DG
   adds 450–1,000 stores a year. Over the eight years shown, working capital **absorbed
   $1,043.1M net, about $130M a year**. That is the genuine structural increment, and it stays
   inside owner earnings exactly as the convention intends.

**And the window spread is the same artifact.** Fiscal 2021–25 is net WC-**absorbing** by
$1,471.4M (−$294M/yr, which makes the corpus-default 5-year mean *conservative*); fiscal
2023–25 is net WC-**releasing** by $937.4M (+$312M/yr, which makes the 3-year mean *generous*).
**The +14.7% conservative-end window spread is almost entirely a question of which side of one
inventory roundtrip the window lands on.** Under [E4-25] the range width IS the finding, and
the finding here is that **the width is measurable, attributable and largely non-operating**.
It is **not** UNKNOWABLE: every dollar of the swing is traceable to a filed line.

**[E4-41] — normalize DOWN for luck.** Fiscal 2020 is the stimulus year: same-store sales
**+16.3%**, net sales +21.6%, operating profit +54.4%, and a **+$558.4M** working-capital
tailwind (payables +$745.6M, accrued +$388.6M) on top. It is named and removed: the eight-year
mean excluding it is **$1,316.4M** (capex end) versus $1,479.0M including it. **But note the
scope: fiscal 2020 sits OUTSIDE the corpus-default five-year window [E2-42] altogether**, so
unlike the SHOE case the boom year does not contaminate the primary estimate. It costs 11% on
the nine-year window and nothing on the five-year one.

### (b)(ii) Shrink — quantified in dollars, from the filed segment note

The fiscal 2025 10-K Note 12 segment reconciliation discloses **shrink included in cost of
goods sold** as a dollar line (the ASU 2023-07 significant-segment-expense disclosure). This
is a hard filed number, not an estimate:

| fiscal | shrink in COGS ($000) | % of net sales |
|---|---|---|
| 2023 | **910,674** | 2.35% |
| 2024 | **928,896** | 2.29% |
| 2025 | **634,293** | **1.48%** |

**Excess over the fiscal-2025 rate: $336M in fiscal 2023 = 13.7% of that year's operating
profit; $326M in fiscal 2024 = 19.0%.** The $294.6M year-on-year reduction in fiscal 2025 is
**60% of the entire $489.6M operating-profit improvement**. The dollar disclosure does not
exist before fiscal 2023 because the standard did not require it; the qualitative trail runs
*"an improved rate of inventory shrinkage"* (fiscal 2017–18) → *"a higher rate"* (2019–20) →
*"significantly elevated and materially impacted our results"* (2023 and 2024) → *"has
significantly improved from prior elevated levels"* (2025 and Q2 fiscal 2026).

**Ruling: one-off in its magnitude, structural in its floor.** DG names the causes itself —
*"materially higher inventory levels in the first half of the year, **store standards and other
operational challenges such as self-checkout and store manager turnover**, as well as the
macroeconomic environment"* — and all three were self-inflicted and have been acted on. But a
**1.48% base rate is a permanent cost of the format**, and DG's own standing risk factor says
so: *"We experience significant inventory shrinkage and damages … sustained high rates of
inventory shrink at certain stores have historically contributed … to the closure of certain
stores and the impairment of long-term assets."* A 20,900-store chain running **9.3 employees
per store** cannot supervise its own shelves the way a 373-employee Costco warehouse can.

retail prices and **flat items per transaction**"* — traffic up, basket units flat, so units

plainly and it is not forced.**

**What the test does give, and it is the finding:** DG added **~30.6 million selling square feet

| **real $/sq ft, 2020 dollars** | 240 | 238 | 240 | **273** | 250 | 241 | 224 | 217 | **217** |

**Real sales per square foot has fallen in each of the last five years, from $273 to $217
(−20.5%), and now sits 9–10% BELOW the 2017–2019 pre-pandemic level.** The productivity of the
square foot has not held; it has been held *nominally* by price.

**And the company's own answer on the incremental store is the opening cadence:** 1,315 (2017)
· 900 · 975 · 1,000 · 1,050 · 1,039 · 987 · **725** · **589 (2025)** · **~460 planned for
2026** — a 56% cut in four years, with the reason filed: *"while new store returns remain
strong, these increased costs have negatively impacted our **projected new store returns** and
influenced our new store growth plans."*

## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**

- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language.** Dollar General rents **20,893
  boxes** — the newest ones about **8,500 square feet of selling space**, roughly a ninth of a
  Walmart supercenter — in small towns, strip centres and low-income neighbourhoods across 48
  states, staffs each with about **nine people in total** (194,000 employees ÷ 20,893 stores),
  and fills them with a deliberately narrow assortment of cheap consumables priced *"typically
  $10 or less."* **Eighty-two per cent of what it sells is consumables** — food, paper,
  cleaning products, health and beauty, pet — the lowest-margin category in retail, carried
  because it brings people through the door often. It keeps **30.7 cents of gross profit** on
  the dollar and gives **25.5 cents straight back** as selling, general and administrative
  expense, overwhelmingly store payroll and rent, leaving **5.2 cents of operating profit**.
  It owns almost none of the real estate: rent is **$2.01 billion of cash a year** against
  $2.20 billion of operating profit, and the capitalised lease obligation is **$11.1 billion**
  — 2.4 times its funded debt and 1.3 times its book equity. The average store does **$2.045
  million of sales a year and about $105,000 of operating profit**, against roughly **$96,000
  of rent**. The whole business is: *put a small cheap shop within five miles of nearly
  everybody, sell them washing powder at a thin margin, and hope the rent and the payroll and
  the shrink leave five cents on the dollar.*
- **The scarce input this business controls.** **Locations, and nothing else.** The merchandise
  is other people's national brands plus private label; the real estate is rented on a 9.1-year
  average remaining term; the customer is not contracted; there is no membership, no
  subscription, no switching cost, no proprietary product. What DG has is a **physical
  position**: *"approximately 75% of the U.S. population located within five miles of a Dollar
  General store."* Whether that position is a moat is the whole of Q2, and it is tested there
  rather than assumed here.
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** **Yes as to the mechanism.**
  People on low and fixed incomes will still buy consumables in small quantities close to home;
  nothing about the physics is changing, and the format has survived since 1939 and through
  *"a variety of economic cycles."* What is uncertain is the **count and the margin** — how many
  boxes are open and what each earns — and those are questions for Q2 and Q4, not for
  understandability.
- **[E4-46] check — is this inside the circle?** One reportable segment, one country
  (17 Mexican stores excepted), one currency, a twelve-note financial statement set, no
  derivatives beyond none, no equity-method investees, and a product a child can identify.
  There is nothing here that would take five months. **It is inside the circle and it is one of
  the simplest large businesses in this queue.**
- **The honest residual, named:** the one thing that is genuinely hard to model is the
  **customer's income**, which DG says depends on *"unemployment and underemployment rates,
  inflation, wage growth, changes in federal and state tax policies, interest rates, changes in
  U.S. and global trade policy (including price increases resulting from tariffs), and changes
  in U.S. government policy and assistance programs … such as SNAP, unemployment benefits, and
  economic stimulus programs."* That is a macro dependency, not an unintelligible business, and
  it is carried to Q4 as a named exposure rather than used to fail Q1.
