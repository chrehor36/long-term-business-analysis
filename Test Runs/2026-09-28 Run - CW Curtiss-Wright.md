## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**
- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language:** Curtiss-Wright designs and builds engineered parts and subsystems that go inside other companies' long-lived machines, and sells the spares and services that keep them running. Three groups. **Naval & Power ($1,503M in FY2025, 15.4% operating margin)**: the pumps, motors, generators, valves and turbines inside US nuclear submarines and carriers (*"main coolant pumps, power-dense compact motors, generators, steam turbines, valves ... most notably supporting the Virginia-class and Columbia-class submarine programs, as well as the Ford-class aircraft carrier program"*), ship repair at three Navy service centres, aircraft arresting gear, and hardware, valves, pumps and instrumentation for commercial nuclear plants (*"content on every reactor operating in the U.S., Canada, and U.K."*), plus severe-service valves for refineries. **Defense Electronics ($1,019M, 27.3%)**: rugged computer boards and boxes built to open standards for military aircraft, vehicles and ships, flight-test data recorders, tactical network radios and turret stabilization. **Aerospace & Industrial ($977M, 17.0%)**: sensors, actuators and flight controls for commercial and military aircraft, shot-peening and coating services on engine and airframe parts, and throttle, shifter and power-electronics controls for off-highway and specialty vehicles. The customers are defense prime contractors and the US Government (47% of sales, *"a subcontractor to prime contractors for the vast majority of our government business"*), Boeing and Airbus and their suppliers, nuclear utilities and Westinghouse, and vehicle makers. The work is sold on mostly fixed-price contracts (*"The most significant portion of our defense revenues is comprised of long-term programs and primarily fixed-price contracts"*), so the company bears overruns; backlog $4.1bn, 90% to be recognized within 36 months. Costs are engineers, machinists and materials; research runs 2.7% of sales; capital spending 1.5-2.6% of sales since FY2016.
- **The scarce input this business controls:** qualification on the platform. A part designed into a submarine's reactor plant, a fighter's mission computer or a nuclear plant's safety system is certified for that platform and, with rare exceptions, is bought from the same supplier for the platform's life (*"unique, sole-source positions"*; *"a presence on more than 400 defense platforms and more than 3,000 programs worldwide"*; *"We have a long legacy of providing products that support nuclear propulsion systems on naval vessels"*). The scarce inputs are that installed position, the engineering staff that holds it (the 10-K says the largest asset it buys in an acquisition is *"the value of their assembled workforces"*), and nuclear-quality manufacturing that few suppliers still keep (*"suppliers abandoning the commercial nuclear market"* is listed among its growth drivers).
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** For Naval & Power and the aircraft components, yes: the Virginia, Columbia and Ford programs run for decades, a nuclear plant licensed to 60 and 80 years buys qualified parts from qualified suppliers, and aircraft built this decade fly for twenty-five years. For Defense Electronics the product changes every few years (the filer: *"The commercial and government markets in which we operate are characterized by rapidly changing technologies"*), though the way it earns (winning a design slot and supplying it for the program's life) does not; whether that lead is maintained or rebuilt from zero each generation is [E4-04]'s question and is asked at Q2. The general-industrial vehicle controls depend on truck and off-highway cycles.
- **Why this is inside the circle despite the breadth:** the company is many small businesses, but each earns the same way (a qualified component or service on a long-lived platform, priced in a contract), the numbers are filed by segment and end market every year, and the [E2-75] exclusion (*"simple businesses (if there's lots of technology, we won't understand it)"*) bites on one segment's product, not on how the company makes money. [E4-46]'s test is met: this is not a business that would take months of study to follow; the open questions are about the width of each position, which are Q2's.
- **VERDICT: [x] IN  [ ] OUT  [ ] UNRESEARCHED → ____  [ ] UNKNOWABLE → ____** **IN.** A maker of qualified components, subsystems and services for long-lived defense, aerospace and nuclear platforms, sold mostly on fixed-price contracts to prime contractors, the Navy, aircraft makers and utilities; how it makes money is plain in the filed segments, and what needs judging is whether each position is priced like a franchise, which is Q2.
