## A.3b THE LANGUAGE SHIFT BETWEEN VINTAGES — A DISCLOSURE-TONE FINDING

Compare how the same event is described one year apart. Both rung [1], both verbatim.

**FY2024 10-K** (`goog-20241231.htm`, filed 2025-02-05, accession **0001652044-25-000014**):

> "**Search:** In October 2020, the DOJ and a number of state Attorneys General filed a lawsuit in
> the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia **alleging that Google violated U.S.
> antitrust laws** relating to Search and Search advertising. In August 2024, the U.S. District
> Court for the District of Columbia **ruled that Google violated such U.S. antitrust laws.** A
> separate proceeding is being held to determine remedies, the range of which vary widely. The DOJ
> has proposed a high level remedy framework, which includes alterations to our products and services
> and our business models and operations, **including structural remedies, and/or our distribution
> arrangements**, among other changes, some of which could have a material adverse effect on our
> business. We have filed our own remedies proposal ahead of a hearing on remedies in April 2025.
> **We expect a decision likely in the second half of 2025, after which we intend to appeal.**"

**FY2025 10-K** (filed 2026-02-05, accession **0001652044-26-000018**), same bullet:

> "…concerning Google's Search and Search advertising practices and **its compliance with US
> antitrust laws**. In August 2024, the US District Court for the District of Columbia **ruled
> against Google.**"

Three softenings in one year, none explained:
1. "**violated** U.S. antitrust laws" → "**ruled against Google**"
2. "**alleging that Google violated**" → "concerning … **its compliance with**"
3. The FY2024 acknowledgement that structural remedies and distribution arrangements were on the
   table is **removed** from the FY2025 Search bullet (it survives only in the *ad tech* bullet).

This is not a misstatement — the FY2025 language is accurate. It is a measurable reduction in the
specificity of an adverse disclosure at the exact moment the adverse outcome became final. Record
it under Q3 (are they honest, and are they rational?) as tone, not as a violation.

## A.5 **DOES ANY DOLLAR FIGURE FOR DEFAULT-PLACEMENT / DISTRIBUTION PAYMENTS APPEAR ANYWHERE IN ANY ALPHABET FILING?**

**One line, and it was withdrawn on 2019-10-29.**

The only dollar figure Alphabet has ever filed that measures the cost of default placement is
**"TAC to distribution partners"** — the line documented in full in `B2-tac-and-metrics.md`. Its
last filed value was **$10,856 million for the nine months ended September 30, 2019** (Q3-2019
10-Q, accession 0001652044-19-000032). Alphabet's own definition of who those partners are, from
the FY2016 10-K forward, verbatim:

> "…amounts paid to our distribution partners who make available our search access points and
> services. Our distribution partners include browser providers, mobile carriers, original equipment
> manufacturers, and software developers."

Browser providers = Mozilla and (via the ISA) Apple's Safari. Mobile carriers and OEMs = the
Android MADA/RSA counterparties.

**Since 2019-10-29, no Alphabet filing has contained any dollar figure for these payments.** Not
the total, not a partner-level figure, not a range, not a percentage of the specific revenue they
buy. The FY2025 10-K discloses "Accrued revenue share" as a balance-sheet accrual ($9,802m at
2024-12-31; **$10,864m at 2025-12-31**) — but that line commingles Google Network publisher
share, Play developer share and distribution-partner share, and Alphabet files no breakdown of it.

There is **no Alphabet filing, of any type, that names Apple as a payee, and none that states the
amount of the default-placement payments.** The widely cited ~$20bn/year Apple figure is a
**RUNG [2] COURT-RECORD fact from *US v. Google*, not a rung-1 Alphabet disclosure** — see Part B.

**One further absence, worth recording.** The FY2025 10-K carries a full risk factor on
distribution dependence:

> "People access our products and services through a variety of platforms and devices that continue
> to evolve with the advancement of technology and user preferences. If manufacturers and users do
> not widely adopt versions of our products and services developed for these interfaces, our
> business could be harmed. … Each manufacturer or distributor may establish unique technical
> standards for its devices … **Some manufacturers may also elect not to include our products on
> their devices.** … Failing to attract and retain a substantial number of device manufacturers,
> suppliers, distributors, developers, and users … could harm our business, financial condition, and
> operating results."

The risk factor describes the dependence on manufacturers and distributors in full — and never once
says that Google **pays** them, or how much. "Some manufacturers may also elect not to include our
products on their devices" is written as a technology-adoption risk. It is, in fact, a
contract-renewal risk on tens of billions of dollars of annual payments.

**This is the single most important disclosure finding in the GOOGL run.** The number that
determines whether Google's search franchise is bought or earned exists in the court record and
does not exist in the filings.

*(For a bounded reconstruction of that number from filed inputs — approximately $38–41bn in 2025,
about two-thirds of all TAC, rising every year — see `B2-tac-and-metrics.md` §1.6b. That
reconstruction is explicitly labelled **CONVENTION** and is not a rung-1 fact.)*

## A.7 THE FRANCHISE CONSEQUENCE — WHY A4/A5/A6 MATTER TOGETHER

Framework question 2 requires the **competitor row**: a moat is a relative claim and cannot be
asserted without naming who is being beaten. As of the FY2025 10-K, Alphabet's own filings supply
**no competitor names at all**. The analyst building the competitor row for GOOGL cannot build it
from the filings; the names must come from outside the rung-1 evidence base.

Three disclosure withdrawals, dated, all unexplained, all pointing at the same question:

| Withdrawn | Last filed | First omitted | Days | What it measured |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| TAC split (distribution partners vs Network) | 2019-10-29 | 2020-02-04 | **98** | **the price Google pays for default placement** |
| Named competitors in Item 1 | 2022-02-02 | 2023-02-03 | **366** | **who Google competes with** |
| Aggregate + Network click metrics | 2018-02-06 | 2018-04-24 | **77** | Network monetisation direction |

The two facts a franchise claim needs — *who are the rivals* and *what does the moat cost to
maintain* — have both been removed from Alphabet's periodic reports, without explanation, in the
years the monopolization case was pending. Neither is recoverable from any Alphabet filing.

## B.1 US v. GOOGLE (SEARCH), No. 1:20-cv-03010-APM (D.D.C.), Judge Amit P. Mehta

### B.1.1 LIABILITY — Memorandum Opinion, 2024-08-05

**[2] COURT DOCUMENT** — Mem. Op., **ECF No. 1033**, filed **2024-08-05**, 286 pp.; reported at
*United States v. Google LLC*, 747 F. Supp. 3d 1 (D.D.C. 2024).

**The holding, verbatim (Op. at 4):**

> "After having carefully considered and weighed the witness testimony and evidence, the court
> reaches the following conclusion: **Google is a monopolist, and it has acted as one to maintain
> its monopoly. It has violated Section 2 of the Sherman Act.**"

> "Specifically, the court holds that (1) there are relevant product markets for general search
> services and general search text ads; (2) Google has monopoly power in those markets; (3)
> **Google's distribution agreements are exclusive and have anticompetitive effects**; and (4)
> Google has not offered valid procompetitive justifications for those agreements. Importantly, the
> court also finds that **Google has exercised its monopoly power by charging supracompetitive
> prices for general search text ads. That conduct has allowed Google to earn monopoly profits.**"

**Formal conclusion (Op. at 276–277):** liability on **Counts I and III**; judgment for Google on
**Count II**.

**What Google WON, verbatim (Op. at 4):**

> "Other determinations favor Google. The court holds that (1) there is a product market for search
> advertising but that **Google lacks monopoly power in that market**; (2) **there is no product
> market for general search advertising**; and (3) **Google is not liable for its actions involving
> its advertising platform, SA360.** The court also **declines to sanction Google under Federal Rule
> of Civil Procedure 37(e)** for its failure to preserve its employees' chat messages."

**Conduct held exclusionary:** (i) browser-developer default agreements (Apple, Mozilla);
(ii) Android MADAs, "exclusive in practice"; (iii) Android RSAs with OEMs and carriers, which
"formalized the practical exclusivity of the MADAs."

**THE SPOLIATION AND PRIVILEGE FINDINGS — directly relevant to framework Q3 (are they honest?).**
Verbatim (Op. at 275–276):

> "On the request for sanctions, the court declines to impose them. **Not because Google's failure
> to preserve chat messages might not warrant them.** But because the sanctions Plaintiffs request
> do not move the needle on the court's assessment of Google's liability."
>
> "The court's decision not to sanction Google should not be understood as condoning Google's
> failure to preserve chat evidence. **Any company that puts the onus on its employees to identify
> and preserve relevant evidence does so at its own peril. Google avoided sanctions in this case.
> It may not be so lucky in the next one.**"

And on the "Communicate with Care" privilege program (Op. at 275):

> "the court is **taken aback by the lengths to which Google goes to avoid creating a paper trail for
> regulators and litigants**."

**This is a federal judge's finding on the record about the company's document practices.** It is
rung [2], it is verbatim, and **it appears nowhere in any Alphabet filing.**

### B.1.2 REMEDIES — **THERE ARE THREE DATES, NOT ONE**

This corrects the task brief's premise, and it reconciles exactly with Alphabet's rung-1
"December 2025" statement.

### B.1.3 CHROME DIVESTITURE — **DENIED**

**[2]** Rem. Op., ECF No. 1436, 2025-09-02, summary at 3–4, verbatim:

> "**Google will not be required to divest Chrome; nor will the court include a contingent
> divestiture of the Android operating system in the final judgment. Plaintiffs overreached in
> seeking forced divesture of these key assets, which Google did not use to effect any illegal
> restraints.**"

Reasoning (Rem. Op. at 114, 117–118), verbatim:

> "But the complete divestiture of Chrome is a poor fit for this case." … "**Plaintiffs have not
> shown that their behavioral remedies will be ineffective without the immediate divestiture of
> Chrome.**" … "**Put simply, Plaintiffs have not met their heavy burden to warrant the 'radical
> structural' remedy of a forced divestiture of Chrome and the Chromium open-source project.**"

The court expressly did **not** hold divestiture categorically barred — "the fact that neither
Google's ownership of Chrome nor its self-preferencing of Google Search in Chrome was found to be
anticompetitive is not a per se bar to this remedy." It failed on fit, causation, logistics and
product-degradation risk.

### B.1.4 ANDROID DIVESTITURE — **DENIED, and not held in reserve**

Plaintiffs sought a *contingent* divestiture triggered five years post-judgment. Rem. Op. at
118–119, verbatim:

> "The court does not dwell on this proposed remedy for long. It suffers from similar legal
> infirmities as the Chrome divestiture. **Plaintiffs have never alleged that Google's ownership or
> use of its Android operating system causes anticompetitive effects in the relevant markets, and
> they have not explained how a future sale of Android would promote competition in those markets.**
> A sale of Android also would reach beyond the U.S. market. The remedy therefore does not fit the
> wrong."
> "**There can be no remedy absent a factual basis to support it.**"

**No contingent divestiture appears in the Final Judgment.**

### B.1.5 THE PAYMENTS TO APPLE, MOZILLA AND CARRIERS — **PERMITTED, subject to conditions**

**This is the finding that matters most for the GOOGL run.** The default-placement payments were
**not banned**.

Rem. Op. at 4, verbatim:

> "**Google will not be barred from making payments or offering other consideration to distribution
> partners for preloading or placement of Google Search, Chrome, or its GenAI products.** Cutting
> off payments from Google almost certainly will impose substantial—in some cases, crippling—
> downstream harms to distribution partners, related markets, and consumers, which counsels against
> a broad payment ban."

Rem. Op. at 120–121: "Though the bases for a payment ban are sound, **the court declines to impose
such a remedy at this time.**" Two reasons: (1) crippling harm to OEMs, carriers and browser
developers — Mozilla testified that a lower revenue share could "potentially start a downward
spiral of usage… could at the end of the day put Firefox out of business"; (2) rivals would pay
less than Google does.

**And the court's own statement of the magnitude — verbatim, Rem. Op. at 120–121:**

> "**Freed of having to pay billions in revenue share, Google's profits would increase. Not paying
> Apple alone would result in a windfall worth tens of billions of dollars.**"

**That sentence is the rung-[2] corroboration of the magnitude reconstructed at
`B2-tac-and-metrics.md` §1.6b.** A federal judge, on the record, characterises the Apple payment
alone as "tens of billions of dollars." Alphabet has never filed the number.

**The operative conditions — Final Judgment, ECF No. 1462, § III, verbatim:**

- **§ III.A–D — no tying to Play.** "Google shall not condition the licensing of Google Play or any
  other Google application on the distribution, preload, placement, display, use, or license of the
  **Google Search Application** [§A] / the **Chrome Browser Application** [§B] / the **Google
  Assistant Application** [§C] / **any Google GenAI Product** [§D] on any device sold in the United
  States."
- **§ III.E–G — no anti-rival conditions.** Google may not condition Consideration or a Play licence
  on a partner "refraining from developing, distributing, preloading, placing, displaying, using,
  selling, or licensing any **Third-Party General Search Service** [§E] / **Third-Party Browser**
  [§F] / **Third-Party GenAI Product** [§G]."
- **§ III.H–J — no cross-conditioning** of payments across Search / Chrome / Assistant / GenAI.
- **§ III.K — THE ONE-YEAR TERM CAP.** "Google shall not enter or maintain any agreement requiring
  or conditioning Consideration on the distribution, preload, placement, display, use, license, or
  assignment of an access point for the Google Search Application, the Chrome Browser Application,
  the Google Assistant Application, or any Google GenAI Product in the United States **unless the
  agreement terminates no more than one year after the date it is entered.**"
- **§ III.L (browser developers) and § III.M (Apple, named).** No cross-conditioning of
  default-search or default-GenAI consideration across access points. Both add: "**Any agreement
  containing a default condition permitted by this provision must expire after one year and must
  expressly permit** [the Browser Developer / Apple] **to promote any Third-Party General Search
  Service and Third-Party GenAI Product.**" § III.M names "Safari, Siri, Spotlight, and any Privacy
  Mode within those products."
- **§ IX.C** defines "Browser Developer" to include "**Apple, Mozilla Corp., and Samsung Electronics
  Co., Ltd.**"

**Net effect: the payments survive; exclusivity, bundling, cross-conditioning and multi-year
lock-ups do not. Every US distribution contract becomes annually renegotiable.**

**The analytic consequence for Q4 and Q6.** Google keeps the ability to pay for defaults but loses
the ability to lock them up for more than twelve months. That converts a multi-year contractual
moat into an annual auction. Whether the clearing price of that auction rises or falls is
*precisely* what the withdrawn TAC-split line would have measured — and no Alphabet filing now
reports it.

### B.1.6 DATA SHARING — **ORDERED** (index and user-side; **no ads data**)

Rem. Op. at 4: "Google will have to make available to Qualified Competitors certain search index
and user-interaction data, **though not ads data**… The court, however, has **narrowed** the
datasets Google will be required to share."

**FJ § IV.A — Web Search Index**, within 30 days of a Qualified Competitor's certification, **at
marginal cost**: (1) per document, "a unique identifier (DocID) and another notation sufficient to
denote all the documents Google considers duplicates of each other"; (2) "a DocID to URL map";
(3) per DocID, "(A) time that the URL was first seen, (B) time that the URL was last crawled,
(C) spam score, and (D) device-type flag."

**FJ § IV.B — User-Side Data**, at marginal cost, for the term of the judgment: the data used to
build/operate the **GLUE** statistical models and to train/build/operate the **RankEmbed**
model(s). "Google shall make this data available to Qualified Competitors **at least twice**."
Express carve-out, verbatim: "**this Section IV.B shall not require disclosure of intellectual
property or trade secrets, such as algorithms, ranking signals, or post-trained LLMs.**"

### B.1.7 SYNDICATION — **ORDERED**, with a five-year taper

**FJ § V — Search syndication.** Licence term **five years**, on "financial terms no worse than
those offered to any other user of Google's search syndication products," via real-time APIs:
ranked organic desktop and mobile web results, user-facing query-rewriting features, and the
"**Local, Maps, Video, Images, and Knowledge Panel** Search Feature content."

**§ V.B.4, verbatim:** "Qualified Competitors' use of Google's syndication services in the first
year will be **capped at 40% of Qualified Competitors' annual U.S. queries** and decline over the
course of a **5-year period** with an expectation that Qualified Competitors will become independent
of Google over time through investment in their own search capabilities."

**FJ § VI — Search Text Ads.** § VI.A requires Google to report **all changes to its Search Text
Ads auction** to the Technical Committee and Plaintiffs. § VI.B orders a **Search Text Ads
Syndication Licence**, term **five years**, including "all types of Search Text Ads," with Qualified
Competitors included "in its **Search Partner Network**."

**"Qualified Competitor" — FJ § IX.V:** must meet Plaintiffs' approved data-security standards,
accept TC audits, show "a plan to invest and compete," and "**does not pose a risk to the national
security of the United States.**" **Annual re-certification required.**

## B.2 US v. GOOGLE (AD TECH), No. 1:23-cv-00108-LMB-JFA (E.D. Va.), Judge Leonie M. Brinkema

### B.2.1 LIABILITY — Memorandum Opinion, 2025-04-17

**[2] COURT DOCUMENT** — Mem. Op., **ECF No. 1410**, filed **2025-04-17**, 115 pp.

**Opening holding, verbatim (Op. at 1):**

> "With the benefit of a three-week bench trial and extensive post-trial filings, the Court finds
> that **Plaintiffs have failed to prove that there is a relevant market for open-web display
> advertiser ad networks**, but have proven that **Google has violated Section 2 of the Sherman Act
> by willfully acquiring and maintaining monopoly power in the open-web display publisher ad server
> market and the open-web display ad exchange market, and has unlawfully tied its publisher ad
> server (DFP) and ad exchange (AdX) in violation of Sections 1 and 2 of the Sherman Act.**"

**Conclusion, verbatim (Op. at 114–115):**

> "Plaintiffs have proven that Google has willfully engaged in a series of anticompetitive acts to
> acquire and maintain monopoly power in the publisher ad server and ad exchange markets for
> open-web display advertising. **For over a decade, Google has tied its publisher ad server and ad
> exchange together through contractual policies and technological integration**, which enabled the
> company to establish and protect its monopoly power in these two markets. Google further
> entrenched its monopoly power by imposing anticompetitive policies on its customers and
> eliminating desirable product features. In addition to depriving rivals of the ability to compete,
> **this exclusionary conduct substantially harmed Google's publisher customers, the competitive
> process, and, ultimately, consumers of information on the open web.** Accordingly, Google is
> liable under Sections 1 and 2 of the Sherman Act."

**Count disposition (Op. at 115):** **Count III** (advertiser ad network monopolization)
**DISMISSED**. Liability on **Count I** (publisher ad server), **Count II** (ad exchange), **Count
IV** (unlawful tying of AdX and DFP). Plaintiffs: the United States and **seventeen states**;
complaint filed 2023-01-24.

**Spoliation — the same finding, a second time, in a second court (Op. at 113–114), verbatim:**

> "**Google's systemic disregard of the evidentiary rules regarding spoliation of evidence and its
> misuse of the attorney-client privilege may well be sanctionable.** But because the Court has found
> Google liable… based on trial testimony and admitted evidence, including those Google documents
> that were preserved, it need not adopt an adverse inference or otherwise sanction Google for
> spoilation at this juncture. **As in Google Search, the Court's decision not to sanction 'should
> not be understood as condoning Google's failure to preserve chat evidence.'**"

**Two federal district judges, in two separate cases, have now made materially the same finding
about Google's document-preservation and privilege practices.** Neither appears in any Alphabet
filing. This is the strongest rung-[2] material bearing on framework Q3.

### B.2.2 REMEDIES — **DIVESTITURE OF AdX AND DFP: REJECTED, 2026-09-02**

**[2] COURT DOCUMENT** — Order, **ECF No. 1857**, filed **2026-09-02**, 2 pp. The entire operative
holding, verbatim:

> "For the reasons stated in the accompanying Memorandum Opinion, it is hereby
> **ORDERED that Plaintiffs' proposals for structural remedies in the form of the divestiture of
> AdX, the open-sourcing of DFP's final auction logic, and the contingent divestiture of DFP
> Remainder, be and are REJECTED**; and it is further
> **ORDERED that most of the parties' proposed behavioral remedies, as modified by this Court, be
> and are ACCEPTED.**"

**Neither AdX nor DFP (Google Ad Manager) will be divested. All three structural asks were

## B.5 THE READING FOR THE FRAMEWORK

**Google has now avoided structural relief in both US cases.** Chrome and Android divestiture
denied (D.D.C., 2025-09-02); AdX/DFP divestiture rejected (E.D. Va., 2026-09-02). The EC's stated
preference for divestment in adtech remains a preliminary view pending assessment of Google's
behavioural proposal. **The only live structural question in the US is narrow:** the government's
D.C. Circuit cross-appeal seeks a **payment ban**, not a breakup — and it is the single issue
presented.

**Q2 (is it a franchise?).** The court found the moat was, in material part, *purchased*:
"Google's distribution agreements are exclusive and have anticompetitive effects," and Google
"exercised its monopoly power by charging supracompetitive prices for general search text ads."
The remedy leaves Google free to keep buying the defaults but forbids locking them up for more than
a year. The franchise survives the judgment; what changes is that its rent must be re-bid annually,
against rivals who will hold Google's own index data and syndicated results. **The competitor row
cannot be built from Alphabet's filings (§A.6) — but the court has now supplied one:** the D.D.C.
named eleven Expressly Identified Competitors (ECF 1543, 2026-08-25) — **Anthropic, Brave, DeepSeek,
DuckDuckGo, Ecosia, Meta, Microsoft, OpenAI, Perplexity, Yahoo, SpaceXAI.** That list is rung [2],
not rung [1], and must be labelled as such wherever it is used.

**Q3 (are they honest, and are they rational?).** Two federal judges have found, in two separate
cases, that Google's document-preservation and privilege practices "may well be sanctionable," with
Mehta "taken aback by the lengths to which Google goes to avoid creating a paper trail for
regulators and litigants." Neither finding appears in any Alphabet filing. Set that alongside the
three unexplained disclosure withdrawals dated at §A.7. Nothing here is proof of intent; the
pattern is what it is, and it is on the record.

**Q6 (what would prove me wrong, and when do I sell?).** Three dated, observable triggers now
exist:
1. **The D.C. Circuit ruling on the payment-ban cross-appeal.** Briefing closes ~2026-09-29; oral
   argument unscheduled as of 2026-09-06. If a payment ban is reinstated on remand, the distribution
   economics change discontinuously — in Google's *favour* on cash flow (Mehta: "Google's profits
   would increase… a windfall worth tens of billions") and against it on query share.
2. **The first Qualified Competitor to access data-sharing and syndication.** Plaintiffs must give
   45 days' notice (ECF 1514); "most optimistically, by late fall or early winter" of 2026. That is
   when the five-year syndication taper clock starts.
3. **The unsealing of the E.D. Va. remedies opinion (~2026-09-16) and the joint proposed Final
   Judgment (~2026-10-02).** Until then the ad tech behavioural remedies are unknown.

**And the standing evidentiary problem, restated.** All three triggers act on the
distribution-partner TAC line — **which Alphabet stopped filing on 2019-10-29.** The question "did
the remedy raise or lower what Google pays for its defaults?" has no resolving document in
Alphabet's filings. It is **UNKNOWABLE from rung 1**, and can only be approached through rung-[2]
compliance filings, if any become public, or through the aggregate TAC rate — which moves on mix,
not on price.
