## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**
- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language.** A drug or device company that must test a product in people hires Medpace to run the trial: design it, find hospitals and doctors to run it, sign them up, recruit and track the patients, visit the sites to check the records, clean and analyse the data, run the laboratory tests, and write it up for the regulator. Medpace is paid a negotiated price per trial, mostly fixed-fee (*"The majority of our Phase I–IV contracts are fixed-fee contracts"*), and recognises it as its own costs are incurred (*"the input method of cost to cost"*), so its margin is the gap between the price it bid and the hours its people actually spend. It also passes through what it pays the investigators and sites, at no margin: FY2025 revenue **$2,530.2M**, of which *"Reimbursed out-of-pocket expenses | 1,037,488"* thousand, so **the service revenue it earns on is about $1,492.7M**. Against that it spent $732.1M on the people who do the work (direct service costs, 49.0% of service revenue), $197.6M on selling and administration and $28.1M on depreciation and amortization, and kept **$534.9M of operating income (35.8% of service revenue, 21.1% of total revenue)**. About 6,200 staff in 46 countries. **The customer pays ahead**: *"The majority of our contracts are structured with an upfront fee that is collected at the time of contract signing"*; advanced billings were $854.4M at 2025-12-31 against receivables and unbilled of $402.1M. The buyers are small drug developers funded by investors: *"82% and 13% of our net revenue was derived from small biopharmaceutical companies and mid-sized biopharmaceutical companies, respectively, which may have limited access to capital."* Plant is small (property $131.1M; capital spending $31.4M, *"1.24%"* of revenue).
- **The scarce input this business controls.** Trained clinical-operations and medical staff organised in one house method (therapeutic leads, trial managers, monitors, data managers, labs, imaging, Phase I clinic in Cincinnati) running on its own trial-management system (*"ClinTrak, our proprietary information management system for clinical trials"*), and the record with small sponsors that wins the next award. Whether that input is scarce against the other clinical research organisations, or merely better run, is Q2's question, not Q1's.
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** The trade will: a regulator will still require human trials and small sponsors will still hire someone to run them. The IPO prospectus of 2016 describes the same service, the same fixed-fee and upfront-fee structure, the same customer segment and the same named rivals as the FY2025 10-K. The filer names two live exposures in its own words: artificial intelligence (*"Our current competitors or other businesses might develop technologies or services that are more effective or commercially attractive than, or render obsolete, our current or future technologies and services"*) and the funding of its customers (*"if biopharmaceutical companies become less able to access capital in the future, they may commit less capital to our services"*).
- **Relatively simple and stable in character [E3-31]? A decision in five minutes [E4-46]?** Yes on the mechanism: hours of trained people sold per trial at a negotiated price, pass-through costs at cost, cash taken up front. Two features make the reported numbers estimate-dependent and are carried forward, not held against understanding: revenue on fixed-fee contracts rests on estimated cost to complete (*"it is possible that estimates may change in the near term, resulting in a material change in revenue reported"*), and backlog is cancellable (*"most of our customers for clinical trial services can terminate our contracts without cause upon 30 days’ notice"*). Neither is a technology forecast; the demand cycle (biotech funding) is a Q4 matter.
