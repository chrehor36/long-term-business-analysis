## GATE 1 — CIRCLE OF COMPETENCE
HP designs, manufactures, and sells personal computers, printers, and
related supplies/services across ~170 countries, organized into Personal
Systems and Printing segments. Straightforward hardware-plus-consumables
economics (sell devices, sell ink/toner, increasingly sell subscriptions —
"Instant Ink"). **VERDICT: PASS.**

## GATE 2 — MOAT
Franchise test [E3-03], from HP's own 10-K: **"We encounter strong
competition in all areas of our business activity. We compete on the basis
of technology, innovation, performance, price, quality, reliability, brand,
reputation, distribution, range of products and services... The markets for
each of our key business segments are characterized by strong competition
among major corporations with long-established..."** — HP's own words
describe a business competing on essentially every dimension simultaneously,
the opposite of a defined pricing-power franchise.
- **Needed/desired**: yes, generically (PCs/printers are needed), but not
  HP's product specifically.
- **No close substitute**: **FAILS**. Dell, Lenovo, Canon, Epson, Brother,
  and Apple (partial overlap) all produce functionally interchangeable
  hardware for most buyers; HP competes on price/spec sheets like any PC/
  printer OEM.
- **Unregulated**: largely yes, not the binding constraint here.
- **Proven by pricing power + returns on capital**: **FAILS**. PC and
  printer hardware is a low-margin, price-competitive category industry-
  wide; HP's own "Instant Ink" subscription model is a genuine attempt to
  build recurring, stickier revenue on top of a commodity hardware base,
  but this is a minority of segment revenue, not the core PC/printer sale.

**Class: NONE** on the core hardware business, with a small recurring-
revenue exception (ink/toner subscriptions) that does not change the
overall classification. **VERDICT: FAIL — STOP.**
