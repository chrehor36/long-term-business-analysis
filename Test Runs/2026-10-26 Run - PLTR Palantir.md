The routing is fixed: a business whose ten-year economics cannot be foreseen because its industry changes fast closes here, TOO HARD, since where "the future technology could hurt the business as it presently exists [...] it won't make it through the filter" **[M1998-008]**.

**The tests, applied.**
1. *Where will it be in ten years?* The filings show a business whose shape changed within five years: from 60% foreign revenue in 2019 to 80% U.S. in H1 2026; from operating losses to a 47% quarterly operating margin; from 24% growth in 2022 and 17% in 2023 to 93% in Q2 2026, the turn coming with a product (AIP) that did not exist before 2023. A ten-year picture would have to say what an LLM-driven "operating system" for institutions earns in 2036, and none can be drawn from these statements.
2. *The key variables and how predictable they are* **[M1998-044]**: (a) U.S. federal defense and intelligence software spending and Palantir's share of it, which the filer says depends on budgets that "may not be sustained at the levels associated with government fiscal year 2025", on a current administration that "has launched efforts to evaluate and reduce overall government spending", and on FASA, whose "change in or repeal [...] would adversely affect our competitive position"; (b) commercial adoption of AIP against the model makers whose LLMs it routes (AIP's own description names "commercially available LLMs") and the data platforms that sell into the same budgets; (c) the price of software seats and workloads in an industry where the underlying models change by the year. None of the three is predictable ten years out; (a) is a political variable and (b) and (c) are technology variables.
3. *Do the past statements tell the future ones?* **[M2008-033]** No: the 2021 to 2023 statements (operating losses, decelerating growth, a majority of revenue from the government segment, large foreign exposure) would have told a reader nothing about 2025 to 2026, and the filer's own explanation of the change is a product launched in 2023.
4. *Important and knowable?* The deciding variable, what an AI software platform earns over a decade against its rivals, is important and is not knowable from any filing: "If something's important but unknowable, forget it." **[M2006-076]**.
5. *Would the insiders write it down?* The row is about exactly this field: insiders "would not want to put down on paper their predictions about where 10 companies you would choose in the tech field would be in 10 years, in terms of their economics" **[M2000-105]**.
6. *Can I name the winner, not just the industry?* "there's industries we know that may have a wonderful future, but we don't have the faintest idea who the winners will be" **[M2012-067]**; dramatic growth ahead for an industry "does not mean we can judge what its profit margins and returns on capital will be as a host of competitors battle for supremacy" **[L2009-005]**. The competitors named in the filer's own risk factors include the largest technology companies in the world (see the facts after the stop); a buyer cannot say which will hold the margin.
7. *Customers or technology?* **[M2017-019]** Technology: Palantir's customers are institutions buying a technical capability, not consumers whose habits can be projected.
8. *How far off could I be?* **[M2011-084]** Very far: the price requires owner cash to grow about 45% a year for ten years to earn 10% (the computation below), and the filed record contains a year of 17% revenue growth only three years ago.
9. *Do I doubt it is inside?* Yes, and "if you have doubts about something being into your circle of competence, it isn't." **[M2002-092]**.
10. *Do I only think I understand it?* The product can be described; "what would bother me is if I think I understand a business and I don't" **[M1997-024]**. Knowing what Foundry does is not knowing its economics in 2036.

**Contrary evidence for IN, written down** **[M1997-127]**: the government franchise is old (software for the intelligence community since the company's founding in 2003, 10-Q 2026-06-30, Item 1), the filer describes "high installation costs, high failure risks, complexity of data environments, and the long sales cycles" as barriers (FY2025 10-K, Item 1), contracts closed in 2025 carry a dollar-weighted average duration of four years, top-20 customer revenue rose every year read (from $33.2M to $124M), and U.S. government revenue rose every year read. These show a customer base that stays and spends more. They do not show what the field will pay in ten years, which is the question asked.

**Which cause.** The deciding question is not one more filing can answer: the rows' own case is "a business that must deal with fast-moving technology is not going to lend itself to reliable evaluations of its long-term economics" and "We couldn't solve this problem, moreover, even if we were to spend years intensely studying those industries." **[L1993-023]**; "Our problem -- which we can't solve by studying up -- is that we have no insights into which participants in the tech field possess a truly durable competitive advantage." **[L1999-018]**. By the framework's test 5 the cause is NATURE. "It doesn't mean it isn't a good buy. [...] It just means that we don't know how to evaluate it." **[M2000-038]**.

## WHAT IN THE FRAMEWORK WAS WRONG OR UNCLEAR
(1) **Specific (b) gives a hyper-growth business a one-point range built on its loss years.** The window holds two loss years from the listing period, the halves rule finds a sign change, and (b) then sets the range to the no-growth case alone on a $134M average while trailing owner cash is $2.26B. The close does not depend on it (no case earns the floor even on trailing cash grown at the business's own sales growth), but the reporting figures ($4.61 and $4.16) say more about the window than about value; I reported them as the convention builds them and showed the trailing sensitivity beside, unranked. The text could say whether a sign change caused by listing-period stock pay makes an end year aberrational under (a). (2) **Specific (f)'s "never above the discount rate"** can be read as capping the substituted sales growth at the discount rate inside the ten years, against "inside the ten years the shown rate is the cap"; not reached here, but unclear. (3) **Whose tax** prices a carryforward that "runs out inside ten years"; it says nothing of a shelter that regenerates every year from stock-pay deductions, as this one does ($9.0B of NOLs, up $3.5B in a profitable year). I took the company's own tax and showed the statutory figure beside. (4) **The per-share count** is unstated: the framework requires dilution computed but does not say whether the range per share uses the cover count or a diluted count; with 7.5% of the count in deep in-the-money awards it matters, and I confessed a convention. (5) **Interest income on the all-equity basis** is not named: "owner cash before interest" plainly removes interest paid; I read it as removing interest earned too, since the cash is credited, and said so. (6) **The equity-only figure for a net-cash company** credits the cash only through its interest capitalized at ten percent, so it falls far below the all-equity figure ($0.97 against $4.16); the text could say whether that is intended.
