## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**
- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language.** Natural Grocers rents small stores (about 11,000 selling square feet, *"approximately 7,000 to 17,000"*), fills them with about 20,000 products that pass its own ingredient rules (organic-only produce, no artificial additives, pasture-raised dairy, about a quarter of the floor given to vitamins and supplements), buys most of that stock from one distributor (*"approximately 69% of our total purchases were from United Natural Foods Inc."*), and sells it at a markup. Of each sales dollar in fiscal 2025, **70.1 cents** went to the goods and the rent (*"Cost of goods sold and occupancy costs 70.1"*), **21.8 cents** to running the stores (mostly wages), **3.3 cents** to head office, leaving **4.7 cents** of operating income (10-K 2025, results table). The money is made by turning over the stock quickly on thin margins in stores whose rent is fixed: sales per store must rise faster than wages and rent for the margin to widen, and the filer says so (*"due to the fixed nature of certain of our costs (in particular, our rent obligations and related occupancy costs), our ability to leverage costs may be limited"*). Growth is by opening stores (*"target an annual new store unit growth rate of 4% to 5%"*), each financed mostly by leases and by suppliers' credit.
- **The scarce input this business controls.** Little that is scarce. The filer's own list is the product standard, the free nutrition coaching (one Nutritional Health Coach per store), the small-store format and a loyalty programme (*"approximately 2.7 million registered {N}power members as of September 30, 2025"*). None is protected: *"We do not own or license for use any patents, franchises or concessions that are material to our business"*, and the industry has *"few barriers to entry"*. The one thing a rival cannot copy quickly is a curated shelf with a sixty-seven-year reputation among customers who care about ingredient rules; whether customers treat that as having no close substitute is Q2's question.
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** Yes as a mechanism: in 2036 it will still be a grocer buying from distributors and selling at a markup in leased stores. The product rules have been stable since the founding (*"Our founders, Margaret and Philip Isely"*, first store 1958). What moves is the competition around it (conventional grocers, Sprouts, Whole Foods, Trader Joe's, Costco and online grocers all selling the same organic lines), which is a Q2 matter.
- **Relatively simple and stable in character [E3-31]? Yes.** A single-segment grocer with no acquisitions, one product category family, one distributor, one country; I can state how it makes money in under five minutes **[E4-46]**. The corpus names retailing as the business where you *"have to stay smart"* and *"cannot coast"* **[E3-74]**; that is a durability judgement for Q2, not a comprehension limit here.
- **VERDICT: [x] IN**: the money is made visibly, a markup on stock turned over in leased stores, measured line by line in the 10-K. **IN is not a finding on the franchise.**
