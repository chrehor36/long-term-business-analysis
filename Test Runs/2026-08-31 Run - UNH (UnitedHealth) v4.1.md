## Q1: CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**

- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language.** UNH does four things.
  **First and largest, it sells insurance.** It collects a fixed sum per person per month: from
  the federal government for the 8.4 million people in its Medicare Advantage plans and the 10.4
  million in its Part D plans, from thirty-two state governments for 7.4 million Medicaid members,
  and from employers and individuals for 8.2 million risk-based commercial members, and out of
  that sum it pays whatever medical care those people actually consume. In 2025 it collected **$352,229M of premiums and paid $313,995M of medical costs**, so
  **89.1 cents of every premium dollar went straight back out as care.** Another 13.3 cents of
  revenue went on running the company. What is left is the margin, and in 2025 there was almost
  none: **4.2 cents of operating income on every dollar of revenue, against 8.7 cents two years
  earlier.**
  **Second, it administers.** For 21.5 million more people whose employers carry the medical risk
  themselves, UNH processes claims and rents out its provider network for a fee. No claims risk,
  thin fee.
  **Third, it delivers and buys care.** Optum Health employs or affiliates physicians and clinics
  and takes capitated risk on 93 million people's care; Optum Rx runs $188 billion of drug spend
  through 64,000 retail pharmacies plus its own mail and specialty pharmacies, earning spread and
  fees on rebates and dispensing.
  **Fourth, it sells the resulting software and data** back to hospitals, other insurers, states
  and drug companies (Optum Insight, $19.4bn of revenue at a 13.5% margin, $31.1bn of backlog).
  The businesses feed each other: **$167,956M of 2025 revenue (38% of the consolidated total) 
  is intersegment and eliminated.** It employs no meaningful capital to do any of this:
  **$10,762M of property and capitalised software against $447,567M of revenue**, and capex of
  0.81% of revenue. What it employs instead is **$110,499M of goodwill** bought with $54bn of
  acquisitions in five years, and **$77,681M of debt**. Cash arrives about **47 days before** the
  claims are paid, so a $39.3bn float pool sits on the balance sheet; it earns $3.9bn of
  investment income, under 1% of revenue.
- **The scarce input this business controls: the ability to pay less for the same care than
  anyone else can, multiplied by the number of people whose care it directs.** Not the customer
  relationship, a commercial employer re-bids annually, a Medicare Advantage member can leave
  every autumn, a state re-tenders its Medicaid contract on a schedule. Not the product: the
  benefit design is largely prescribed. What is genuinely scarce is the contracted unit cost:
  UNH directs the care of 48.5 million people, and the discounts it negotiates from hospitals and
  physicians are a function of that volume, which no new entrant can assemble. The vertical
  extension of the same input is that a growing share of the care is bought from itself.
  **The evidence that the input is real is the competitor row at Q2: UNH's medical cost ratio has
  been the lowest of the four full-line managed-care companies for years, and was again in the
  worst year of the cycle.**
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** **The mechanism yes; the
  parameters no, and the filing says so.** Americans will still get sick, someone will still pool
  the risk, and the government will still buy most of it. UNH's format has been recognisably the
  same since the 1980s and its revenue has grown every year without exception. What will not look
  the same are the terms: within the last three years alone the Medicare Advantage risk-adjustment
  model was rewritten (v28), the Inflation Reduction Act redesigned Part D and shifted risk onto
  plans, Medicaid eligibility redeterminations removed millions of members, the No Surprises Act
  changed out-of-network provider economics, and CMS's RADV audit programme was extended. Each of
  those was a legislative or regulatory act, not a business decision, and each moved UNH's
  economics by billions. **That is recorded here and it is the substance of Q2.**
- **[E4-46] check, and it is the closest call in this run's Q1.** *"if we can't make a decision
  in five minutes, we can't make it in five months."* The case for saying UNH is outside the
  circle is real and it should be stated: this is a $448bn, four-segment, 400,000-person company
  whose reported segment margins depend on internal transfer prices covering 38% of gross revenue;
  whose earnings are an actuarial estimate where a **1% error in medical costs payable moves net
  earnings by $300M**; and whose own management, holding every internal number, **affirmed a
  $38.5bn operating-income plan on 16 January 2025 and delivered $18.96bn.**
  **The ruling, and the reason.** [E4-46]'s test is whether the *decision* needs months of study,
  not whether the *business* is large. It does not. The mechanism above was written from one
  10-K, one 10-Q and one proxy; the filings are legible; the segment disclosure is granular; the
  reserve sensitivity is published in two tables. **What UNH's complexity actually destroys is not
  my ability to describe how it makes money: it is my ability to estimate what it will earn,
  which is [E5-34]'s separate test and lives at Q5.** Ruling Q1 IN is therefore **not** a claim
  that the earnings are forecastable, and no later gate is permitted to borrow that claim.

## Q2: IS IT A FRANCHISE? **[E3-03]**

**The question, posed plainly: is UnitedHealth a franchise, or is it the largest and best-run
operator inside a regime that sets the industry's price?** The answer this run reaches is the
second, and the decisive evidence is not the 2025 collapse (one year proves nothing) but the
**competitor row**, which shows five companies with utterly different business models losing
290 to 590 basis points of medical cost ratio in the same twenty-four months. Class **NONE** in
[E3-03]'s sense; **NARROW and NARROWING** as a cost position. Worked below.

**[E3-03], the three criteria:**

- **Needed or desired: [x] YES, unambiguously.** Health coverage is non-discretionary, and 44%
  of consolidated revenue comes from a customer (CMS) legally obliged to buy it.

- **No close substitute: [ ] FAILS, and the customers proved it inside twelve months.**
  A Medicare Advantage member can switch plan or return to Original Medicare every autumn at zero
  cost; **785,000 of them left UNH in the year to June 2026, a 9% decline**, while Elevance's
  Medicare Advantage membership **grew 7.9%**. A commercial employer re-bids annually; UNH's
  risk-based commercial membership fell 785,000 (−9%). A state re-tenders on a schedule; UNH's
  Medicaid membership fell 710,000 (−9%) *"due to the exit from one state and reduced Medicaid
  eligibility."* **Total medical membership went 49,760k → 48,525k in twelve months.** UNH's own
  Item 1 concedes the shape of the market: *"we operate in **highly competitive markets across
  the full expanse** of health care benefits and services. Our competitors include organizations
  ranging from startups to highly sophisticated Fortune 50 global enterprises … **New entrants**
  to our markets and business combinations among our competitors and suppliers also contribute to
  a dynamic and competitive environment."*

- **Not subject to price regulation: [ ] FAILS OUTRIGHT, on the majority of revenue, in the
  company's own words.** This is the criterion the operator flagged as live, and it is not close:
  - *"**Premium revenues from CMS represented 44% of UnitedHealth Group's total consolidated
    revenues** for the year ended December 31, 2025."*
  - *"**premium rates for our health insurance and managed care products are subject to
    regulatory review or approval in many states and by the federal government.** Additionally,
    we must submit data on proposed rate increases to HHS on many of our products."*
  - *"Fully insured commercial products of U.S. health plans, **Medicare Advantage and Medicare
    Prescription Drug Benefit (Medicare Part D) plans with medical loss ratios (MLRs) … falling
    below certain targets are required to rebate ratable portions of their premiums annually.**
    … Certain of the Company's Medicaid business is also subject to state minimum MLR rebates."*
  - *"States … **select health plans by using a formal bid process**"*; and under Part D, *"**our
    bids must result in an enrollee premium below a regional benchmark**."*
  **So the price is reviewed or set by the payer, and the margin retained above it is capped by
  statute with mandatory rebates. That is not a franchise cap; it is the whole pricing
  mechanism.** UNH went further voluntarily in 2026: *"we have **voluntarily pledged to rebate
  2026 profits on our individual exchange products** to customers as policymakers continue to
  work to determine how to improve affordability."*

**Score: one of three. [E3-03] is not met.**

### [E2-59]: the governing ledger row, and it fits this business exactly

> administered pricing (regulation, cartel) can floor a commodity business's profits — pre-1970s
> insurers *"could legally price their way to profitability even in the face of substantial
> over-capacity"* — but **the moat belongs to the regime**, and *"That day is gone"* is how it
> ends. — **[E2-59]**

Medicare Advantage is the clearest administered-price regime in the modern American market. From
roughly 2014 to 2023 the benchmark rates and the risk-adjustment model together produced margins
that made the whole industry look like a franchise. Then the regime changed, and UNH's own filing
describes the change without euphemism:

> *"**Medicare Advantage rate notices for numerous years have resulted in industry base rates well
> below the industry forward medical cost trend.** While the Final Notice for 2026 approached the
> expected industry forward medical cost trend, **the Advanced Notice for 2027 is far below.**
> Additionally, increased medical costs in 2025 … have added to the compounding impact of the
> previous multi-year rate shortfalls creating **sustained pressure on the Medicare Advantage
> program.** Further, **substantial revisions to the risk adjustment model** … have resulted and
> will continue to result in **reduced funding**"* — FY2025 10-K

And the Q2 2026 10-Q, four months later: *"the Final Notice for 2027 moved towards the expected
industry forward medical cost trend, **it remains below**."*

**[E2-59]'s test is whether the profits belong to the position or to the regime. The competitor
row answers it.**

### The method, stated explicitly and defended, because the convention does not fit

The framework's convention is a multi-year mean of (operating cash flow − share-based
compensation) − (c). Two departures are required and both are argued from the corpus.

**1. (c) = total capital expenditure, and the D&A end of the band is REFUSED.** UNH is squarely
the [E2-41] 95% class, not the [E5-20] capital-intensive exception: net property and capitalised
software is $10,762M against $447,567M of revenue, and capex is 0.81% of revenue. But **$1.7bn of
the $4,361M D&A line is acquired-intangible amortisation that renews nothing** (Note 6). Cash
depreciation excluding it is roughly $2.7bn: **below** capex of $3,622M. Using D&A as the proxy
for (c) would import a charge that buys no capacity and would flatter the series as the
acquisition amortisation runs off. **(c) = capex, a disclosed judgment, and it is the
conservative end.**

**2. Float growth is stripped, and the corpus scopes why.** [E2-61] rules cash-based scoring
invalid for a float business and [E3-69] makes float a *source of funds*, not earnings. UNH's
operating cash flow is inflated every year by growth in the claims-payable pool: **money the
company holds because claims have not yet been paid is borrowed, not earned**, and it reverses if
membership contracts, which it is now doing. The two float lines isolable from the filed
cash-flow statement, **medical costs payable** and **unearned revenues**, are stripped.
**What cannot be isolated is disclosed rather than guessed:** "accounts payable and other
liabilities" (+$1,665M in 2025) contains customer funds, rebates payable and CMS-related
accruals that are also float-like, and "other current receivables" (−$3,608M in 2025) contains
CMS risk-adjustment and Part D receivables running the other way. **Neither is separable from the
filed lines. The net of the two would reduce owner earnings further in most years, so the
undisclosed error runs in the conservative direction.**

**3. The corpus's own sector number is computed and reported, and it says something surprising.**
[E3-69]: cost of float is the insurer's one number. UnitedHealthcare's earnings from operations
were $16,415M (2023), $15,584M (2024) and $9,425M (2025) against a claims-payable pool of
$32,395M, $34,224M and $39,337M. **UNH's cost of float is deeply negative: it is paid 24% to 48%
a year to hold it.** But the reading is the inverse of Berkshire's: **UNH does not earn its
return by investing float.** Investment and other income was $3,920M of $447,567M of revenue:
0.88%, and 13% to 21% of pre-tax income. **The float is a 47-day claim lag on a $314bn cost
base, not the business model. So the sector method governs the liquidity read and does not govern
the valuation.** Stating that is the point of running the test.
