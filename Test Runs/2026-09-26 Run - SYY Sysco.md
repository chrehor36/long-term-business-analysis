## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**
- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language.** Sysco buys food and kitchen supplies by the truckload from thousands of manufacturers, packers and growers (none over 10% of purchases), stores them in refrigerated, frozen and dry warehouses, and delivers them in its own trucks, usually within 24 hours of the order, to restaurants (59% of FY2026 sales), schools, hospitals, hotels and caterers. It earns the gap between what it pays (after the suppliers' rebates and marketing payments, which the 10-K calls vendor consideration) and what it charges: **gross margin 18.5% of sales in FY2026**. Out of that it pays for the warehouses, the drivers and trucks, the fuel and the salespeople who call on each restaurant, leaving **operating margin 3.7%** ($3,095M on $84,553M). The money is in the mix: an independent restaurant ordering a few cases a week pays more per case than a national chain whose contract is bid, and Sysco's own-brand products carry a higher margin than national brands. So U.S. Foodservice (broadline and specialty: meat, produce, Italian, equipment) earned **6.0%** operating margin, SYGMA (which delivers to quick-service chains) **1.1%**, International **2.9%**. Sales move with case volume and food-cost inflation (*"Our sales are driven by changes in case volumes and product inflation"*); FY2026 had 3.0% inflation and 1.4% U.S. case growth.
- **The scarce input this business controls.** **Route density and purchasing scale**: about 18% of a $377bn U.S. market by the registrant's own estimate, the largest single foodservice distributor in the U.S. and Canada, a network of multi-temperature warehouses, a sales force calling on independents, and a consolidated buying programme with own brands. **Not** a licence, a resource or a patent. Whether density and scale are scarce enough to be a franchise is Q2's question.
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** **Yes, on the filings.** The business is the one formed in 1969 when nine distributors merged; the FY2009 and FY2026 10-Ks describe the same customers, the same product categories and the same delivery model. **The one change of character in view is the Jetro Restaurant Depot purchase**: 167 cash-and-carry warehouse stores where the restaurant owner drives in, shops and pays, a retail format rather than a delivery one. It is understandable in the same terms (buy by the truckload, sell by the case, keep costs low), and the release says it will *"operate as a standalone business segment"*. It would be roughly 16% of combined revenue and, on the release's own figures, about a third of combined EBITDA. It does not make the business harder to understand; it changes what Q2 and Q4 are judging.
- **Relatively simple and stable in character [E3-31]?** Simple: a wholesaler that buys, stores and delivers food, paid a thin margin for doing it reliably. Stable in character; not a business that turns on technology. **Not [E4-46]'s months-of-study case.**
- **VERDICT: [x] IN**: the money is made visibly, as a thin spread on food bought by the truckload and delivered by the case, earned mainly from independent restaurants. **IN is not a finding on the franchise.**
