## Q2 — IS IT A FRANCHISE? **[E3-03]**

*Every Where Food Comes From figure below is read from its filed 10-Ks for fiscal 2008 to 2025 (accessions in Step 0 and `getdocs.py`), its 2026 10-Qs and its 8-K exhibits, or from SEC companyfacts checked against those statements. Peer figures are each nonprofit competitor's own Form 990 as served by the ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer API (the filed return, transcribed). Scripts and outputs, all in `Test Runs/_research 2026-09-27 WFCF/`: `series.py` (the income-statement lines of every 10-K), `xb.py` (`xb_out.txt`), `pricing_hits.txt` (every sentence in every downloaded filing that mentions pricing, price increases, discounts or competitive pricing), `riskfactors.txt`, `peers/row.py` (the JKHY construction, unchanged; `peers/WFCF_row.txt`), `peers/pp.py` and `peers/org.py` (`peers/pp_<EIN>.json`), `peers/summary.py` (`peers/summary_out.txt`).*

**The hypotheses I formed while reading, both stated so they can be attacked [E4-26].** *(a) The largest independent verifier of on-farm label claims in North American beef, with more than 50 standards under one roof, its own label and its own standards (Where Food Comes From Source Verified, CARE Certified, RaiseWell, adopted by Whole Foods Market in 2026), the designated certifier of at least one branded beef program, a customer book of 17,500, and pre-tax returns on its tangible operating capital of 41-79% in every year 2019-2025; customers who have enrolled their herds and records keep coming back, and a buyer who needs one verifier for all proteins has few alternatives.* *(b) A labour business that sells inspection days in a market with eight named competitors, several of them nonprofit certifiers who price at cost, whose own filings say competition is "growing more intense" in every year for a decade, whose customers are "very sensitive to costs", whose margins have fallen for five years, whose management says the moat is the breadth of the portfolio (which is bought and rebuilt, not owned), and whose filer names two people as its competitive advantage; the high return on tangible capital is what any business whose assets are people shows, and says nothing about price.* The file does not get to pick. Each is tested on the filings below.

**Where the money is (10-K 2025, income statement, thousands).** *"Verification and certification service revenue | | $ | 20,102"*, *"Product sales | | | 3,616"*, *"Professional services | | | 1,174"*; *"Total revenues | | | 24,892"*. One reportable segment. **No split between beef and non-beef is filed**; on the Q2 2026 call a holder asked for it (*"if you'd ever consider breaking out the non-beef certification business from the beef"*) and was not given it.

**[E3-03], criterion by criterion.** *"An economic franchise arises from a product or service that: (1) is needed or desired; (2) is thought by its customers to have no close substitute and; (3) is not subject to price regulation."*
- **(1) Needed or desired [x]: passes, conditionally.** A verified claim is desired wherever the buyer pays a premium for it and required where a rule demands third-party proof (the EU high-quality beef quota *"includes a restriction on hormones and must be third party verified"*; USDA organic; the 2024 animal disease traceability rule). The filer is plain that the desire is conditional: *"Because of the tight cattle supply, ranchers are receiving the highest cattle prices per head in a decade without verification; and beef packers are reluctant to incur additional costs of verification to their supply chain because of the high costs of acquiring cattle for slaughter."* A premium that disappears when the commodity is scarce is a desire that rises and falls with the commodity cycle. And one rule change has already removed a premium the company lived on: *"In January 2013, the Japanese government announced a change to its import requirements on U.S. beef ... it negatively impacted the premiums typically seen in the marketplace for source and age verified cattle."* Where the need is created by a rule, the demand belongs to the regime **[E2-59]**, and any accredited verifier can meet it.
- **(3) Not subject to price regulation [x]: passes.** No regulator sets an audit fee. Standard owners and accreditors approve verifiers; they do not price them.
- **(2) Thought by its customers to have no close substitute: FAILS in the filer's own words, in every 10-K for a decade.**
  - *"The competition for third-party verification services in the food and agriculture industry is growing more intense, especially within the organic market."* (the same sentence in every 10-K from fiscal 2015 to fiscal 2025, eleven in a row, and in none before; `competition_sweep.txt`). Earlier 10-Ks named the rivals differently and said how the company differentiated itself: fiscal 2010, *"Our key competitors for our SupplyVerified Program are: AgInfolink, MicroBeef Technologies, and Sterling Solutions"* (the company bought Sterling Solutions in 2014); fiscal 2014, *"We believe we differentiate ourselves in the marketplace by offering a cost effective bundled service package"*; *"we estimate there are approximately eight key competitors serving the food and agricultural industry, including Quality Assurance International, California Certified Organic Farmers, Oregon Tilth, Organic Crop Improvement Association, Earth Claims, FoodChain ID, NSF International, SGS and SCS Global Services"* (seven named in fiscal 2015, nine names under "eight" since fiscal 2016).
  - Risk factors: *"We operate in a competitive industry with a limited market characterized by changing technology, frequent introductions of new service offerings, service enhancements, and evolving industry standards."* *"Our competitors may have significantly more financial, technical and marketing resources than we do. Competition is likely to intensify ... Increasing competition may result in reduced margins and the loss of market share."* And of the customers: *"our customers for these products historically have been very sensitive to costs and reluctant to adopt new technologies and business practices."*
  - The contract form: a customer is *"in effect, provided a pricing list for animal audit services, and pricing is effective over a one-year period"* and *"enforceable rights and obligations do not arise until a customer actually engages us to perform an audit service"* (10-K 2018, revenue note). **Every audit is a fresh purchase decision against a yearly price list**; there is no multi-year lock and no filed retention figure (the filings say *"high retention rates"*, a claim, not a number).
  - The product line: ear tags sold into *"market conditions that foster a competitive environment for selling cattle ear tags"* (10-K 2025; 10-Q Q3 2025; 10-Q Q1 2026), after *"competitive pricing offered to dairy calf ranches for significantly higher purchase volumes of cattle identification ear tags"* (10-K 2019).
  - **Several of the named competitors are nonprofit certifiers** (California Certified Organic Farmers, Oregon Tilth, the Organic Crop Improvement Association, NSF International; their Form 990s are in the row below). An accredited organic certifier certifies to the same USDA standard whoever owns it; a nonprofit prices to cover its costs. A for-profit selling the same certificate against them cannot be the one customers think has no substitute.

- **Returns, read against [E3-46]:** *"the best businesses, by definition, are going to be businesses that earn very high returns on capital employed over time"*: **41-79% pre-tax on tangible operating capital in every year 2019-2025; 15-31% with every acquisition's goodwill and intangibles counted; 1-10% in 2016-2018.** The tangible base is $2-4M because the company's assets are its auditors, who are not on the balance sheet. **The operating margin is where the price shows, and it has never exceeded 10.7% since 2011** (0.5% in 2013 and 2017).
- **Pricing, read against [E2-44]'s first characteristic** (*"an ability to increase prices rather easily (even when product demand is flat and capacity is not fully utilized) without fear of significant loss of either market share or unit volume"*): **not shown anywhere in the filings read.** The sweep of every downloaded filing (`pricing_hits.txt`, 38 distinct passages) finds **no sentence in eighteen 10-Ks (fiscal 2008-2025), the 10-Qs or the releases since 2024 attributing verification revenue to a fee increase.** The one filed price rise is on ear tags in 2022, *"primarily due to increased pricing reflective of the market, and limited supply elsewhere in the market"*: **tight supply, which is [E3-43]'s "a business" route by name, and it reversed within two years.** The direction since runs the other way: tag makers' *"inflationary price increases passed on to us"* that the company could not pass on (10-K 2025); and bundling sold to customers as a discount: *"a one-stop-shop approach that simplifies their vendor structure and affords them favorable pricing through our bundling program"* (Q2 2024 call, 8-K EX-99.2, 2024-08-12); *"this provides our customers with cost and time savings"* (Q2 2026 call). **In 2025, with demand flat, verification gross margin fell to 39.2% from 48.0% five years earlier, while the verification line grew 44% from 2020 to 2024.** Volume was bought with margin. Munger's inverse metric reads the same: *"it's not a great business when you have to have a prayer session before you raise your prices a penny"* **[E4-37]**. The second characteristic (*"only minor additional investment of capital"*) is met on the balance sheet and not in the economics: growth is added auditors, whose cost rises with the revenue they bring.
- **The low-cost door and tight supply, the two routes [E3-43] says "a business" uses:** the low-cost door is not claimed and not shown (*"Our competitors may have significantly more financial, technical and marketing resources than we do"*; the operating margin sits inside the nonprofits' band in the row below). Tight supply occurred once, in tags, in 2022, and passed. **The class is [E3-43]'s "a business", which "unceasingly faces the possibility of competitive attack".**

**The key-person defect, recorded at Q2 as [E4-23] requires.** The filer locates its advantage in two people: *"Our future success depends to a significant degree upon the continued service of key senior management personnel, in particular, John and Leann Saunders. Both John and Leann Saunders' reputation and prominence in the field provide us with a strong competitive advantage."* *"if a business requires a superstar to produce great results, the business itself cannot be deemed great ... The partnership's moat will go when the surgeon goes"* **[E4-23]**. This is recorded as a moat defect, not as a compliment to the founders, and it points the same way as everything above.

**[E4-04], the moat management names.** On the Q2 2026 call: *"The size and scope of our portfolio is the cornerstone of the moat we have built for our business."* The portfolio is built by *"a combination of M&A transactions and internal development"*, *"over the past 14 years we've averaged one acquisition per year"*, and by new standards as buyers' fashions change (source-and-age verification for Japan, then non-hormone-treated cattle for the EU quota, then CARE, then RaiseWell). **A portfolio whose members lose their premium when a trade rule or a buyer's preference changes, and which is refilled by buying and launching new standards, is a moat whose basis is periodically replaced**: the kind [E4-04] rules out (*"A moat that must be continuously rebuilt will eventually be no moat at all"*), as distinct from defending one trademark **[E5-23, E3-49]**. This is supporting evidence, not the ground of the verdict, and it does not take the perimeter form (the durability of a labour business is not beyond judgment; the filings judge it).

**The attacker's test [E2-45]** (*"how I would like, assuming I had ample capital and skilled personnel, to compete with it"*): an entrant needs accreditation from the standard owners and experienced auditors. Both are for sale: the company itself built its breadth by buying certifiers (International Certification Services, Validus, Sterling Solutions (a named competitor in the fiscal 2010 10-K), SureHarvest, JVF Consulting and Postelsia, per the 10-Ks) and hiring their staff, and nonprofits already sell the same certificates at cost. **With capital and skilled people, I would compete with it without fear.**

- **Peers named: 5 of the filer's 9 named competitors measured (the filer itself and four nonprofits); five unmeasured, named:** Quality Assurance International (not separately filed; commonly described as part of NSF, which this run did not verify from a filing), Earth Claims, FoodChain ID and SCS Global Services (private; no filing on any rung), and SGS (listed on the SIX Swiss Exchange, reporting in Swiss francs through divisions in which food and agriculture sit inside a larger testing business; rung 3 of the ladder, the company IR site, was not pulled because no filed SGS figure isolates the line Where Food Comes From sells). **The row is partial and says so.** It is not needed to carry the verdict, which rests on the filer's own words and figures; it is shown because the framework requires the relative view, and what it shows points the same way.
- **The surplus margins are not operating margins**: a 990's total revenue includes contributions, grants and investment income (California Certified Organic Farmers' 2021 surplus of 39.4% is the sort of year a grant or a pandemic loan forgiveness produces; not traced). Read with that caveat, **Where Food Comes From's best operating margins (10.5-10.7%) sit inside the range nonprofit certifiers produce by pricing to cover cost**, and its revenue growth after its last acquisitions (7.1% a year, 2018-2023) sits beside theirs (5.9-10.3%). A franchise would show a margin its competitors cannot reach; this row shows a for-profit earning about what a nonprofit's surplus looks like.
- **The row's limit [E3-61]:** it shows position, not conduct. It cannot show whether the nonprofits would follow a price rise; the filer's own sentences (above) say its customers are cost-sensitive and its competitors many.
- **Untapped pricing power [E3-33]:** none claimed and none visible. Claiming the class is claiming *"a monopoly or a near monopoly"* **[E5-28]**; the filer names eight competitors and describes its bundles as discounts.
- **Direction [E4-32]: NARROWING.** Verification gross margin 48.0% (2020) to 39.2% (2025), 39.7% in H1 2026 against 40.3% a year earlier; operating margin 10.7% (2023) to 4.8% (2025); the first half of 2026's 8.0% is flattered by the returned 2025 bonuses (*"reflecting management's first quarter decision to return approximately $400,000 in 2025 bonus compensation"*). **Units [E4-55]:** no volume series is filed (no head counts, audit counts or customer counts over time). The customer claim reads *"more than 10,000"* (fiscal 2015), 12,000 (2016), 15,000 (2017-2020), and *"more than approximately 17,500"* unchanged in five consecutive 10-Ks (2021-2025): the steps coincide with acquisitions, and the plateau is a repeated estimate, not a count. The one unit figure is new: RaiseWell *"more than 270,000 head of cattle now enrolled"* (Q2 2026 release).

**THE STRONGEST EVIDENCE AGAINST THIS VERDICT, hunted hardest [E4-26, E3-41, E4-51].**
1. **The returns on tangible capital are high (41-79%) and have been for seven years.** Answer: [E3-03] ties the return to pricing (*"demonstrated by a company's ability to regularly price ... aggressively and thereby to earn high rates of return"*). These returns come from a tiny tangible base, not from price; the margin that carries the price signal has never passed 10.7% since 2011 and is falling. A staffing firm shows the same ratio.
2. **Verification revenue nearly doubled, $10.4M (2016) to $20.6M (2024), and kept growing through a cattle herd contraction.** Answer: partly acquired (SureHarvest at the end of 2016, its remaining 40% in 2019, Validus's remaining 40% in 2016), and the growth after 2020 came with a nine-point fall in the line's gross margin. Growth with a falling margin is the opposite of the pricing test.
3. **The company owns standards now, not only audits to other people's.** RaiseWell was adopted by Whole Foods Market *"as an approved animal welfare certification for its beef supply"*, and a standard owner can control who certifies to it. This is the one place a franchise could form: a proprietary standard a large retailer requires. Answer: it is young (the Q2 2026 call dates the adoption to *"the first quarter of this year"*), measured in enrolled head (270,000) with no filed revenue, and the release says Whole Foods adopted it as *"an"* approved certification, one among others. **It is the thing to watch, not a finding** (see the reversal condition).
4. **"Where Food Comes From is the primary certifier for Progressive Beef."** Answer: the company sold its 10% stake in Progressive Beef back to its owner in 2025; the filings do not say the certification contract is exclusive or long-dated.
5. **Customers' records sit in the company's systems, so switching has a cost.** Answer: no filed retention figure; contracts are yearly price lists with per-audit engagement; the organic certificate a customer holds is portable between accredited certifiers by the USDA's own design (general knowledge, not relied on for the verdict; the verdict stands on the filer's sentences quoted above).

None of the five turns the pricing evidence. The reading this run favoured, (b), survives its own hardest test; reading (a)'s one live point is item 3, and it is carried to the reversal condition.

- **Must the moat be continuously rebuilt? Does success depend on a great manager? [E4-04]** Yes and yes, by the filer's own account: the portfolio moat is refilled by acquisition and new standards, and the advantage is placed in two named founders **[E4-23]**.
- **Class: [ ] WIDE [ ] NARROW [x] NONE [ ] PROVISIONAL · Direction: narrowing.** The row is partial (5 of 9 named competitors) and would ordinarily hold a class PROVISIONAL; it does not here, because the verdict rests on the filer's own evidence, which is complete, and the measured part of the row agrees with it.
- **VERDICT: [ ] IN  [x] OUT  [ ] UNRESEARCHED  [ ] UNKNOWABLE**: on the business. **[E3-03] criterion (2) fails in the registrant's own filings**: *"approximately eight key competitors"*, *"growing more intense"* in every 10-K for eleven years, competitors with *"significantly more financial, technical and marketing resources"*, customers *"very sensitive to costs"*, a yearly price list with every audit a separate purchase, nonprofit certifiers selling the same certificate, and bundles sold as *"favorable pricing"*. **[E3-43]'s demonstration is absent in its pricing half**: no filed fee increase in eighteen annual reports, a verification gross margin down nine points in five years while the line grew, an operating margin never above 10.7% since 2011, and the one price rise on record produced by tight supply in ear tags, which is [E3-43]'s "a business" route by name. **The high return on tangible capital is the arithmetic of a labour business, not the fruit of pricing.** The filer places its advantage in two people **[E4-23]** and its management places its moat in a portfolio rebuilt by acquisition **[E4-04]**. **The file closes here.** Q3 to Q6 are not opened as gates; the material gathered beneath the close follows without verdicts.

**THE REVERSAL CONDITION, in words (a Q2 OUT arms no price band; the QLYS ruling of 2026-09-07).** Q2 would be re-opened only on filed evidence of price-setting, not on a lower price: (1) a proprietary standard (RaiseWell or CARE) required by a major retailer or packer for its whole supply, with Where Food Comes From the sole or controlling certifier, shown in filed revenue and enrolled head growing over at least three years; **and** (2) verification gross margin back above its 2020-2021 level (about 48%) on flat or falling cattle volume, with a filed fee increase held without customer loss; **and** (3) a filed retention or renewal figure. Any one alone would not re-open the file; the three together would be [E3-43]'s demonstration.
