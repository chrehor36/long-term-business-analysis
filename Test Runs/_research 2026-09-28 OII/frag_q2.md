### THE STRONGEST EVIDENCE AGAINST THIS VERDICT, stated as its holders would state it [E4-51, E4-26, E3-47]
The verdict below is OUT, so the case for IN is set out first, at full strength.
1. *"This is a near-monopoly and it prices. Sixty percent of the contracted floating rigs carry an Oceaneering robot (59% in 2014, 63% in 2019, 60% in 2025). Revenue per ROV day has risen from $7,344 (2020) to $11,187 (2025), 52%, while utilization stayed between 58% and 68%, which is [E2-44]'s own test: prices raised 'even when product demand is flat and capacity is not fully utilized'. The 2025 10-K credits 'the realization of improved pricing in energy markets', and the June 2026 quarter again 'higher average revenue per day in 2026, reflecting a mix of improved pricing'."* **Answer:** accepted as the fact of the last five years, and it is the best fact in the file. But the series has a second half. From FY2014 to FY2020 the same fleet, at the same share, gave up 32% of its price per day ($10,875 → $7,344) and its whole operating margin (30% → 0% in FY2018 and FY2019, −13% in FY2020); a near-monopoly [E5-28] does not do that. And the filer said, at the top of the cycle, that the share was not pricing power: FY2014, *"Although demand for ROVs has been increasing, our margins have not increased in recent periods due to increasing competition, increasing costs and pricing pressure."* FY2015 to FY2018, in the same words each year: *"our margins have decreased in recent periods due to lower utilization and pricing pressure, as price has become a more important factor"*. Over the whole cycle, FY2014 to FY2025, the price per robot-day rose 2.9% in nominal dollars in eleven years, which is a fall in real terms [E4-47]. The 2021-2025 rise is the recovery [E2-58] describes: *"over-capacity may eventually self-correct, either as capacity shrinks or demand expands"*, and capacity shrank: Oceaneering's own available ROV days fell from 121,944 to 91,250 (−25%), and the 10-K counts *"186 floating drilling rigs in operation or available for work"* at the end of 2025 against 263 at the end of 2017. **And two recent years do meet [E2-44]'s test in its mild form, stated here because it is the part of this item the answer does not dispose of:** in FY2024 the price per day rose 12.6% while days utilized fell 0.8% (61,874 → 61,382) and utilization 68% → 67%; in FY2025 it rose 6.1% while days fell 2.9% and contracted floating rigs averaged 137 against 146; and in the six months to 2026-06-30 days utilized fell again (30,382 → 28,604) with *"higher average revenue per day in 2026, reflecting a mix of improved pricing and the impact of non-recurring"* benefits. That is price taken in a softening market, for two and a half years, in a fleet cut by a quarter and an industry whose rig supply had shrunk. [E3-43] names that route for *"a business"*: *"if supply of its product or service is tight. Tightness in supply usually does not last long."* The filer's own outlook for 2026 is *"stable pricing for ROVs"*. Two and a half years of price after six years of giving it back is not yet *"regularly"*; the reversal condition below asks for the test that the last cycle failed.
2. *"The leader earns more than its competitors on the same robots. Helix's Robotics segment, the one filed ROV peer, earned 25.3% gross in 2025 and 29.7% in 2024; Oceaneering's Subsea Robotics earned 36.7% and 34.7%. In the trough Helix's robotics gross margin went negative (−7.8% in 2016, −3.1% in 2018) while Oceaneering's stayed positive (11% and 8%). That is a cost advantage from scale, which [E2-58] admits."* **Answer:** accepted as the relative position, and it is carried into the reversal condition. [E2-58]'s exception is for producers who *"consistently do well"* on a cost advantage *"both wide and sustainable"*. Oceaneering's lead over the one filed robotics peer is real, but it did not keep the leader doing well: its ROV operating margin was 0% in FY2018 and FY2019 and consolidated returns on net capital were negative for three years. Helix names ten robotics competitors, Oceaneering among them (*"Our principal competitors in the robotics business include Asso Divers, Atlantic Marine, Briggs Marine, C-Innovation, DeepOcean, DOF Subsea, Fugro, James Fisher, Oceaneering and UTROV."*); a lead over one of ten, filed for one of ten, is position, not width.
3. *"The returns are now high: 35% pre-tax on net capital in 2024 and 2025, 28% in 2023. [E3-46]'s second question, 'Has it earned high returns on capital?', answers yes."* **Answer:** accepted as the number of the last three years, on a capital base written down by the impairments of 2016-2020 (equity $2,043.4M in 2013, $505.0M in 2021). A return on a written-down base measures the recovery, not the capital put in; over seventeen filed years the mean is 13.2%, over the last ten 4.9%, and three consecutive years were negative (−7.9%, −19.5%, −49.3%). [E3-43] ties the high return to the ability to *"regularly price"*; the price series above shows the price given back for six years.
4. *"The mix is changing toward businesses the oil cycle does not touch: ADTech (Navy, NASA) is 17% of revenue and the filer's 'primary growth engine' for 2026; OPG carries Jones Act-compliant owned vessels that foreign competitors cannot use in the U.S. Gulf; the umbilical market has lost capacity."* **Answer:** accepted as the direction of the mix. The filer's own words place each of these in the competitive class: ADTech, *"Engineering services is a very broad market with a large number of competitors"*; OPG, *"We are one of many companies that offer these services. In general, our competitors can move their vessels to where we operate from other locations with relative ease"*, with the Jones Act point stated as *"some of our competitors’ vessels are not Jones Act-compliant"*, and five of its seven deepwater vessels are chartered; umbilicals, *"We believe the reduction in capacity by some of our competitors over the last few years, coupled with an increase in demand, should help with balancing a historically over-supplied market."* The last is [E2-58]'s self-correction again, in the filer's words, with its cause named as competitors' capacity leaving, not Oceaneering's price.
5. *"The installed base is sticky: an ROV is contracted with the rig, the tooling interface is Oceaneering's, drillers do not switch mid-campaign, and the 60% share has held for eleven years through the worst downturn in the industry's history."* **Answer:** accepted that the share held (59% → 63% → 60%). That is the finding, not the rebuttal: the share held and the price did not. A customer who stays at a lower price has shown that switching is slow, not that the product has *"no close substitute"* at the old price; the filer's own list of what customers buy on includes price (*"Competition for ROV services, including ROV tooling, historically has been based on equipment availability, location of or ability to deploy the equipment, quality of service and price."*), and it adds that for simpler work *"small companies have been able to bid for contracts at prices uneconomical to us."*

None of the five shows Oceaneering holding a price through a period of spare capacity. The first and second are the strongest facts against this verdict and are carried into the reversal condition.

### THE READING
- **The fleet's price follows the rig count, not Oceaneering.** Average contracted floating rigs 280 (2014) → 150 (2017) → 139 (2020) → 137 (2025), per the 10-Ks' own tables; ROV days utilized 98,302 → 47,282 → 54,411 → 59,629; revenue per day $10,875 → $8,326 → $7,344 → $11,187. The price fell when drillers idled rigs and rose back when the industry's capacity (rigs and ROVs both) had shrunk to fit. That is [E2-58]'s equation (*"persistent over-capacity without administered prices (or costs) equals poor profitability"*) and its closing sentence: *"What finally determines levels of long-term profitability in such industries is the ratio of supply-tight to supply-ample years."* In this record the supply-ample years ran FY2016 to FY2021 (utilization 46-59%), six of the eleven.
- **The filer says what it competes on and against whom.** *"We are one of several companies that provide underwater services and specialty subsea hardware on a worldwide basis. We compete for contracts with companies that have worldwide operations, as well as numerous others operating locally in various areas."* Customers award work *"on a competitive-bid basis"*. Price is named among the four bases of ROV competition, and the filer states the relative importance *"can vary over time based on market conditions"*, which the FY2015-FY2018 10-Ks resolved: *"price has become a more important factor"*.
- **No administered price [E2-59].** Oceaneering's customers' prosperity rides the oil price; Oceaneering's own price is bid. ADTech's government work is cost-audited, which caps rather than floors.
- **[E2-44] (1), tested in the years it bites.** Capacity was not fully utilized in any year from FY2015 to FY2025 (46-69%). In FY2015-FY2020, when rigs were being idled, the price per day fell in five of six years (FY2019 the exception, +1.7%, *"slightly improved pricing"*). In FY2021-FY2023 it rose as utilization climbed from 58% to 68%. In FY2024, FY2025 and the first half of 2026 it rose while days utilized slipped (by 0.8%, 2.9% and 5.9%), which reads as [E2-44]'s test met in a mild softening, after the fleet and the rig supply had been cut. On the eleven-year record the price was taken in two and a half years of tight or tightening supply and given in five of the six ample ones; that is not yet *"an ability to increase prices rather easily"*, and it is the fact the reversal condition turns on.
- **[E3-62], the second step, in the capex line.** The fleet is renewed, not expanded (*"In 2025, we retired sixteen of our conventional work-class ROV systems and replaced them with sixteen upgraded conventional work-class ROV systems"*; *"We currently plan to add new ROVs only to meet contractual commitments"*); what the upgrades save reaches the owner only in the years supply is tight.
