## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**
- Unit economics in my own words, no management language: Oceaneering rents out underwater robots with the crews that fly them, and sells and installs the hardware that connects offshore oil and gas wells to production platforms. **The core is the robot fleet:** 250 work-class remotely operated vehicles, each a tethered machine lowered from a drilling rig or a vessel, charged by the day while it works (*"compensation is based on a rate per day for each day the unit is used"*). Revenue is days worked times the day rate; costs are the pilots, the upkeep and the depreciation of the fleet, which run whether or not the robot works, so profit swings with how many of the 91,250 available days are sold and at what rate. In 2025 the robots worked 59,629 days (65%) and the Subsea Robotics segment earned $257.1M of operating income on $855.2M of revenue (30%). About two-thirds of robot days sit on floating drilling rigs, so demand follows how many deepwater rigs oil companies hire. **Around that core:** a factory business (Manufactured Products: control umbilicals, connectors, valves, and a mobile-robotics and theme-park-ride line) that sells on fixed-price contracts and earns what it bids ($569.0M revenue, 13% margin, 2025); a vessel business (Offshore Projects Group: installation and well intervention from two owned and five chartered vessels, day rates or lump sums; $616.0M, 16%); an inspection business (IMDS: $284.0M, 4%); and a government engineering business (ADTech: Navy submarine and ship work, NASA; $459.9M, 13%). Total 2025 revenue $2,784.2M; operating income $304.6M after $189.6M of unallocated corporate cost (*"Operating expenses (189,558)"*). Customers award most energy work *"on a competitive-bid basis"* in contracts of *"less than one year in duration to multi-year contracts"*.
- The scarce input this business controls: **the largest work-class ROV fleet (*"we own and operate the largest fleet of work-class ROVs in the world"*), the trained pilots and tooling that go with it, and the installed position on drilling rigs (*"an estimated 60% market share of the 136 contracted floating drilling rigs at the end of 2025"*); for the product lines, umbilical plants in the United States, Scotland and Brazil; for OPG, two Jones Act-compliant owned vessels; for ADTech, standing Navy and NASA relationships.** Whether any of this is scarce in [E3-03]'s sense is Q2's question, not Q1's.
- Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years? **The way the money is made, yes; the volume, no one can say from the filings.** The company was formed in 1969 from diving contractors, and the FY2008 10-K already describes the same model and the same segments under older names. What changes is demand: it follows offshore oil and gas spending, which the filer calls *"a historically cyclical industry"*, and the filer names a transition risk (*"increased transition to renewable or other alternative energy sources"*). That bears on the moat (Q2) and on survival and the reliability of earnings (Q4); it does not change the unit economics, which are a day rate on a fleet and a margin on bid contracts.
- **Simple and stable in character [E3-31]; a decision that can be made on the filings [E4-46].** Renting a machine and its operator by the day, and building hardware to a bid price, require no technology forecast to understand. Five segments are more to read than one, but each is described in the filings in terms that need no months of study; ADTech's government contracting is the least visible of the five and is 17% of revenue.
