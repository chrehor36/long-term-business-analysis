## Q2 — IS IT A FRANCHISE? **[E3-03]**

*Every Aptar figure below is read from its filed annual reports 2005-2025 (the segment note of each 10-K, the MD&A, Item 1 and Item 1A), from the 10-Q for the quarter to 2026-06-30 and the release of 2026-07-30, or from SEC companyfacts checked against the 2025 statements (Step 0). **The question this file has to answer: is Aptar one business or two, and does the one that earns the money set its own price?** A maker of pumps, valves, caps and rubber stoppers that has told its owners in every 10-K since 2005 that it experiences *"price competition in all product lines and markets"* is the shape of a business that fails [E3-03]'s second criterion. A drug-device maker whose nasal pumps and inhaler valves are designed into medicines years before launch, and whose Pharma segment has earned 24-31% of its sales in every one of twenty years, is the shape of a franchise. **The two hypotheses, stated so they can be attacked [E4-26].** *(a) A narrow franchise in drug delivery with a packaging business attached: Pharma carries 79% of segment operating profit on 46% of sales; its devices are qualified into each customer's drug over years and are not swapped when a cheaper pump appears; the 20-year record, held through 2009, the resin cycles and the pandemic, is the demonstration.* *(b) A components maker in a price-competitive trade, whose own filings say so about every product line, whose consumer half passes resin through and faces low-cost Asian rivals, whose Pharma segment has cut prices to secure contracts, lost margin to inflation, and leans on one high-margin product, and whose company-wide return on capital is ordinary.* Each is tested on the filings below.

**[E3-03], criterion by criterion, the unit being the company [E4-08].** *"An economic franchise arises from a product or service that: (1) is needed or desired; (2) is thought by its customers to have no close substitute and; (3) is not subject to price regulation."*
- **(1) Needed or desired [x]: passes**, both halves.
- **(3) Not subject to price regulation [x]: passes.** Aptar's prices are negotiated; the regulated price is the customer's drug price, which the filer names as a risk to demand and price, not a regulation of Aptar's price.
- **(2) Thought by its customers to have no close substitute: PASSES FOR PHARMA, FAILS FOR THE OTHER HALF, and the company's economics are Pharma's.** For the drug maker, the device is part of the medicine's development and approval (*"a significant amount of time and research from initially working with pharmaceutical companies at the molecular development stage of a medication through the eventual distribution to the market"*; the Combination Products guidelines under which the device *"now is required to be appropriately designed, developed and documented"*): the substitute for an Aptar pump already in an approved nasal spray is a re-development, not a cheaper pump. That some customers pay Aptar **royalties on their own product's sales** is what a customer pays for something it could not buy elsewhere. For the fragrance house, the shampoo brand and the ketchup bottler, the filer's own words say the substitute is close: low-cost Asian suppliers, *"local and regional competitors"*, *"certain of our dispensing closures"* under pricing pressure, resin passed through. The other half is a business; it is 54% of sales, 52% of segment assets and 21% of segment profit.

### THE STRONGEST EVIDENCE AGAINST THIS VERDICT, stated as its holders would state it [E4-51, E4-26, E3-47]
The verdict below is IN, NARROW, so the case for OUT is set out first, at full strength:
1. **The filer itself says, in every 10-K for twenty-one years, that it experiences *"price competition in all product lines and markets"*.** "All" includes Pharma. [E3-03]'s second criterion is about what the customer thinks, and a company that tells its owners every customer is shopping on price has answered it. The ePlus and Quaker Houghton files closed OUT on sentences of this kind.
2. **The Pharma segment has never shown [E3-43]'s aggressive price.** Two price rises in twenty-one years, both in injectables, one of them a pandemic year; a price cut in 2025 to secure longer contracts; three points of margin lost to inflation in 2021-2022. West, the largest SEC filer in injectable components, reports *"continued pricing pressure from our customers and competitors"*. A level margin is what a well-run supplier with sticky customers earns; it is not proof the customer thinks there is no substitute.
3. **The company is not Pharma.** Half its sales and half its segment assets are in a consumer business with no franchise by the filer's own words, whose profit has not grown in seventeen years and whose return on assets is 3-6%; the company's own return on operating capital is 12-16%, ordinary for industry. [E4-08]'s unit is the company.
4. **Pharma leans on a few products.** The 2026 margin fell on *"the disproportionate margin discrepancy on our emergency medicine products"*; royalties are rising inside the segment's profit; one product line's destocking cut the prescription division's core sales 9% in a half-year.

**Answered.** (1) is accepted as the filer's sentence and weighed against the filer's numbers: the sentence has been identical since 2005 and names no segment, while the MD&A, year after year, locates the pricing pressure in *"the low-end of the fragrance/cosmetic market and ... certain of our dispensing closures"* (2005) and in *"our Beauty & Home and Closures segments"* (2009), and Pharma's margin never moved with resin as the other half's did. ePlus sold other companies' products at 3-8% margins; Quaker's price followed raw materials both ways and its return on all capital had not exceeded 9.7% in any year since 2019; neither had a segment earning 24-31% for twenty years. (2) is accepted and is why the class is NARROW and the direction level: the demonstration here is the level held, not the increase taken, the NDSN form. (3) is accepted as the most serious and is carried where the framework puts it: the consumer half is a moat defect recorded here, a capital-allocation question at Q3 ([E2-56], [E3-40]) and a great/good/gruesome question at Q4 ([E4-20]); **the precedent is HAS** (*"a franchise with a toy company attached"*, WotC at 92-97% of segment profit) **and GRMN** (the dominant segment at 75.5% of segment operating income), here 79%. (4) is accepted and carried to Q4 as a named exposure; the named weakness is one product line inside a segment whose core sales grew in every year the filer states (2016, 2018-2025).
