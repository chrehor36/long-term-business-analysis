## Q2 — IS IT A FRANCHISE? **[E3-03]**

**The hypothesis I formed while reading, stated so it can be attacked [E4-26]:** *SunCoke sells a commodity (blast-furnace coke) to two steelmakers who buy three-quarters of everything it makes, at a price built from their own coal cost plus a fixed fee that the filing says is set to earn "an adequate return on invested capital"; the take-or-pay contract makes the earnings steady while it lasts, but the contract, not the product, sets the price, and at every renewal the customer's alternatives (its own coke batteries, other merchants, imports, and in the end the electric arc furnace) set it again. Steady and low is what the record should show, and a franchise would show the reverse.* The evidence against it is hunted and answered before the verdict.

**[E3-03], criterion by criterion.** *"An economic franchise arises from a product or service that: (1) is needed or desired; (2) is thought by its customers to have no close substitute and; (3) is not subject to price regulation. The existence of all three conditions will be demonstrated by a company's ability to regularly price its product or service aggressively and thereby to earn high rates of return on capital."* The same 1991 passage: *"In contrast, "a business" earns exceptional profits only if it is the low-cost operator or if supply of its product or service is tight."* **[E3-43]**

- **(1) Needed or desired [x]: passes.** A blast furnace cannot run without coke; 3.7 million tons of 2025 production were delivered or sold.
- **(3) Not subject to price regulation [x]: passes, with a note.** No regulator sets the coke price. The power sold from Haverhill and Middletown is under FERC's market-based-rate authority (Item 1A), a small line. The price of the main product is administered instead by contract, which is [E2-59]'s question below, not criterion (3)'s.
- **(2) No close substitute [ ]: FAILS, in the registrant's own words, and in the way its price is made.**
  - *The registrant calls the product market competitive and names the substitutes.* Item 1 (FY2025): *"The cokemaking market is highly competitive. Competitors include merchant coke producers as well as the cokemaking facilities owned and operated by blast furnace steel companies. The principal competitive factors affecting our cokemaking business include coke quality and price, reliability of supply, proximity to market, access to metallurgical coals and environmental performance. Most of the world's coke production capacity is owned by blast furnace steel companies."* Item 1A: *"some blast furnace operators have relied upon natural gas, pulverized coal, and/or other coke substitutes"*; *"electric arc furnace technology is a commercially proven process widely used in the U.S."*; *"exports of blast furnace and/or foundry coke from China and/or other coke-producing countries also may reduce our customers' demand for coke capacity."* The customer's own coke battery is the first substitute, and the customer's decision to stop making iron in a blast furnace is the last.
  - *The price is made by the buyer's costs plus a fee designed to be adequate, not by what the customer would pay.* Item 1: coal is passed through *"subject to meeting contractual coal-to-coke yields"*; operating costs are either *"fixed subject to an annual adjustment based on an inflation index"* or *"passed through to the respective customers subject to an annually negotiated budget, in some cases subject to a cap annually adjusted for inflation, and we share any difference in costs from the budgeted amounts with our customers"*; and *"The fixed fee component for each ton of coke sold to the customer is determined at the time the coke sales agreement is signed and is effective for the term of each sales agreement. The fixed fee is intended to provide an adequate return on invested capital and may differ based on investment levels and other considerations."* The customer even controls the input: *"Most metallurgical coal procurement decisions are made through a coal committee structure with customer participation. The customer can generally exercise an overriding vote on most coal procurement decisions."* A product the customer thinks has no close substitute is not priced as cost plus an adequate return with the customer holding the vote on the raw material.
  - *The price is re-set at renewal, and the record of renewals is down.* Granite City (U.S. Steel): the original contract ran to 2025; the October 2024 extension (FY2024 10-K) *"will operate at a turn-down capacity, supplying 295 thousand tons of coke"* and *"results in significantly lower overall economics compared to the current long-term, take-or-pay agreement"*; it has since been renewed one year at a time (2025, and 2026 at 590 thousand tons, *"key terms of the agreement are similar to the prior extension"*, 8-K `0001514705-26-000005`), and the FY2025 MD&A lists *"the impact of the Granite City contract extension economics"* among the causes of lower revenue and Adjusted EBITDA. Haverhill II (Cliffs): a three-year extension to 2028 (8-K `0001514705-25-000038`). Haverhill I: its second customer, Algoma, *"refus[ed] to accept any additional coke tons"* in 2025, the plant was impaired by $90.1M and closed in Q1 2026. Item 1A states the general case: *"We may not be able to obtain long-term agreements at favorable prices, compared either to market conditions or to our cost structure. Price changes provided in long-term supply agreements may not reflect actual increases in production costs."*
  - *The buyers have consolidated.* FY2011: *"ArcelorMittal, our largest customer, accounted for approximately 64 percent of our total revenues"*, U.S. Steel 18%, AK Steel 14%. FY2020 10-K: *"In March 2020, Cleveland-Cliffs Inc. ("Cliffs") ... completed the acquisition of AK Steel"* and *"In December 2020, Cliffs completed the acquisition of AM USA"*; FY2025 Note 4: Cliffs 61.6% and U.S. Steel 14.2% of revenue. Three buyers became two, and the larger buys three plants' output.

- **High returns on capital: no.** Over fourteen years the business earned **6.2% before tax on the net capital employed (8.3% before impairments)** and **3.5% a year on SunCoke's equity**; in the four best years (2021-2024) 12.1-15.1% before tax. [E3-46]: *"the best businesses, by definition, are going to be businesses that earn very high returns on capital employed over time."* These are not those returns.
- **Pricing aggressively: no.** The fee per ton was **$57.38 in 2012 and $58.27 in 2024 in nominal dollars**: in 2025 dollars it fell from about $80 to about $60 (−26%) before the 2025 fall to $46.35 (−42% over thirteen years). The contract design explains it: the fixed fee is *"effective for the term of each sales agreement"* and only the operating-cost piece is indexed. [E4-47]: *"The best business to have during inflation is one that retains its earning power in real dollars without commensurate investment"*; this one's margin per ton was fixed in nominal dollars for a decade.
- **Stable is not the same as high.** The operating margin over 2012-2025 is 4.7% of revenue on the latest-filed figures (5.5% on the earliest-filed vintage in the row below), and the revenue includes pass-through coal.

**[E2-58] and [E2-59], the commodity doctrine, applied as the framework states it.** Coke is undifferentiated *"in any customer-important way"* except quality, which the registrant claims but which the contract prices only through the fixed fee set at signing. [E2-59]: commodity businesses may escape *"if prices or costs are administered in some manner and thereby insulated at least partially from normal market forces"*. The take-or-pay contract is that administration, done privately: coal cost passed through, operating cost indexed or budgeted, a fee fixed for the term, volume guaranteed or paid for. **That is why the earnings are steady, and it is why the steadiness belongs to the contract, not to the product.** The framework's own reading (Q2, *"the moat belongs to the regime"*): the regime floors the profit while it lasts, and the renewal is where *"That day is gone"* is written; Granite City's *"significantly lower overall economics"* is that sentence in this filer's words, and the 2019 bankruptcy of a logistics customer, *"Murray American Coal, Inc.'s bankruptcy and the rejection of its take-or-pay contract"* (FY2019 10-K), shows that the contract is only as strong as the buyer. **The one exception [E2-58] allows is "a cost advantage that is both wide and sustainable".** The registrant claims quality, heat-recovery power sales, environmental performance and new plants against *"aging by-product coke batteries"*; if that were a wide cost advantage it would show in the fee at renewal and in the return on capital, and neither shows it.

**The remaining Q2 tests.**
- **[E2-44], the two characteristics.** *"an ability to increase prices rather easily (even when product demand is flat and capacity is not fully utilized) without fear of significant loss of either market share or unit volume"*: the fee cannot be raised during a contract at all, and at renewal it has been cut (Granite City) or the tons lost (Algoma, Haverhill I). *"only minor additional investment of capital"*: the business is, in its own MD&A's words, *"capital intensive, requiring significant investment to upgrade or enhance existing operations and to meet environmental and operational regulations"*, and *"Expansion capital"* includes spending to *"enable the renewal of a coke sales agreement"*. Fails on both.
- **[E4-55], the physical series.** Domestic Coke sales 4,184 kt (2014) to 3,668 kt (2025), −12%, with Haverhill I's capacity now closed; per-ton economics −42% in real terms 2012-2025. Both honest series point down.
- **[E4-37], the agony metric.** The fee is not raised; it is negotiated at renewal against a buyer with an alternative, and the 10-K's own risk factor says the price it gets *"may not reflect actual increases in production costs"*.
- **[E3-33] / [E5-28], untapped pricing power.** None: the price is contractual and fixed for the term. [E5-28]: *"If you name some business that has incredible pricing power, you're talking about a business that's a monopoly or a near monopoly"*; with 38% of US blast-furnace coke capacity, the captive batteries of its own customers holding most of the rest, and imports at the margin, SunCoke is not a near monopoly, and its customer is closer to a near monopsony.
- **[E2-53], the dominance test.** *"Once dominant, the newspaper itself, not the marketplace, determines just how good or how bad the paper will be."* SunCoke is the largest US merchant coke maker, and it does not set its own economics: the buyer's coal committee votes on its input and the buyer's renewal decision sets its fee.
- **[E2-45], the attacker's test.** New coke capacity is hard to build for anyone (*"the only greenfield cokemaking facilities in the U.S. in over 35 years"*), which protects the existing plants against a new merchant. But the attacker that matters is the customer, and the customer attacks by not renewing, by running its own batteries, or by switching route: Item 1A, *"As alternative processes for production of steel become more widespread, the demand for blast furnace coke, including the coke we produce, may be significantly reduced."* The Phoenix Global purchase was made for exactly this reason, in management's own presentation: *"Diversifying our customer base to include Electric Arc Furnace (EAF) operators"*.
- **[E4-04] and [E4-23].** [E4-04] is applied as a competence limit (ruling of 2026-09-20), and it is not the ground of this verdict: the ground is criterion (2) and the [E3-43] record. No single key person is essential; the renewals turn on the customer.
- **Industrial Services, the second segment, tested the same way.** The terminals: *"Other logistics facilities and independent terminal operations in some areas may compete directly with our logistics facilities. In some markets, trucks may competitively deliver products"* (Item 1A); the Convent terminal, bought in 2015, was impaired by $247.4M in 2019 after a customer's bankruptcy and the rejection of its take-or-pay contract. The mill services: *"competes principally with a small number of businesses for these services that are outsourced by customers on a global basis and, to some degree, customers that may decide to perform certain services themselves"* (Item 1). Neither segment claims no close substitute.
- **The unit of the test [E4-08].** Two segments, one customer base (steel), one pricing method (contracted fees per ton). Domestic Coke was 87.8% of 2025 revenue.

**THE STRONGEST EVIDENCE AGAINST THIS VERDICT, hunted hardest [E4-26, E3-41, E4-51].**
1. **The contract works like a moat while it runs.** Take-or-pay, coal passed through, operating costs indexed: SunCoke's operating margin stayed at 5.4-5.6% through the steel busts in which Cleveland-Cliffs' aggregate operating margin was −26.6% (2012-2019). Earnings that steady are what a franchise's earnings look like.
2. **Scarce, permitted capacity.** 38% of US blast-furnace coke capacity; the only new US coke plants in over 35 years; three plants joined to the customer's furnace by conveyor. Nobody can build a competing plant easily, including the customer.
3. **The long renewals.** Indiana Harbor was renewed in April 2023 to September 2035 for the full 1,220 thousand tons with *"Other key provisions of the agreement, including the pass-through of coal costs"* unchanged; Middletown runs to 2032; Haverhill II was renewed to 2028.
4. **In the short run the blast furnace has no substitute for coke.** The customer who wants iron from that furnace must buy coke from someone.
5. **[E3-47]**: a wrongly closed file on an understood business is the costliest error, and this business is understood.

**Answered.** (1) Steadiness is the contract's and the price is the contract's; [E3-43] does not ask for steady returns, it asks for pricing aggressively and high returns on capital, and the fourteen-year record is 6.2% pre-tax on net capital and 3.5% on equity, with the fee per ton down 42% in real terms. The contract floors the profit, which is [E2-59]'s administered-price escape, and the framework states that the moat then belongs to the regime. (2) Scarce capacity protects the tons from a new entrant; it does not protect the price from the buyer, who holds the alternative of its own batteries, imports and, on the longer view, the electric arc furnace, and who has used it (Algoma; Granite City's *"significantly lower overall economics"*). A barrier that protects volume but not price is not [E3-03]'s *"no close substitute"* as the customer sees it; it is a supply side that is tight, which [E3-43] assigns to "a business" (*"Tightness in supply usually does not last long"*). (3) The Indiana Harbor renewal kept the coal pass-through and moved operating costs to an inflation-indexed fixed amount; the filing does not disclose the new fixed fee, and the segment's per-ton figure fell after it (2022 $65.34, 2023 $61.25, 2024 $58.27). A renewal at undisclosed and falling unit economics is not evidence of pricing power. (4) Correct in the short run, and the reason the contracts exist; but the price for that short-run necessity is set once, for a decade, at an "adequate" return, and the customer re-decides at every renewal. (5) I weighed [E3-47]. The file closes on the product, the price mechanism and fourteen years of returns, not on the manager or the price of the shares: **[E5-42] keeps the judgments apart.** Nothing here says SunCoke is badly run; the oven operations, yields and safety record are its own work.

**Why OUT and not the perimeter close (UNKNOWABLE at Q2).** The 2026-09-20 ruling reserves UNKNOWABLE for *"A name that passes [E3-03] and whose durability cannot be judged from filings"*. SunCoke does not pass [E3-03]: criterion (2) fails in its own Item 1 (*"The cokemaking market is highly competitive"*, captive batteries and merchant producers named, *"coke quality and price"* the competitive factors), in its price mechanism (cost plus a fee *"intended to provide an adequate return"*, the customer holding the vote on coal), and in its renewal record; and [E3-43]'s demonstration is absent over fourteen filed years. The durability of blast-furnace demand is uncertain, but that question (can a franchise's durability be judged?) does not arise when the franchise criterion itself fails.

- **Untapped pricing power [E3-33]**: none; the price is fixed by contract for its term.
