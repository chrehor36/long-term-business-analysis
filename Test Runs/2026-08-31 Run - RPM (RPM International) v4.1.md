## (c) Is the stated yield a boom-window artifact? — **PASS with a named caveat, and the answer is that it depends on which window you use, which is itself the finding [E4-41, E4-25]**

The five-year window is FY2022 to FY2026. Four exogenous or one-time items sit inside it.

1. **The 2021–22 raw-material inflation shock hurt RPM; it did not flatter it.** Gross margin fell
   from 39.4% (FY2021) to **36.3% (FY2022)**, and working capital absorbed **$448.3M** of cash in
   FY2022 alone as inventories and receivables built. **FY2022 owner earnings were −$83.8M.**
   The window's *first* year is a trough, not a boom.
2. **FY2024 is the mirror image and it is the real artifact.** Working capital **released $360.0M**
   as supply chains normalised, producing $1,122.3M of operating cash flow and **$882.4M of owner
   earnings — the highest in RPM's history by 55%.** Operating working capital fell from 27.5% of
   sales (FY2022) to **23.5% (FY2024)** and has since gone 24.3% and **23.7%. The release happened
   once and has stopped.**
3. **The commodity cycle is named by RPM itself as a margin driver, and the price was kept.** The
   FY2024 10-K: gross margin rose 320bp *"primarily from our MAP 2025 initiatives … **in
   conjunction with benefits generated from the commodity cycle.** Selling price increases also
   aided in the margin expansion."* Gross margin went 36.3% → 37.9% → **41.1% → 41.4% → 41.4%**.
   The 41.4% now standing rests in part on inflation-era pricing that has not been given back.
   **Every 100bp of gross margin is $78.6M of pre-tax income — 17.5% of five-year mean owner
   earnings.** This is the live [E4-41] item.
4. **FY2025's 12.9% effective tax rate is a book event, not a cash one.** It came from a $43.9M
   deferred-tax recognition for foreign tax credits. **Cash taxes paid ran 22.1% to 28.4% of
   pre-tax income across the whole window with no downward drift**, so owner earnings are
   untouched. Flagged and dismissed with the arithmetic.

**The verdict on leg (c), stated precisely because the two windows disagree:**
- The **five-year mean of $449.3M is NOT a boom artifact.** Across FY2022–26 working capital
  absorbed a net $245.0M, so the mean is if anything *depressed* by the build-and-release cycle

- The **three-year mean of $678.7M IS flattered**, materially: FY2024–26 released a net $307.6M of

- **The two windows are 51% apart.** That spread is carried into Q4 as part of the range, not

## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? [E3-31]

- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language.** RPM buys petroleum derivatives,
  resins, solvents, pigments, minerals and metal, mixes them into paint, caulk, sealant, adhesive,
  grout, admixture, coating and roofing membrane, puts them in cans and tubes and drums, and sells
  them under about forty brand names into three channels. **A third of the revenue goes into a
  big-box store or a hardware store** and is bought by a homeowner or a painter to stop rust
  (Rust-Oleum), seal a gap (DAP), block a stain (Zinsser), stain a deck (Ready Seal) or clean an
  oven (The Pink Stuff). **Two-thirds goes to a contractor or a building owner** and is sold, very
  often, as a *system with labour attached* — Tremco does not just sell roofing membrane, it sends
  a crew and writes a warranty; Stonhard installs the floor it manufactures. RPM converts raw
  material at a **41.4% gross margin**, spends 29.1 points of revenue on selling, administration
  and $97.9M of research, and 0.5 points on restructuring, leaving **11.7 cents of operating
  income on every revenue dollar**. Interest takes 1.4 points and tax 2.6, so **8.4 cents becomes
  net income**. It employs 17,546 people, occupies 20.3 million square feet in about 120
  manufacturing locations, and carries $1,556.5M of net plant, $1,058.9M of inventory and
  $1,661.5M of receivables against $7,863.4M of revenue. It spends $223.5M a year on capital and
  another $200M–$600M buying companies. **It turns roughly $0.9bn of operating cash, keeps about
  a quarter of it in the business, and posts $272M of dividends and $77M of buybacks out of the
  door.** There is no float, no subscription and no annuity; there is a small construction-contract
  book (roofing and flooring installation) recognised over time, and $92.4M of long-term deferred
  warranty revenue.
- **The scarce input this business controls: the specification and the shelf position — a
  low-cost consumable whose failure is expensive, sold under a name the buyer already trusts.** Not
  the raw material, which RPM's own filing says is "generally available on the open market from a
  variety of producers." Not scale, which RPM explicitly disclaims: "we do not believe that we
  have a significant share of the total protective coatings market." What is scarce is that
  Rust-Oleum is the can the hardware store has always stocked and the painter has always used;
  that DAP is written into the builder's spec; that Tremco's warranty is what the building owner
  bought and re-buys every time the roof needs work. **Roughly two-thirds of the business is
  repair and maintenance rather than new construction**, which is the durable half of a cyclical
  industry, and RPM says so repeatedly.
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** Yes. Buildings will still leak,
  metal will still rust, floors will still wear, and the people who fix them will still buy from
  brands they know. The company has existed since 1947 and has increased its dividend for 52
  consecutive years through every cycle in that period. The named change is **channel**: a third
  of the business depends on retail shelf space at a small number of very large retailers whose
  own economics are under pressure, and that segment's volumes have now fallen for three
  consecutive years. That is a change in the customer, not in the nature of the business, and it is
  carried to Q2 and quantified at Q4.
- **[E4-46] check:** this decision does not need five months. Three reportable segments, one
  short 10-K, plain notes, and a product line a homeowner can hold in one hand. It is inside the
  circle. The one thing that would genuinely take months — valuing a hundred-odd separate
  operating companies from the bottom up — is not required, because RPM discloses enough at the
  segment level to test the aggregate.

## Q2 — IS IT A FRANCHISE? [E3-03]

**The question as posed by the operator: are RPM's brands franchises, or shelf-space tenants?**
The answer this run reaches is neither of the two clean ones. **RPM owns a portfolio of real but
narrow brand and specification positions, and the competitor row shows that the portfolio earns
less on its operating capital than most of its listed peers.** Class **NARROW**, direction
**MIXED and, in the segment that carries the brands, DETERIORATING.** Worked below.

**[E3-03], the three criteria:**
- **Needed or desired [x].** Roof leaks, rust, concrete spalling and floor failure are not
  deferrable indefinitely. Roughly two-thirds of revenue is repair and maintenance. The FY2020
  record proves the point: in the pandemic year net income *rose* from $266.6M to $304.4M.
- **No close substitute [~] — PARTIAL, and RPM's own Item 1 says so more bluntly than I would.**
  Verbatim: *"We conduct our business in highly competitive markets, and all of our major products
  face competition from local, regional, national and multi-national firms. **Our markets,
  however, are fragmented**, and we do not face competition across all of our products from any
  one competitor in particular. **Several of our competitors have access to greater financial
  resources and larger sales organizations than we do.**"* And: *"**we do not believe that we have
  a significant share of the total protective coatings market** (on a world-wide basis)."* The
  filing names eight competitors: Akzo Nobel, Axalta, Carlisle, H.B. Fuller, Masco, PPG,
  Sherwin-Williams and Sika. **At the enterprise level there is no "no close substitute" claim to
  make.** At the individual position level there often is — the DIY buyer reaching for a familiar
  can, the architect who specified Tremco, the plant that has run Stonhard floors for twenty
  years. **That is the honest shape of the moat: many narrow positions, no wide one.**
- **Not price-regulated [x].**

**Must the moat be continuously rebuilt? [E4-04], scoped as the corpus scopes it.**
The test is whether a lapse in spending destroys the structure or merely narrows it, and whether
the spending defends the same advantage or buys its replacement. **RPM's answer is mixed and the
uncomfortable half is real.** Advertising, $97.9M of research and 120 plants defend the same
brands and specifications — that is maintenance, not replacement, and Rust-Oleum and DAP are
generations old. **But a material part of RPM's growth is bought rather than defended:
$988.7M of acquisitions in five years, 44% of cumulative owner earnings.** Where a roll-up buys
new revenue because the old revenue is not growing, the spending is closer to Mitsui's Rhodes
Ridge than to Coca-Cola's advertising. RPM sits between the two and the run does not pretend
otherwise.
**The second half of [E4-04] — does success depend on having a great manager?** Partly, and it is
recorded here rather than at Q3 per the guardrail. **RPM's operating margin went from 8.09%
(FY2019) to 11.74% (FY2026) entirely on the back of two named restructuring programmes** — MAP to
Growth and MAP 2025 — and a third ("MAP 3.0") is being formulated for an investor day on
2026-11-09. **The margin is a programme, not a position.** It is a smaller dependence than ITW's,
because RPM's brands would keep selling through mediocrity; but it means the *level* of the
earnings, as distinct from their existence, rests on management execution. **Recorded here as a
moat defect. It is the mechanism of the primary named death at Q4.**
