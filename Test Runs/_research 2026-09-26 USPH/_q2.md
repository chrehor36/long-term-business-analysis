## Q2 — IS IT A FRANCHISE? **[E3-03]**

*Every figure below is read from the filed MD&A tables and statements of the 10-Ks named at Step 0; the extraction is saved as `rpv_out.txt`, `cf_FY*_flat.txt` and `peers/`. Tagged data was used only to locate years and is not the source of any figure here.*

**[E3-03], criterion by criterion.** *"An economic franchise arises from a product or service that: (1) is needed or desired; (2) is thought by its customers to have no close substitute and; (3) is not subject to price regulation. The existence of all three conditions will be demonstrated by a company's ability to regularly price its product or service aggressively and thereby to earn high rates of return on capital."*

- **(1) Needed or desired [x] — passes.** Post-operative and injury rehabilitation is prescribed by physicians and paid for by every class of insurer; 6,150,104 visits in FY2025.
- **(2) No close substitute [ ] — fails, in the registrant's own words.** 10-K FY2025, Item 1, COMPETITION: *"The healthcare industry, including the physical therapy business and the industrial injury prevention services business, is highly competitive. The physical therapy business as well as the industrial injury prevention services business are both highly fragmented with no company having a significant market share nationally."* And: *"Our clinics compete, directly or indirectly, with many types of healthcare providers including the physical therapy departments of hospitals, private therapy clinics, physician-owned therapy clinics, and chiropractors."* The patient goes where the doctor sends him and the insurer will pay; the filing names who else can take him. The company's claimed advantage is not a customer-side one: *"We believe that our partnership strategy provides us with a competitive advantage. Our clinics are partly owned by therapists who have developed exceptional reputations in their local communities"*, which is an advantage of the local therapist (see [E4-23] below), not a product the customer thinks has no substitute.
- **(3) Not subject to price regulation [ ] — fails.** **35.8% of FY2025 net patient revenue ($232.7M) and 40.1% of visits** are Medicare or Medicaid, paid on the government's fee schedule: *"The Medicare program reimburses outpatient rehabilitation providers based on the Medicare Physician Fee Schedule (“MPFS”)."* The schedule was **cut in each of the five years 2021 to 2025**: *"decreases of approximately 3.5%, 0.75% and 2.0%"* (2021-2023), *"an approximate 1.8% reduction"* (2024 after Congress intervened), and for 2025 *"decreased Medicare reimbursement for therapy services by approximately 2.9%"* (MD&A; Item 1 of the same filing says *"approximately 1.5%"* for 2025, an inconsistency inside one document, recorded and carried beneath the close as a Q3 prompt). Medicare also pays assistant-delivered care *"at 85% of the otherwise applicable Part B payment amount"*. The other 64% is priced by contract with payors who set the terms: *"we are obligated to provide services for the reimbursement rates stipulated in the payor contracts"* (critical accounting policies) and *"If insurers or managed care companies from whom we receive substantial payments were to reduce the amounts they pay for services, our profit margins may decline, or we may lose patients if we choose not to renew our contracts with these insurers at lower rates"* (Item 1A). Workers' compensation is priced by state schedules. **This is [E2-59]'s administered price, and it runs the wrong way for the owner:** the regime does not floor a commodity business here; it caps and cuts, and *"That day is gone"* is how an administered price ends.

**[E2-44] — the two-characteristic test fails on characteristic (1).** *"an ability to increase prices rather easily (even when product demand is flat and capacity is not fully utilized) without fear of significant loss of either market share or unit volume."* Volume rose 3.2 times from 2010 to 2025 and the price did not move. Characteristic (2), growth *"with only minor additional investment of capital"*: the growth was bought (below), not accommodated.

**[E4-37] — the prayer-session metric.** The company does not decide its price at all: 36% is decided in Washington, the rest by payor contract. The 2024 MD&A credits a rise to *"higher reimbursement rates from commercial and other payors as a result of contract negotiations"*; that rise took the rate from $102.80 back to $104.71, below 2010.

**[E3-33], [E5-28] — untapped pricing power.** None can exist where the price is set by the payor. Claiming it would be claiming near-monopoly in a business the registrant calls highly fragmented.

**[E4-23] — the moat defect, recorded here, not at Q3.** *"A medical partnership led by your area's premier brain surgeon may enjoy outsized and growing earnings, but that tells little about its future. The partnership's moat will go when the surgeon goes."* The filing describes this structure almost word for word: the clinic partnership in which *"the managing healthcare practitioner is a physical therapist who owns, directly or indirectly, the remaining limited partnership interest"*, whose referrals are the business (*"Our therapists are the front line for generating these referrals and we are dependent on their talents and skills to successfully cultivate and maintain strong relationships with these physicians"*), protected by a non-compete that ends *"up to two years"* after employment, and bought out on departure *"at a predetermined multiple of earnings performance"*. The company's own stated competitive advantage is the local therapist's reputation. **Clinic closures are the moat going when the surgeon goes, at retail:** 45 owned clinics closed in 2024 and 23 in 2025 (*"We closed 45 underperforming clinics in the 2024 Year"*); *"Periodically, we have clinics in isolated communities that are temporarily unable to operate due to the unavailability of a therapist who satisfies our standards."*

**[E4-04] — rebuilt or maintained?** The basis is re-bought: a partner who retires is bought out and replaced, and growth comes from buying new practices. **Recorded, not relied on**: under the 2026-09-20 ruling [E4-04] is a competence limit, and the verdict below does not need it.

Total-company GAAP operating margin: **15.7% (2010), 14.7% (2013), 14.0% (2019), 10.3% (2022), 8.4% (2023, after a $17.5M goodwill impairment), 9.4% (2024), 11.1% (2025)**. And a quarter of the earnings are not the owner's: $18.4M of FY2025's $58.0M net income went to the partners.

**Direction [E4-32]:** narrowing. Flat price, rising labour cost per visit, falling margin, falling return on what the owner paid.

That is a commodity signature. The row also shows **USPH is the best operator of the three**, by a clear margin in every year shown.

**THE STRONGEST EVIDENCE AGAINST THIS VERDICT, hunted hardest [E4-26, E3-41, E4-51].**
1. **USPH out-earns both public peers in every year shown**: 11.1% GAAP operating margin in 2025 against Select's 7.0% segment EBITDA margin and ATI's 0.3%. ATI, with private-equity capital and a similar clinic count, lost money: **[E2-45]'s attacker, with ample capital and skilled personnel, did not match it.**
2. **The return on net tangible operating assets is 150-215%**, and the company has grown for 35 years through three payment regimes.
3. **Mature-clinic revenue rose 3.1% in the first half of 2026**; the 2026 Medicare schedule rose 1.75%; commercial contracts were renegotiated upward in 2024; and ten-year hospital alliances were signed in February 2026.
4. **Visits per clinic per day rose from 20.5 to 32.2**, so the owner has been paid by efficiency even with the price flat.

**Answered.** (1) and (4) are the evidence of a well-run **business** in [E3-43]'s sense, *"In contrast, “a business” earns exceptional profits only if it is the low-cost operator or if supply of its product or service is tight"*; being the best operator in a price-regulated, fragmented field is the textile lesson [E2-37], *"a remarkable textile company — but not a remarkable business"*, and the attacker who failed (ATI) failed on execution and leverage, not against a customer who thought USPH had no substitute. (2) is the character of a people business, where the capital is the payroll: the owner's return on what he actually paid is **9.6% pretax and falling** [E2-43]. (3) is a partial year and a price set by the government upward this year after five years down; the direction of the administered price is not the owner's to set, which is criterion (3) itself. None of the four reaches criterion (2) or (3); and the demonstration clause asks for *"regularly"* pricing aggressively, against fifteen years of $105.92 to $105.76.

- **VERDICT: [x] OUT** — on the business. **[E3-03] criterion (3) fails** (36% of patient revenue on the government's fee schedule, cut in five consecutive years 2021-2025; the rest at rates *"stipulated in the payor contracts"*); **criterion (2) fails in the registrant's own words** (*"highly competitive"*, *"highly fragmented"*, hospital departments, private clinics, physician-owned clinics and chiropractors named as competitors); **the demonstration clause fails on the physical series** (net patient revenue per visit $105.92 in 2010, $105.76 in 2025, while salaries per visit rose $57.54 to $62.04); and **[E4-23]'s moat defect is the business model itself** (the local partner-therapist holds the referral relationships). This is [E2-58]'s commodity class under [E2-59]'s administered price, run by an operator who is better than its public peers. **The file closes here.** Q3 to Q6 are not opened as gates; the material gathered beneath the close follows without verdicts.
