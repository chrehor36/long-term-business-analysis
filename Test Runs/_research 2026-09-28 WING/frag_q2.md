## Q2 — IS IT A FRANCHISE? **[E3-03]**

*Every Wingstop figure below is read from its filed statements: the IPO prospectus of 2015 for FY2012-FY2014, the 10-Ks FY2015-FY2025, the 10-Qs of 2026 and the furnished releases of Q4 2024 to Q2 2026 (scripts and outputs in `Test Runs/_research 2026-09-28 WING/`: `sents.py` for every sentence quoted, its pricing output saved as `pricing_search.txt`, `peers_row.py` → `peers_out.txt` for the row arithmetic; the peers' filings in `cache/peers/`, not committed).*

**The question is put at both altitudes, as the MCD run of 2026-09-03 put it.** Wingstop sells to two customers: the **franchisee**, who buys the brand and the system and pays a royalty, and the **diner**, whose purchases are the royalty's base. The MCD run held that *"the diner faces close substitutes on every corner; the franchisee does not"* and rested McDonald's IN on a structure the franchisee cannot leave: the company *"maintains control of the underlying real estate"* at term end, with $31.5bn of contracted rent. **Wingstop has no such structure.** Its franchisee pays for the restaurant and its site (*"a low average initial investment of approximately $580,000, excluding real estate purchase or lease costs"*), signs *"a 10-year initial term, with an opportunity to enter into one or more renewal franchise agreements subject to certain conditions"*, and pays a percentage of sales. What the franchisee buys is access to the diner's demand for the Wingstop product. So the franchise, if there is one, is the diner's, and [E3-43]'s single-company test is read there first, then at the franchisee.

### THE STRONGEST EVIDENCE AGAINST THIS VERDICT, stated as its holders would state it [E4-51, E4-26, E3-47]
The verdict below is OUT, so the case for IN is set out first, at full strength.
1. *"Twenty-one straight years of same store sales growth, 2004 to 2024, and on the filer's word it came from customers, not price: 'driven primarily by an increase in transactions, which demonstrates the growing awareness and popularity of our brand'. Domestic AUV went from $1.13M to $2.14M in nine years. Over 2019-2025 Wingstop's comparable sales compounded +106.6% against +31.2% at Popeyes, +27.6% at Domino's US and +21.7% at El Pollo Loco. Customers who choose you that much more often than every filed rival do not regard the rivals as close substitutes."* **Answer:** this is the best fact in the file and it is accepted as demand: the diner wanted Wingstop more every year for two decades. But [E3-03]'s second criterion is proved, in [E3-43]'s own sentence, by *"a company’s ability to regularly price its product or service aggressively"*, and the filer's explanation of that record is traffic, not price: rising visits at a price customers regarded as good value is the opposite signature from pricing power, and it is what a wave looks like while it runs [E3-51]. The years that carry the record are the ones the screen row flags: 2020 (+21.4%) and 2023-2024 (+18.3%, +19.9%); remove those three years and the remaining four (2019, 2021, 2022, 2025) compound to about +20% (1.111 x 1.080 x 1.034 x 0.967), below every peer's full seven-year figure. The first year the diner's demand was tested, it fell (item 5 and THE READING).
2. *"Wingstop raised its price to its real customer, the franchisee, and the customer paid. The royalty went from 5% to 6% on agreements signed after July 2014, the advertising contribution from 2% of sales (2016) to 5.5% (2025), a total take on a new unit from about 7% to 11.5% of sales, and franchisees kept building: 'Existing franchisees accounted for more than 90% of franchised restaurants opened in each of 2024 and 2025', a record 493 net openings in FY2025, 'a franchisee unlevered cash-on-cash return of approximately 70%+' targeted in year two. That is [E3-43] at the altitude where Wingstop sets the price."* **Answer:** accepted as the franchise-level conduct, and it is why the verdict is not written lightly. Two limits. The royalty moved once, in 2014, and has stood at 6.0% on new agreements for eleven years, beside Domino's 5.5% (*"Each franchisee is generally required to pay a 5.5% royalty fee on sales"*), Papa John's 5% and Restaurant Brands' 3.0-6.0%: a rate at the top of the filed range, not beyond it. The advertising contribution is not Wingstop's price: it is spent on the franchisees' own advertising through *"a consolidated not-for-profit advertising fund"*, and the fund spent more than it collected (FY2025 advertising expense $261.5M against fees of $247.6M). And the franchisee's willingness to pay is derived from the diner's: when domestic AUV fell 11.5% from its FY2024 peak, domestic franchised openings fell from 206 in H1 2025 to 143 in H1 2026 (−31%), and the chief executive's first-quarter letter put *"enhancing unit economics for our brand partners"* first. A toll that rests on the traffic it tolls, without MCD's land, is the diner's franchise or none.
3. *"The returns are extraordinary. Operating income of $179.3M in FY2025 on property of $130.6M; the franchisees put up the capital for every new unit. [E3-46] and [E2-44]'s second characteristic, growth in dollar volume with only minor additional capital, are both met."* **Answer:** accepted as the return and as [E2-44] (2). But every franchisor in the row has the same structure (the franchisee funds the unit), so the return on Wingstop's own tangible capital does not separate it from Domino's or Papa John's; it measures the franchise model, not a moat inside it (the XPEL run's answer on the asset-light return). [E3-43] ties the return to the pricing; the pricing half is the half not shown.
4. *"The attacker's test [E2-45] points Wingstop's way. It is the one that attacked: the category's former public leader, Buffalo Wild Wings, printed franchised same-store sales of (2.7)% in 2016 and declines through 2017 while Wingstop grew 3.2% and 2.6%. The brand won the wing category."* **Answer:** accepted as the direction of the contest to 2024. It also shows what the category does to its leader: a wing brand with *"16 signature sauces"* and its own franchise system went from +6.5% franchised same-store sales (2012) to (2.7)% (2016) and filed its last periodic report in October 2017, which is the [E3-74] have-to-stay-smart class (*"If you’re doing something successful, they’re in your store the next day trying to figure out what it is about your success"*), and the filer says the same of itself: *"new or existing competitors will copy our business model, menu options, presentation, ambiance, marketing or advertising"*.
5. *"The 2025-2026 fall is the consumer cycle, [E3-30]'s 'aberrational cycle', not the moat: the filer names 'continued pressure on consumer spending', Popeyes fell 5.8% and Papa John's 7.4% in the same half-year, and a brand that grew 21 years running does not lose its franchise in six quarters."* **Answer:** part of it is accepted: the category was weak (Popeyes −5.8%, Papa John's North America franchised −7.4% in H1 2026). But the half-year did not fall on everyone: Domino's US +0.5% and El Pollo Loco, a chicken chain, **+4.9%** system-wide, against Wingstop −8.1%; the fall was in *"lower transaction volumes"* while the filer's own cost of bone-in wings was falling (so no price rise drove the diner away); and the response the chief executive filed was not a price held but *"our continued investments in value, flavor innovation and Smart Kitchen"* and the company's *"first customer loyalty program"*. Whether it is a cycle is Q6's question [E3-30]; what Q2 asks is whether the diner treated Wingstop as having no close substitute when money was short, and the filed answer is that the diner came less often while cheaper chicken sold more.

None of the five shows a price raised to the diner. The first and second are the strongest facts against this verdict and are carried into the reversal condition.

### THE READING
**[E3-43]'s own test is single-company: *"The existence of all three conditions will be demonstrated by a company’s ability to regularly price its product or service aggressively and thereby to earn high rates of return on capital."*** Wingstop shows the returns and the traffic; its filings do not show the pricing, and the two filed episodes that test it point the other way.
- **The system price is not filed. A search recorded here, not an absence claim beyond it:** every MD&A of the prospectus, the 10-Ks FY2015-FY2025, the 10-Qs of 2026 and the six releases Q1 2025-Q2 2026 was searched for *price increase, pricing, menu price, average ticket, average check, transaction size* (`pricing_search.txt`). **No system-wide menu-price figure and no price-versus-transactions split for the system was found in any of them**; the one sentence that defines the terms, *"Transaction size increases are driven by price increases or favorable mix shift from either an increase in items purchased or shifts into higher priced items"*, recurs every year without a number. The filer does not file the one series ([E4-55]'s units against price) that would show pricing power; CMG files it every year and passed Q2 on it.
- **The one price series that is filed runs with the commodity, not ahead of it.** At the company restaurants, the food cost ratio moved in the same direction as the filer's stated bone-in wing cost in **every year FY2013-FY2024, twelve of twelve** (table above): the restaurant, not the diner, bore each swing. The test year is **FY2021**, *"a 40.9% increase in the cost of bone-in chicken wings, representing record-high wing inflation"*, in a year of rising demand (domestic same store sales +8.0% on top of +21.4%): company-restaurant same store sales rose **3.4%**, *"driven by an increase in average ticket"*, and the food cost ratio went from **35.9% to 44.8%**. Price was raised, and by a fraction of the cost. (Limit: 57 company restaurants of 2,586 domestic; franchisees set their own menu prices, *"If menu prices are increased by us and our franchisees"*, so this is a sample of the system's pricing, not all of it.)
- **The filer answers [E4-37]'s question in writing** (*"if we raised the price 10 cents a pound, would sales fall off a cliff?"*): *"If menu prices are increased by us and our franchisees to cover increased labor costs, the higher prices could adversely affect transactions which could lower sales and thereby reduce our margins and the royalties that we receive from franchisees."* (FY2024 and FY2025 10-Ks), and every 10-K FY2020-FY2025 warns that *"we may not be able to anticipate or successfully react to changing food costs, including the price of bone-in chicken wings, by adjusting our purchasing practices, increasing our menu prices to pass along commodity price increases to our customers or making other operational adjustments"* (FY2021 text; the FY2025 text is the same with a page number inside it).
- **The first flat-demand test in the record, [E2-44] (1), failed.** [E2-44] asks for *"an ability to increase prices rather easily (even when product demand is flat and capacity is not fully utilized) without fear of significant loss of either market share or unit volume"*. The record had no flat year from 2004 to 2024. In the first, the filer reports the loss of unit volume (*"a 7.5% decline in domestic same store sales contributed by lower transaction volumes, reflecting continued pressure on consumer spending"*, Q2 2026 release; Q1 2026, 8.7%, the same words), the guidance moved down three times in five months (*"Flat to low-single digit domestic same store sales growth"*, 2026-02-18; *"Low-single digit decline"*, 2026-04-29; *"A decline of 4% to 6%"*, 2026-07-29), and the response was *"investments in value"*, not price. **Direction [E4-32]: narrowing.** Domestic AUV $2.138M → $1.893M (−11.5%), transactions falling in the filer's words, franchised openings −31%.

**[E3-03] criterion by criterion.**
- **(1) needed or desired: passes**, strongly: 3,255 restaurants and $5.3bn of system sales.
- **(2) thought by its customers to have no close substitute: fails, on the filed conduct and in the filer's words.** The filer: *"The restaurant industry in general, and the fast casual category in particular, are intensely competitive, and we compete with many well-established restaurant companies on the basis of food taste and quality, price, service, value, location, convenience, digital engagement, delivery and overall customer experience."* and *"Our competitors include individual restaurants and restaurant chains that range from independent local operators to well-capitalized national and regional restaurant companies, including restaurants offering chicken wings, tenders, and chicken sandwiches"*. When money was short the diner substituted: Wingstop −8.1% while El Pollo Loco +4.9% and Domino's +0.5%. The 21-year record shows a product customers chose more often, at a price that was not pressed; it does not show a product for which they would pay more rather than go elsewhere.
- **(3) not subject to price regulation: passes.**
- **[E2-58] is not argued**: no cost advantage is filed (Wingstop buys chicken at *"prevailing market conditions"* like its rivals; *"there are no established fixed price markets for fresh bone-in chicken wings"*), so the low-cost door is not its door. The CAT, GWW, ABT and ABBV operator questions do not bear: one segment, no government price, no cost-advantage claim.
