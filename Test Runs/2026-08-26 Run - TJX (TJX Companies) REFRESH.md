## ON "A WONDERFUL BUSINESS AT A FAIR PRICE" — WHY THAT PATH IS ALSO SHUT HERE
The wonderful-at-fair path is the FAIR line ($71.84) and the Statute starter, not the
CHEAP line. It was tested and it does not open:
- **Statute (Book One), the wonderful-at-fair route:** fails on every anchor. The most
  generous framing, the most recent tier at 3.11%, is still 208bp **below** the risk-free
  rate. The price at which the most-recent tier clears the sovereign is **$82.52**.
- **FAIR (Book Two):** $71.84 against $137.75. Relaxing from CHEAP to FAIR closes only
  20 points of a 92-point gap. **The gap is not a margin-of-safety question.**

**Coca-Cola 1988, the control case for paying a full price for a wonderful business:**

| | Coke 1988 | TJX today |
|---|---|---|
| Multiple | ~15× earnings | **28.3× GAAP EPS / 32.2× OE** |
| Earnings yield | ~6.7% | **3.11%** |
| Long bond | ~9.0% | 5.19% |
| Yield ÷ bond rate | **0.74×** | **0.60×** |

Buffett's most famous full-price purchase of a wonderful business was at fifteen times
earnings, at 74% of the bond rate. TJX is at roughly twice that multiple and 60% of the
sovereign. **The Coca-Cola precedent does not stretch to this price.** For scale only,
and explicitly **not** a framework line: TJX would sit at Coke's 1988 yield-to-bond ratio
at about **$110.85**.

The honest summary of the wonderful-at-fair doctrine applied here: Munger's point is that
you should not demand a bargain from a great business, and that is right. But "do not
demand a bargain" is not "pay any price." At a 0.30-point equity premium you are not
paying a full price for a wonderful business, you are accepting a bond-like return with
equity risk attached. That is the error the doctrine is meant to prevent in the other
direction, and Ruling 4-C requires checking both directions on every constant.
