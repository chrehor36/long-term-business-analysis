## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? [E3-31]

- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language.** Lowe's owns 1,759 big-box
  stores averaging 112,000 square feet of selling space, **89% of them owned outright**
  (including stores on leased land), sited over sixty years in the parking-lot positions
  that serve American suburbs. It buys roughly 37,000 items — lumber, appliances, paint,
  plumbing, tools — from vendors worldwide, marks them up **33.5%**, and sells them to
  **780 million** customer visits a year at an average ticket of **$106**. Out of that
  33.5% gross margin it spends 19.5 points running the stores and 2.3 points depreciating
  them, leaving **11.8 points of operating margin**. Suppliers finance a large part of the
  inventory ($9,762M of payables against $17,300M of stock). Interest takes 1.6 points and
  tax 2.4, so **7.7 cents of every dollar of sales becomes net income**. The business
  converts that into roughly **$9.9bn of operating cash a year**, spends **$2.2bn** keeping
  the stores and the supply chain current, and returns the rest — historically as
  buybacks, latterly $2.6bn a year as dividends and, in FY2025, $10.1bn on two acquisitions.
  There is no annuity, no subscription, no float, no deferred-fee pool. **Every dollar has
  to be sold again next year.**
- **The scarce input this business controls: real estate and the drive time attached to it.**
  Not the merchandise — the filing itself says "alternative and competitive suppliers are
  available for virtually all of our products." Not the brands. What is scarce is 1,759
  large-format sites with parking, loading, garden centre and highway access, in
  neighbourhoods where the land is now zoned and priced to make a new one impossible; and
  the ten-minute drive that makes a $40 emergency plumbing trip go to Lowe's rather than
  anywhere else. That is a genuinely scarce, genuinely owned asset. It is also, precisely,
  a **local** asset — which is why the moat is measured store by store at Q2 and not as a
  national aggregate.
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** Yes. Americans will still
  own 145 million housing units with a median age near forty; those units will still need
  roofs, water heaters, paint and lumber; the heavy, bulky, urgent half of that demand will
  still be bought within driving distance. The format has been essentially unchanged since
  the 1990s and survived Amazon's first twenty-five years intact. The residual uncertainty
  — how much of the Pro half of the market migrates to specialist distribution — is named
  at Q2 and carried as the structural death at Q4.
- **[E4-46] check:** this decision does not need five months. It is one of the most
  intelligible businesses on the exchange.
- **VERDICT: [x] IN**

## Q2 — IS IT A FRANCHISE? [E3-03]

**The question as posed: is a two-player big-box duopoly a franchise?** The answer this run
reaches is that **the format is a franchise and Lowe's holds the weaker half of it**, and
that the honest series says the half is getting weaker. Class **NARROW**, direction
**NARROWING**. Worked below.

**[E3-03], the three criteria:**
- **Needed or desired [x]** — 780 million transactions a year against an aged housing stock;
  a material share of the spend (a failed water heater, a leaking roof) is not discretionary.
- **No close substitute — [ ] FAILED on the issuer's own words, and recorded as such.**
  Lowe's Item 1 "Our Competition" enumerates its substitutes at length: "national and
  regional home improvement warehouse chains and lumber yards … traditional hardware,
  plumbing, electrical, drywall and home supply retailers and distributors, as well as paint
  stores, lumber yards, garden centers, and maintenance and repair organizations … general
  merchandise retailers, home goods specialty stores, warehouse clubs, online retailers,
  other specialty retailers, providers of equipment and tool rental, service providers …
  and wholesalers," in a market it calls "**highly fragmented**." A 2×4 has close
  substitutes everywhere. **What has no close substitute is the site, not the product** —
  and that is a local monopoly of convenience, not a product franchise. This is the
  narrowness, stated at the top rather than buried.
- **Not price-regulated [x].**

**Must the moat be continuously rebuilt? [E4-04]** No — it must be continuously *defended*,
which [E5-23] prescribes for every moat. The basis (owned sites, local density, supplier
scale) is not a depleting asset being periodically replaced. But note what the FY2025
capital account actually did: $2.2bn of capex defending the same advantage, and **$10.1bn
buying a different one** (Pro distribution). The [E4-04] test — "does the spending defend
the same advantage, or buy its replacement?" — returns *both*, and the $10.1bn is the
larger number. Recorded, and scored at Q3.

**Does success depend on a great manager? [E4-23], the Mayo test.** No single surgeon.
Lowe's has had four CEOs since 2005 and the format survived all four, including a
well-documented mediocre stretch. The dependence is on *ordinary daily competence at 1,759
sites*, which is the [E3-38] class and is recorded as the Q3 weight case, where it belongs —
not here as a compliment to anyone.

- **[E2-53], the dominance class.** Does position, not execution, set the economics —

  24.7% pre-tax. **These are genuinely good returns and they are the strongest pro-franchise
  fact in the file.** They are also declining, and below HD's 36.6% on the same construction.
- **[E2-45], the attacker's test.** With ample capital and skilled personnel: attacking *the

  urgent and advice-dependent, which is why Amazon has not taken the category in
  twenty-five years of trying. **Attacking Lowe's specifically is far easier, and the
  attacker already exists.** Home Depot has run that attack continuously for forty years
  with a better hand and is now extending it into the growing half of the market at 5x
  Lowe's scale. Beneath them, the specialists take one aisle each — Floor & Decor

- **[E4-32], direction — and it outranks existence.** Absolute direction: **narrowing** —

- **[E4-36], which cause of extreme success?** Not extreme maximisation of a variable

  business riding a fifty-year wave of American suburban home formation** — and [E3-51] is

  surfer." Part of what the last five years have shown is the wave flattening.

2. **Pro share loss to Home Depot — the structural death.**

6. **The shareholder's death that is not the company's.** At 17.5x guided earnings and a
