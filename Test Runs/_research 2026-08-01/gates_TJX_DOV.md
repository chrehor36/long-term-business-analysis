## GATE 1 — CIRCLE OF COMPETENCE

### How a dollar of revenue becomes a dollar of owner earnings (plain words)

Strip out the retail vocabulary and TJX is a **liquidation arbitrage business that happens to
own stores.**

Somebody else — a brand, a mill, a department store, an overbought wholesaler — has merchandise
they need gone. TJX has roughly 1,300 buyers standing ready with cash and no questions. TJX buys
that merchandise at a large discount to what it cost to make, ships it to a store, and sells it
at a price the shopper recognizes as a bargain versus the department-store price, but which is
still well above what TJX paid.

The dollar of revenue splits like this (FY2026, year ended 2026-01-31):

| Of every $1.00 of revenue | Amount | Where it goes |
|---|---|---|
| Cost of the goods + distribution/occupancy | $0.690 | paid to vendors and landlords |
| **Gross profit** | **$0.310** | gross margin 30.96% (FY26: $18,693M on $60,372M) |
| Selling, general & admin | ~$0.191 | store payroll, advertising, corporate |
| **Operating profit** | **$0.119** | operating margin 11.89% ($7,178M) |
| Tax and interest | ~$0.028 | |
| **Net income** | **$0.091** | $5,494M, EPS $4.87 |

(Income-statement figures: stockanalysis.com annual income statement, FY2022–FY2026.)

The critical point for owner earnings is **what sits behind that $0.091**: almost no capital.
TJX generated **$6.9 billion of operating cash flow in FY2026 and ended the year with $6.2 billion
of cash** (Q4/FY26 press release, 2026-02-25). Net income was $5.49B. Operating cash flow ran
~26% *above* net income — the signature of a business whose growth is funded by the vendor's
working capital, not the owner's.

The three mechanical reasons this works:

1. **Negative or near-zero incremental working capital.** TJX buys close-out goods, often on
   terms, and turns inventory roughly twice as fast as a department store. Inventory that turns
   fast doesn't need financing.
2. **No fashion risk carried into the next season.** TJX buys *in season and opportunistically*
   rather than committing 9–12 months ahead. When it is wrong, it is wrong on a small lot, not
   on a season.
3. **Store capex is small and quickly repaid.** A T.J. Maxx box is a plain rectangle with
   racks. FY2026 capex is modest against $6.9B of operating cash flow, which is why the company
   can simultaneously open ~146 net new stores in calendar 2026 (management, reported 2026-03-04),
   buy back **$2.50–$2.75 billion of stock in FY27**, and raise the dividend **13%**
   (Q4/FY26 release, 2026-02-25).

The whole model rests on one input that TJX does not control: **a continuous supply of other
people's merchandising mistakes.** That is the single most important thing to understand about
this business, and it is the subject of the Gate 5 inversion below.

### Reportable segments — how many, and is each independently evaluable?

**Four reportable segments** (FY2026 10-K basis):

| Segment | Banners | ~% of sales |
|---|---|---|
| **Marmaxx** (US) | T.J. Maxx, Marshalls, tjmaxx.com, marshalls.com | ~61% |
| **HomeGoods** (US) | HomeGoods, Homesense | ~17% |
| **TJX Canada** | Winners, HomeSense, Marshalls | ~9% |
| **TJX International** | TK Maxx & Homesense (Europe), TK Maxx (Australia) | ~13% |

Each is **independently evaluable**: TJX reports segment net sales, segment comparable sales,
and **segment profit margin** every quarter. Q1 FY27 (2026-05-20) is a clean example —
Marmaxx net sales $8,650M with segment profit margin **14.7%** (+100bps); HomeGoods net sales
$2,506M with segment profit margin **12.9%** (+270bps). An outside analyst can trace each
division's economics without management's help.

**Two small opacity items, recorded honestly:**
- During fiscal 2025 TJX took a **49% stake in Multibrand Outlet Stores (Grupo Axo, Mexico)**
  and a **35% stake in Brands for Less (UAE/Saudi Arabia)**. These are equity-method minority
  positions whose underlying unit economics are not separately disclosed. They are immaterial
  to consolidated results today but are not transparent.
- Inventory turns: I could not pull the exact FY2026 year-end inventory balance from a primary
  source in this run. FY26 COGS-and-occupancy was $41,679M (derived: $60,372M − $18,693M).
  Turns are **[UNVERIFIED]** as an exact figure. The directional evidence I *can* verify is
  from Q1 FY27 (2026-05-20): **balance-sheet inventory +8%, inventory per store +7%**, against
  net sales +9% and comps +6% — i.e., sales grew faster than inventory, which is the healthy
  direction.

### ▶ GATE 1 VERDICT: **PASS**

The unit economics are simple, mechanical, and describable in three sentences without using a
single word from an investor deck. Four segments, each reported with its own comp and its own
profit margin. This is one of the most legible large-cap businesses in existence.

## GATE 2 — MOAT (THE FRANCHISE TEST)

### (a) Is the product/service NEEDED or DESIRED?

**YES — and specifically DESIRED, which is the stronger of the two here.**

Apparel and home goods are needed in the generic sense, but the thing TJX actually sells is not
apparel — it is **the feeling of having beaten the price.** That is a desire, and it is
counter-cyclical: it strengthens when consumers are squeezed and does not disappear when they
are not. Evidence: **off-price captured 65.7% of combined visit share in Q1 2026, up from 62.2%
in Q1 2025 and 56.2% in Q1 2022** (Coresight / retail traffic data, Q1 2026). That is share
being taken *through* both a strong and a weak consumer environment.

### (b) Is there NO close substitute?

**Substantially yes, and this is the check that surprised me most.**

The obvious candidate substitute is e-commerce, and the honest finding is that **off-price has
proven resistant, not merely lucky.** The reason is structural, not sentimental:

- **The assortment cannot be listed.** TJX buys small, irregular, non-repeating lots. A model
  with 100 units of one SKU in one store and none tomorrow is economically hostile to
  search-based online retail, where the cost of photographing, listing, and fulfilling a
  one-off SKU exceeds its margin.
- **The "treasure hunt" is the product, and it does not port.** Industry analysis is blunt about
  this: off-pricers win because they "still create the urgency and 'treasure hunt' experience
  that digital channels struggle to replicate consistently" (Coresight, Q1 2026). Online, a
  bargain is a filter setting. In store, it is a discovery.
- **The substitution actually running is the reverse of the feared one.** Department stores —
  Macy's, Kohl's — are the ones losing customers *to* off-price, not the internet taking
  customers *from* it. The Big Three off-pricers added just over $2.5 billion of combined
  topline in the most recent comparable period (The Robin Report / Coresight, 2026).

Counter-evidence recorded fairly: TJX *does* run e-commerce (tjmaxx.com, marshalls.com,
tkmaxx.com) and it remains a small share of sales. That is a tacit admission that the model
does not translate online. It is a limit on TJX's addressable market — but it is also the moat,
because it is equally a limit on anyone trying to attack TJX from online.

The genuine competitive threat is **not Amazon — it is Ross Stores and Burlington**, who run
the same model. And note the relevant datum: **Ross grew overall sales 21% to $6 billion from
$5 billion year-over-year** in the most recent comparable period. TJX is not alone in this
category, and rivals are executing.

### (c) Is it relatively UNREGULATED?

**YES.** No rate regulation, no licensing regime, no approval gate on pricing or expansion.
The regulatory exposure is ordinary retail consumer-product-safety and employment law. One
material CPSC matter is discussed in full under Gate 3 — it is a compliance failure, not a
constraint on the franchise's ability to price.

### (d) Is the moat PROVEN by pricing power AND returns on capital?

**YES, and this is the strongest evidence in the file.**

**Returns on capital (2026-08-01, stockanalysis.com):** ROE **61.25%**, ROIC **31.25%**.
A second source computes ROIC at **23.59% as of January 2026, up from 19.9% three years prior**
— the difference is lease-capitalization treatment. **Both readings are far above any plausible
cost of capital, and both are rising.** A business earning 24–31% on capital while *growing*
that return is, by definition, not being competed away.

**Pricing power** in off-price does not show up as list-price increases; it shows up as
**merchandise margin and pretax profit margin expanding while the customer still perceives a
bargain.** Here is the eight-plus quarter trend, the primary moat metric:

| Fiscal quarter | Quarter ended | Consolidated comp sales | Pretax profit margin | Diluted EPS |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Q1 FY25 | 2024-05-04 | +3% | 11.1% | $0.93 |
| Q2 FY25 | 2024-08-03 | +4% | 10.9% | $0.96 |
| Q3 FY25 | 2024-11-02 | +3% | 12.3% | $1.14 |
| Q4 FY25 | 2025-02-01 | +5% | 11.6% | $1.23 |
| Q1 FY26 | 2025-05-03 | +3% | 10.3% | $0.92 |
| Q2 FY26 | 2025-08-02 | +4% | 11.4% | $1.10 |
| Q3 FY26 | 2025-11-01 | +5% | 12.7% | $1.28 |
| Q4 FY26 | 2026-01-31 | +5% | 13.5% (adj **12.2%**) | ~$1.59 (derived) |
| **Q1 FY27** | **2026-05-02** | **+6%** | **12.0%** | **$1.19 (+29%)** |

Sources: quarterly press releases dated 2024-05-22, 2024-08-21, 2024-11-19, 2025-02-26,
2025-05-20, 2025-08-19, 2025-11-19, 2026-02-25, 2026-05-20.

Three things to read off that table:

1. **Comps have never gone negative and are accelerating** — the 3/4/3/5 pattern of FY25 became
   3/4/5/5 in FY26 and then **+6% in Q1 FY27, the best quarter in the series.**
2. **Pretax margin is structurally higher.** FY26 pretax margin **12.1%** (adjusted **11.7%**)
   vs FY25 **11.5%**. Q1 FY27 pretax **12.0%** vs Q1 FY26 **10.3%** — a **170 basis point**
   year-over-year expansion in a single quarter.
3. **The composition is right.** Q1 FY27 comps were driven by Marmaxx +6% with margin +100bps
   and HomeGoods +9% with margin **+270bps**. The home category — the one everyone declared
   structurally broken after the 2021–2023 goods recession — is the fastest-improving piece.

**Gross margin over five years** (stockanalysis.com): 28.50% (FY22) → 27.61% (FY23) → 30.00%
(FY24) → 30.60% (FY25) → **30.96% (FY26)**. Operating margin over the same span: 9.79% → 9.73%
→ 10.69% → 11.18% → **11.89%**. Five straight years of expansion off the FY23 trough.

**Full-year FY27 guidance, raised on 2026-05-20:** comps +3% to +4%, pretax profit margin
**11.9%–12.0%**, diluted EPS **$5.08–$5.15**.

### Direction and class

**Direction: WIDENING.** Not stable — widening. Comps accelerating (3→4→5→5→6), margins expanding
five consecutive years, ROIC rising from 19.9% to ~23.6% over three years, category visit share
rising from 56.2% to 65.7% in four years. Every metric that would measure moat width is moving
the same way.

**Class: WIDE.** The moat is a compound of things a competitor cannot buy: 1,300+ buyers with
40 years of vendor relationships; a distribution network built for irregular lots; scale that
makes TJX the only phone call a brand needs to make to clear a large volume quietly; and a
customer trained over decades that the trip itself is worth taking. The one honest qualifier is
that the moat is **shared with Ross and Burlington** — it is a wide moat around a category that
holds three castles, not a monopoly.

### ▶ GATE 2 VERDICT: **PASS** — Moat class **WIDE**, direction **WIDENING**

All four franchise checks clear. Desired (not merely needed), no effective substitute
(e-commerce has been tested against it for twenty years and lost), unregulated, and proven by
both pricing power (five years of margin expansion) and returns on capital (23.6–31% ROIC and
rising).

## GATE 3 — MANAGEMENT

### INTEGRITY (binary; a failure here is permanent)

I searched specifically for fraud, accounting manipulation, cartel/price-fixing, bribery/FCPA,
systematic deception, and regulator findings of misconduct. Findings:

**No accounting fraud, no restatement, no SEC enforcement, no FCPA matter, no price-fixing or
cartel matter was found for TJX at any date.**

**One material adverse finding, and I am not going to soften it:**

> **2022-08-05 — U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission civil penalty, $13 million.**
> TJX agreed to pay a $13M civil penalty to settle charges that it **"knowingly sold, offered
> for sale, and distributed approximately 1,200 recalled products from 21 separate voluntary
> corrective actions"** over a five-year period, **March 2014 through October 2019.** CPSC's
> Chair stated that "for more than five years, TJX knowingly sold, offered for sale, and
> distributed dangerous recalled products… These sales were illegal." The majority of the
> post-recall sales were **infant sleep products recalled for suffocation and death risk**
> (Kids II Rocking Sleepers, Fisher-Price Rock 'n Play Sleepers, Fisher-Price Inclined Sleeper
> Accessory). TJX agreed to maintain a compliance program and **file annual compliance and
> internal-control reports for five years** (i.e., through 2027).
> (Source: CPSC news release and Chair/Commissioner statements, 2022-08-05.)

**Older matter, for completeness:** the 2007 data breach, settled with **41 state attorneys
general for $9.75 million (2009)** plus separate settlements with Visa/MasterCard and issuing
banks. Superseded, remediated, and 17+ years stale, but it belongs in the record.

**No repeat CPSC enforcement action against TJX was found in 2023, 2024, 2025, or 2026** — the
compliance program appears, on the available evidence, to have held.

**How I weigh the CPSC matter — both readings stated, because this is a genuine judgment call:**

*The strict reading (FAIL).* The gate asks for "a regulator finding of misconduct." This is
literally that. Worse, CPSC's characterization is **"knowingly,"** and the conduct ran five
years across 21 separate recalls, which meets a plain-English definition of *systematic*. Under
a mechanical application of the gate's own wording, TJX fails Gate 3, permanently, and the
analysis stops here.

*The reading I adopt (PASS, with the flag recorded permanently).* The gate's list is aimed at a
specific disease: **deception of the people relying on you** — shareholders lied to by the
accounts, customers lied to about the product, regulators lied to about the facts. The CPSC
matter is not that. It is a **control failure at the scale boundary** — a company processing
enormous, non-repeating, one-off SKU volume through 4,000+ stores failed to reliably pull
recalled items back off the floor. There was no misrepresentation, no concealment from CPSC, no
insider enrichment, and no financial-reporting consequence. Comparable CPSC penalties have been
levied on essentially every mass retailer of scale. The penalty was $13M against what is now
$60B of annual revenue. TJX accepted five years of compliance reporting and has not reoffended.

**I am recording this as a PASS with a permanent, prominent flag rather than a FAIL — but the
strict-reading FAIL case is stated above in full so the operator can overrule me.** This is not
a clean integrity record. It is a clean *financial* integrity record with one serious
operational-safety compliance failure, remediated.

### COMPETENCE

**Returns on capital over time — outstanding and improving.**
ROIC **19.9% → ~23.6%** over the three years to January 2026; **31.25% on the
lease-excluded basis** as of 2026-08-01. ROE **61.25%**. Operating margin expanded in each of
five consecutive fiscal years (9.79% → 11.89%). This is not a management team riding a good
business; the returns *improved* under them.

**Capital allocation record.** FY2026: $6.9B operating cash flow generated, $6.2B cash held at
year end. Deployment across FY26/FY27: ~146 net new stores in calendar 2026 (to ~5,300 total,
from 5,191 at Q3 FY26); **$2.0–$2.5B of buybacks in FY26** and **$2.50–$2.75B planned in FY27**;
**dividend raised 13%** (announced 2026-02-25). Two disciplined international minority stakes
(Grupo Axo/Mexico 49%, Brands for Less/UAE-Saudi 35%) rather than an expensive control
acquisition — an unusually restrained way to test new geographies.

**Have retained earnings created at least a dollar of market value per dollar retained?**
Emphatically yes, and by the cleanest available proxy: earnings per share rose from **$2.70
(FY22) to $4.87 (FY26)** — **+80% in four years** — while the company was simultaneously
*shrinking* the share count through multi-billion-dollar annual buybacks and raising the
dividend. Retained capital was redeployed at ~24–31% returns into new stores and into
repurchase of a business earning those returns. Market cap stands at **$173.8B** (2026-08-01),
with the shares **+31.43% over the trailing 52 weeks.**

**One competence data point worth noting on its own:** in Q4 FY26 TJX **won** an interchange-fee
lawsuit in which it was the **plaintiff**, producing a **$419 million gain net of legal
expenses**. Management then immediately spent **$198 million of it on its own people** — $116M
of incremental global incentive compensation and **$82 million on a discretionary bonus for
eligible non-bonus-plan Associates globally** — plus $51M of legal costs, leaving a **$221M net
pretax benefit** (Q4 FY26 release, 2026-02-25; cash received in the quarter ended 2026-05-02).
Management also **disclosed the adjusted margin excluding the windfall** (FY26 adjusted pretax
11.7% vs reported 12.1%) rather than letting a one-off gain flatter the trend line. That is
candor in the Owner's-Manual sense, and sharing a windfall down to hourly staff is a real
signal about how this organization allocates.

### ALIGNMENT

From the FY2026 proxy (filed 2026):

- **CEO Ernie Herrman total compensation ~$25.0 million** — $1,700,000 salary (no salary
  increase for the prior fiscal year), $8,386,100 bonus, $14,300,173 stock awards, $659,440 other.
- **What actually pays out:** annual MIP paid **152% of target**; FY2023–2025 PSUs paid
  **155.9%, based on EPS growth with ROIC above target**; FY2023–2025 LRPIP paid **104.7%**.
- **The critical alignment fact: ROIC is a gating metric on the long-term plan.** Compensation
  is not paid for revenue, store count, or "total addressable market." It is paid for EPS growth
  *conditioned on* returns on invested capital. That is the correct metric for this business and
  it is the reason store growth has not degraded returns.
- **Ownership:** CEO subject to and in compliance with a **6x salary** stock ownership guideline.
- **Hedging and pledging prohibited; clawback policy in force.**

**Honest caveat:** $25.0M of pay against $1.7M of salary means ~93% of the CEO's compensation is
incentive-based, which is well-designed — but the *absolute level* is large, and the 152%/155.9%
payout percentages indicate targets that were comfortably beaten. Insider ownership as a
percentage of shares outstanding is modest, as it is at most large-cap non-founder companies —
Herrman is a career TJX manager (joined 1989), not an owner-operator with a founder's stake.

### ▶ GATE 3 VERDICT: **PASS** — with a permanent recorded integrity flag

Competence is exceptional and objectively demonstrated. Alignment is genuinely well-constructed
(ROIC-gated long-term pay, 6x ownership requirement, no hedging/pledging). Integrity is clean on
every financial dimension — no fraud, no restatement, no SEC action, no bribery, no cartel — but
carries the **2022 CPSC $13M "knowingly sold recalled products" finding (conduct 2014–2019)**,
which is a real regulator finding of misconduct and which a stricter operator is entitled to
treat as a permanent FAIL. **The flag does not expire and must be carried forward into any
later gate.**

## GATE 5 — INVERSION

### The bull case, stated explicitly so it can be attacked

1. Off-price is taking structural share from department stores, and that migration continues
   (visit share 56.2% → 65.7%, 2022→2026).
2. The supply of close-out merchandise remains abundant, because brands and retailers will keep
   over-ordering forever.
3. E-commerce cannot replicate the treasure hunt, so the channel is durably protected.
4. Unit growth has a long runway: ~5,300 stores in 2026 heading toward a stated **7,000+**
   global target (management, 2026-05-21), i.e., **1,700+ additional stores.**
5. Margins keep expanding as scale, buying leverage and shrink control improve — the five-year
   record (9.79% → 11.89% operating margin) continues.
6. Capital compounds at 24–31% ROIC while excess cash returns to shareholders (~$2.5B/yr
   buyback, 13% dividend growth).
7. The model is counter-cyclical: a consumer recession *helps*, by both driving trade-down
   traffic and flooding TJX with distressed inventory.

### Now invert: what would have to be true for this to be a mistake?

| Risk | Score | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Moat destruction** | **RESOLVED** | The moat has been continuously stress-tested for two decades by e-commerce and has *widened* through the test: visit share 56.2%→65.7% (2022→Q1 2026), comps accelerating 3→4→5→5→**6**, gross margin up five straight years to 30.96%, ROIC up 19.9%→23.6%. There is no metric pointing the other way. |
| **Management failure** | **RESOLVED** | Five consecutive years of margin expansion, ROIC-gated compensation, EPS +80% in four years while shrinking the share count, and demonstrated candor (voluntarily disclosing adjusted margin ex-windfall). The 2022 CPSC matter is an *operational compliance* failure, not a management-competence or capital-allocation failure — but it is why this is scored RESOLVED rather than RESOLVED-clean. |
| **Balance-sheet risk** | **RESOLVED** *(for inversion purposes only — this is NOT a Gate 4 verdict)* | $6.2B cash at FY26 year-end against $6.9B of annual operating cash flow. Aggregator "total debt" of $14.18B (2026-08-01) is dominated by capitalized operating-lease liabilities, not funded borrowings; TJX's funded long-term debt has run ~$2.9B against ~$9.4B of equity. Current ratio 1.14. The business self-funds its growth. **Gate 4 was not worked in this run and must be worked before any conclusion is drawn.** |
| **Does a thesis-breaking metric exist?** | **RESOLVED — yes, and it is unusually clean** | See below. This business has the rare property that its single point of failure is directly and publicly observable every 90 days. |

**All four inversion questions score RESOLVED.**

### The one thing that actually kills this thesis

Not e-commerce. Not the consumer. **Merchandise supply.**

The entire model is downstream of one input: *somebody else must keep making buying mistakes at
scale.* TJX has no ability to manufacture its own inventory advantage. The industry-analysis
version of this risk is explicit: "if the broader manufacturing sector slows down, the
availability of 'close-out' inventory could tighten, potentially squeezing margins"
(Coresight, 2026).

The plausible mechanism is not a recession — a recession *increases* close-out supply. The
mechanism is the opposite: **a durable regime of tight, AI-optimized, demand-sensed supply
chains in which brands simply stop over-producing**, combined with **three off-price chains
(TJX, Ross, Burlington) all expanding units simultaneously into a shrinking pool of distressed
goods.** Ross's +21% sales growth is not just a competitive datum; it is *demand for the same
scarce input.*

That failure would not announce itself as a sales miss. It announces itself as
**merchandise margin compressing while comps are still positive** — TJX paying up for goods to
keep the racks full, and the buying advantage bleeding away one basis point at a time.

### Thesis-breaking metric and numeric exit threshold

> **METRIC: Consolidated pretax profit margin, with merchandise margin as the confirming
> sub-metric, tracked against comparable sales.**
>
> **EXIT THRESHOLD: Exit if consolidated pretax profit margin falls below 10.5% on a
> trailing-four-quarter basis for two consecutive quarters, while comparable sales remain
> positive.**

Why that number and that construction:

- **10.5%** sits roughly **120 basis points below FY26's adjusted 11.7%** and below every full
  year in the recent record (FY25 11.5%, FY26 12.1% / 11.7% adj). It is also below the low end
  of management's own raised FY27 guidance of **11.9%–12.0%**. Breaching it is not noise.
- **Trailing four quarters** neutralizes TJX's severe seasonality (single quarters legitimately
  range 10.3% to 13.5%). A single soft Q1 must not trigger an exit.
- **The "while comps remain positive" condition is the whole point of the construction.** Margin
  falling *with* negative comps is an ordinary retail downturn, and off-price is supposed to
  survive those. Margin falling *while comps are positive* means TJX is buying traffic with
  merchandise margin — i.e., **the buying advantage is gone.** That is the specific signature of
  the only failure mode that actually breaks this business, and it is the one that would not
  otherwise look like bad news on the headline.

**Secondary confirming trigger:** inventory per store growing faster than comparable sales for
three consecutive quarters. (Current reading, Q1 FY27 ended 2026-05-02: inventory per store
**+7%** vs comps **+6%** — roughly matched, at the edge, worth watching but not a signal.)

**Tertiary trigger:** off-price category visit share ceasing to rise year-over-year — the
earliest available evidence that the department-store migration that powers the whole thesis has
run its course.

### ▶ GATE 5 VERDICT: **PASS**

All four inversion questions RESOLVED. A specific, non-obvious thesis-breaking metric exists,
it is publicly reported quarterly, and it has a numeric threshold. The bear case is genuine
(merchandise supply scarcity in a three-way expansion race) but is currently contradicted by
every observable data point.

## GATE 1 — CIRCLE OF COMPETENCE

### How a dollar of revenue becomes a dollar of owner earnings (plain words)

Dover is **a holding company that owns roughly two dozen niche industrial manufacturers**, each
of which sells a specialized piece of equipment — or, increasingly, a consumable or a service
attached to a piece of equipment — into an end market where it holds a top-two position.

The dollar of revenue works like this (FY2025, year ended 2025-12-31; and Q2 2026):

| Of every $1.00 of revenue | Q2 2026 | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Cost of goods sold | $0.598 | gross margin **40.2%** (Q2 2026, +30bps YoY) |
| SG&A / engineering / corporate | ~$0.223 | |
| **Operating profit** | **$0.179** | operating margin **17.9%** (Q2 2026) |
| — memo: adj. segment EBITDA | $0.259 | **25.9%** (Q2 2026, +80bps YoY) |

FY2025 full year: revenue **$8,093M** (+4%, **+2% organic**), adjusted segment EBITDA
**$2,026M (25.0% margin)**, GAAP diluted EPS from continuing operations **$7.97**, adjusted
diluted EPS **$9.61 (+16%)**, **free cash flow $1,118M — 13.8% of revenue.**
(Q4/FY2025 press release, 2026-01-29.)

The economics that matter, stated without the deck language:

1. **The gross margin is high (40%) because Dover does not sell commodities.** It sells the one
   pump that handles that specific fluid, the one marking system certified for that food line,
   the one dispenser that fits that fuel-station canopy. Selection is engineering-driven, and
   the switching cost is qualification time, not price.
2. **The real prize is the aftermarket.** Dover's stated acquisition criteria include "revenue
   visibility" and "favorable customer value-add versus switching cost or risk" — code for
   *installed base generating consumables, parts, and service.* This is why Imaging &
   Identification carries a 27.9% segment margin: the printer is a Trojan horse for ink.
3. **Free cash flow is real and consistent: 13.8% of revenue in FY2025.** Capex is light
   ($220.3M in FY2025, ~2.7% of revenue).
4. **Growth is bought, not grown.** This is the defining fact. **Organic growth was +2% in
   FY2025, and +2% annualized over the four years ended 2025-12-31.** Adjusted EPS grew +7%
   annualized over that same four-year period. The gap between 2% organic and 7% EPS is closed
   by acquisitions, margin/restructuring programs, and buybacks. In FY2025 alone: **four
   acquisitions for $665.3 million** and **$541M of announced share repurchases** including a
   **$500M accelerated share repurchase program** initiated in November 2025.

**So: a dollar of Dover revenue becomes about 18 cents of operating profit and about 14 cents of
free cash flow — but the *next* dollar of revenue is mostly purchased with the previous dollar's
free cash flow.** That is a materially different animal from a business that compounds
internally, and it is what the ROIC evidence in Gate 2 confirms.

### Reportable segments — how many, and is each independently evaluable?

**Five reportable segments.** This is the source of genuine Gate 1 pressure and I am going to
work it honestly rather than wave it through.

**What Dover discloses, quarterly, per segment:** revenue, organic growth rate, segment margin,
and bookings/book-to-bill. That is a real disclosure standard — better than many industrials.

| Segment (Q2 2026, qtr ended 2026-06-30) | Revenue | Organic growth | Segment margin |
|---|---|---|---|
| Engineered Products | $283.5M | +2.1% | 20.4% |
| Clean Energy & Fueling | $595.0M | +8.6% | 21.6% |
| Imaging & Identification | $305.1M | +2.9% | 27.9% |
| Pumps & Process Solutions | $552.7M | +0.4% | 32.4% |
| Climate & Sustainability Technologies | $455.1M | +8.3% | 16.7% |

**But "reportable segment" is not the same as "evaluable business," and here is the problem.**
At least three of the five reporting lines are themselves mini-conglomerates of *unrelated* end
markets:

- **Engineered Products** aggregates the **vehicle aftermarket, aerospace & defense, industrial
  winch & hoist, and fluid dispensing.** Those four have nothing to do with one another —
  different customers, different cycles, different competitors, different margins. They are
  reported as one number.
- **Clean Energy & Fueling** aggregates retail-fueling equipment with cryogenic components for
  clean-energy applications — a mature, slow-replacement business bolted to a
  policy-and-buildout-driven one.
- **Climate & Sustainability Technologies** aggregates commercial refrigeration systems, heat
  exchangers, and beverage can-making equipment.

An analyst cannot compute owner earnings for any individual Dover operating company. The best
available resolution is the segment, and the segment is a blend.

**Compounding this: the object of analysis does not hold still.** Dover divested De-Sta-Co in
2024 (producing a large GAAP gain that distorts the 2024/2025 comparison — GAAP EPS fell 21% in
FY2025 purely on that base effect, while adjusted EPS rose 16%). It made four acquisitions in
FY2025 for $665M. Management states plainly that it "opportunistically divests businesses where
it sees limited runway." The Dover you analyze in 2026 is measurably not the Dover of 2030.

### ▶ GATE 1 VERDICT: **PASS — but marginal, and qualified**

**Why PASS:** the gate's own standard is to fail only if "genuinely opaque or too heterogeneous
to assess." Dover is not opaque. Every quarter it publishes revenue, organic growth, margin and
book-to-bill for each of five segments, plus consolidated free cash flow and bookings. A
diligent analyst can form a defensible view of the direction of each segment and of the whole.
The underlying activity — making specialized industrial equipment and selling parts for it — is
comprehensible.

**Why marginal, recorded for the operator:**
- Three of five segments are themselves bundles of unrelated end markets; no sub-segment
  economics are disclosed.
- ~25 operating companies exist beneath five reporting lines. None is individually visible.
- Portfolio composition changes materially every year through M&A and divestiture, so the
  ten-year predictability that Gate 1 is really testing for is weaker here than the disclosure
  quality alone would suggest.
- GAAP results require adjustment to be comparable year-to-year (the De-Sta-Co gain), which
  means the operator must rely on management-defined "adjusted" figures more than is ideal.

**A stricter operator would be entitled to FAIL this gate on the heterogeneity ground.** I do
not, because the segment-level disclosure is genuinely sufficient to reach a verdict on
direction — but the qualification is carried forward and it directly caps the Gate 2 moat class

## GATE 2 — MOAT (THE FRANCHISE TEST)

### (a) Is the product/service NEEDED or DESIRED?

**YES — needed, in the strictest sense.** Nobody desires a positive-displacement pump. They
require one, because the plant does not run without it. Dover's products are non-discretionary
inputs to somebody else's production. The demand is derived, not chosen. Evidence of breadth:
CEO Richard Tobin, 2026-01-29 — "Demand trends are solid and broad-based across the portfolio…
with **no individual end market presenting a material headwind.**"

### (b) Is there NO close substitute?

**MIXED — and this is where the analysis divides Dover in two.**

The honest test is the **segment margin dispersion**, which is nearly a 2:1 spread and is not
random:

| Segment | Q2 2026 margin | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| Pumps & Process Solutions | **32.4%** | Real franchise. Engineered-in, spec'd-in, aftermarket-heavy. Substitution requires requalification. |
| Imaging & Identification | **27.9%** | Real franchise. Installed printers/markers + consumable ink. Classic razor/blade. |
| Clean Energy & Fueling | 21.6% | Mid. Retail fueling has installed-base pull; cryogenic components are more contested. |
| Engineered Products | 20.4% | Weaker. Four unrelated end markets, several genuinely competitive. |
| Climate & Sustainability | **16.7%** | **Weakest — this looks like a price taker.** Refrigeration systems and can-making equipment are project-bid, competitively tendered businesses. |

A 16.7% segment margin in Climate & Sustainability against 32.4% in Pumps & Process Solutions
tells you, without any management commentary, that **these are not the same kind of business.**
The high-margin half has close-substitute protection; the low-margin half competes for orders.

**Direct pricing-power evidence, Q1 2026 (call, 2026-04-23):** on tariffs, Tobin said Dover
assessed "tens of thousands of line items" and concluded the net impact was **"relatively
neutral,"** because Dover is "mostly a build in the region to ship into the region kind of
company." That is a real answer and it is a genuine *cost*-side strength — but note what it is
**not**: it is not a claim that Dover raised prices and customers absorbed it. It is a claim
that Dover avoided the cost. **Pricing power was demonstrated as cost avoidance and mix, not as
price realization.**

### (c) Is it relatively UNREGULATED?

**YES, mostly, with one caveat that cuts both ways.** No rate regulation, no licensing gate on
Dover's ability to price or sell. But **Clean Energy & Fueling and Climate & Sustainability
Technologies both have demand that is materially policy-driven** — refrigerant transition
mandates, fuel-infrastructure standards, clean-energy buildout incentives. That is currently a
tailwind (CS&T +8.3% organic in Q2 2026, +15.2% in Q1 2026), but a tailwind created by policy
is a tailwind that policy can withdraw. It is not regulation *of* Dover; it is regulation
*creating* a chunk of Dover's demand. Recorded as a qualification, not a fail.

### (d) Is the moat PROVEN by pricing power AND returns on capital?

**PARTIALLY — and this is the single most important finding in the Dover file.**

**Pricing power: proven at the segment-margin level.** The primary moat metric is consolidated
**adjusted segment EBITDA margin**, and the trend is genuinely good:

Per-segment margin trend, FY2025 → Q1 2026 → Q2 2026:

|---|---|---|---|

**Returns on capital: this is where the moat claim fails to fully prove out.**

> **ROIC 13.21%. ROE 14.91%.** (stockanalysis.com, 2026-08-01. A TTM-income-statement
> computation gives ~10.46%; a third gives 12.74%. **Every available reading clusters in the
> 10–13% band.**)

Hold that against Dover's own segment margins of 25% and gross margin of 40%. **How does a
business with 40% gross margins and 25% segment EBITDA margins earn only ~13% on invested
capital?**

The answer is the balance sheet. Decades of acquisitions — **$665M in FY2025 alone** — have
piled goodwill and intangibles onto invested capital. The *operating* businesses are good. The
*price paid to assemble them* means the owner's return on the capital actually committed is
roughly 13%, not 25%.

The contrast with TJX in this same file is the whole point: **TJX earns 23.6–31% on invested
capital. Dover earns ~13%.** Both are well-run. Only one of them is a compounding machine in
the Buffett sense. A ~13% ROIC is respectable, exceeds any plausible cost of capital, and is
not a failing business — but it is **not the "wonderful business" return signature**, and the
gate asks whether returns on capital *prove* the moat. Here they prove a *narrow* one.

**Second confirming datum: organic growth of +2% annualized over four years (through
2025-12-31).** A business with a genuinely wide moat and 25% segment margins should be able to
grow its existing franchises faster than 2%. Dover's headline growth requires acquisitions to
manufacture it.

**Fair counter-evidence, recorded:** organic growth has genuinely inflected. FY2025 organic +2%,
but Q4 2025 **+5%**, Q1 2026 **+5%**, Q2 2026 **+5% with all five segments positive.**
Bookings are strong and leading: Q1 2026 bookings **$2,464M with book-to-bill above 1.0 in all
five segments**; Q2 2026 bookings **$2,330M**, described as double-digit growth; FY2025
bookings **$8,138M vs $7,676M in 2024**. FY2026 guidance was **raised on 2026-07-23** to
revenue growth **6–8% (organic 4–6%)** and adjusted EPS **$10.55–$10.75** (from $10.45–$10.65).
Dover is in a genuine upcycle. The question Gate 2 asks is whether that is a *moat* or a
*cycle*, and two quarters cannot answer it.

### Direction and class

**Direction: WIDENING (margins) / STABLE (franchise).** Segment margins are expanding
convincingly — +140bps in FY2025, +80bps in Q2 2026, every segment at or above FY2025 levels.
But a large share of that expansion is attributed by management to **restructuring benefits and
productivity**, which is *self-help*, not moat. Self-help expands margin once. Moat expands it
repeatedly.

**Class: NARROW.** Not NONE — Pumps & Process Solutions (32.4%) and Imaging & Identification
(27.9%) are real franchises with installed-base and requalification protection, and they are
~39% of revenue. Not WIDE — because **~13% ROIC, +2% four-year organic growth, and a 16.7%-margin
price-taker segment are all inconsistent with a wide moat**, and because the entity is a
portfolio whose composition management reshuffles annually.

### ▶ GATE 2 VERDICT: **PASS** — Moat class **NARROW**, direction **STABLE-to-WIDENING**

Checks (a) and (c) clear. Check (b) is **mixed** — two segments have genuine substitute
protection, one (Climate & Sustainability, 16.7%) reads as a price taker. Check (d) is
**partially proven** — pricing power is evidenced in segment margins, but **returns on capital
of ~13% against 25% segment margins say the moat is around the operating businesses, not around
the capital.** NARROW is not NONE, so the gate passes, but it passes at the lower class.

But the test is dollar-of-value-per-dollar-retained, and Dover retains a great deal (it pays out

### The thing that actually kills this thesis

**Not a collapse. A grind.**

The Dover failure mode is not a moat being stormed; it is a **roll-up whose acquisition math
stops working.** The mechanism:

Organic growth is structurally ~2–3%. Headline growth of 6–8% is manufactured by spending ~$665M
a year on bolt-ons. That works only as long as Dover can keep buying niche leaders at prices
that clear a double-digit ROIC. If asset prices rise — more private-equity competition for
middle-market industrials, or Dover simply exhausting the supply of businesses that fit its
screen — then one of two things happens: Dover **pays up** (and consolidated ROIC grinds down
from ~13% toward the cost of capital), or Dover **stops buying** (and reported growth collapses
back to the ~2–3% organic rate).

Either outcome breaks the thesis, and **neither shows up first in the earnings headline** —
adjusted EPS can be held up by buybacks and restructuring for several years while the underlying
return on capital erodes. That is exactly what makes it dangerous.

**The current upcycle makes this harder to see, not easier.** Three quarters of +5% organic and
book-to-bill above 1.0 in all five segments is genuinely good news — and it is precisely the
environment in which a roll-up overpays, because the deal models look best at the top of the cycle
and management has just told the market that the 2026 M&A environment is "more favorable."

### Thesis-breaking metric and numeric exit threshold

> **PRIMARY METRIC: Consolidated return on invested capital.**
>
> **EXIT THRESHOLD: Exit if ROIC falls below 11.0% on a trailing-twelve-month basis for two
> consecutive quarters.**

Why that number: current ROIC is **13.21%** (2026-08-01), with alternative computations at
12.74% and 10.46%. A sustained fall below **11.0%** — roughly 220bps below the primary current
reading — would mean acquisitions are being funded at returns that no longer compensate for the
capital committed. Because ROIC is the metric that already sits at Dover's weakest point, it is
the correct tripwire: **it is where the disease shows up first, and it is the metric that
management's own acquisition screen claims to protect.**

> **CONFIRMING METRIC (the earlier and more practical signal):
> Consolidated organic revenue growth negative for two consecutive quarters while adjusted
> segment EBITDA margin contracts year-over-year in the same two quarters.**

Why the compound construction: Dover is an industrial, and industrials have cycles. Negative
organic growth *alone* is an ordinary downcycle and is not a thesis break — Dover printed +2%
organic for a full year and remained a fine business. Negative organic growth **while margins
are simultaneously contracting** means the self-help lever is exhausted *and* volume is falling
— i.e., the "we make it up on productivity" answer has run out. That is the specific signature
of a roll-up whose underlying franchises were weaker than the segment margins implied.
**Current reading, Q2 2026 (2026-07-23): organic +5%, adjusted segment EBITDA margin 25.9%
(+80bps). Nowhere near the trigger.**

> **THIRD TRIGGER (structural): a single acquisition exceeding ~$1.5B, or annual M&A spend
> exceeding ~$1.5B.**

Dover's FY2025 pace was $665M across four deals — genuinely bolt-on. A step-change into
large-deal territory would mean the bolt-on supply has been exhausted and management has changed
the business model without saying so. **This is the trigger that fires before the ROIC trigger,
and it is the one to actually watch.**

**Open data gap carried from Gate 3:** whether ROIC is a *gating metric in the long-term
incentive plan* is **[UNVERIFIED]**. If it is not — if management is paid on adjusted EPS growth
and TSR without an ROIC gate — then the incentive structure actively rewards the exact behavior
(buying growth regardless of price) that the primary exit metric is designed to catch. **This
should be resolved from the 2026 proxy before Dover advances past Gate 5 in any subsequent run.**

### ▶ GATE 5 VERDICT: **PASS — but at 3 of 4 RESOLVED, with moat destruction UNRESOLVED**

Specific thesis-breaking metrics exist with numeric thresholds, and Dover's quarterly segment
disclosure makes them observable. Balance-sheet risk and management failure are cleanly resolved.
**Moat destruction is scored UNRESOLVED** and I am not going to pretend otherwise: the four-year
organic growth record (+2%), the ~13% ROIC against 25% segment margins, the 16.7%-margin
price-taker segment, and the attribution of margin gains to self-help collectively mean the
available evidence **cannot yet distinguish a widening moat from a favorable point in the
industrial cycle.** Three good quarters is a reason to keep watching, not a resolution.
