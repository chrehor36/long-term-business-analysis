# PART ONE — STARBUCKS CORPORATION (SBUX)

Fiscal year ends late September. FY2025 ended 2025-09-28. Latest reported quarter is
Q3 FY2026, ended 2026-06-28, reported 2026-07-29.

---

## GATE 1 — CIRCLE OF COMPETENCE

### The unit economics, in plain words

Starbucks sells a cheap, chemically habit-forming liquid at a very high markup, many times
per customer per month, from small-footprint boxes on expensive corners.

The economics of one store:
- A cup of drip coffee or an espresso drink costs the company a small fraction of its
  retail price in green coffee, milk and cup. Coffee is a commodity input bought forward.
- The real cost lines are **labour** and **occupancy** — the barista hours to make the
  drink and the lease on the corner. Everything in the business model is about revenue per
  labour hour and revenue per square foot.
- A store is a fixed-cost box. Once rent and the minimum staffing are paid, incremental
  transactions drop to the bottom line at very high margin. This is why **transactions**
  matter more than price: one more customer through the door is nearly pure profit, whereas
  one more dollar of price on an existing customer risks losing that customer entirely.
- Capital per store is modest (build-out, equipment) and payback historically fast.

The company earns money three ways:
1. **Company-operated stores** — full retail economics, full cost, full capital.
2. **Licensed stores** — someone else's capital and labour; Starbucks takes a royalty and
   sells them the coffee. Far less revenue per store, far higher margin, near-zero capital.
3. **Channel Development** — packaged coffee and ready-to-drink in grocery, largely run
   through the Nestlé Global Coffee Alliance and the PepsiCo RTD partnership. This is a
   royalty stream on the brand, not a retail business.

The strategic pivot of the last year is a deliberate shift of the mix from (1) toward (2).
The China joint venture converted **7,991 company-operated stores to licensed stores**
(Q3 FY2026 release, 2026-07-29). That is why revenue fell 1% to $9.3B in Q3 FY2026 while
GAAP operating margin *rose* 60bps and non-GAAP margin rose 430bps. Less revenue, better
revenue.

Total portfolio at Q3 FY2026: **41,304 stores, 33% company-operated / 67% licensed**
(vs. 40,990 at FY2025 close). US 16,933 stores.

### Reportable segments — independently evaluable?

Post-China-deconsolidation the structure is: **North America**, **International**,
**Channel Development**, plus Corporate/Other. Each has its own revenue, operating income
and store base disclosed quarterly, and each is separately understandable — North America
is the retail engine, International is now mostly a royalty-and-licensing book (with the
40% China JV stake running through equity-method income), Channel Development is a
brand royalty. Yes, independently evaluable.

The China transaction actually **improved** legibility: instead of consolidating 8,000
Chinese stores whose economics were deteriorating inside an "International" line, Starbucks
now reports a 40% equity stake plus a licensing royalty. Fewer moving parts.

### VERDICT — GATE 1: **PASS**

This is coffee sold in shops. A retired schoolteacher could describe the business in one
sentence and would be right. Nothing here is opaque, and the segment count is small and
each is legible. The FY2026 accounting is noisy (a $536.3M one-time gain on the China
deal, a deconsolidation mid-year) but noise in the *accounting* is not opacity in the
*business*.

## GATE 2 — MOAT (FRANCHISE TEST)

### (a) Is the product needed or desired?

**Yes, strongly.** This is the most defensible leg. Coffee is a daily habit with a
physiological hook, purchased 15–25 times a month by the core customer. Starbucks Rewards
has been the single largest driver of that frequency. The Q3 FY2026 result — comparable
**transactions** up 4.5% in North America — is direct evidence that the desire is intact
when the company executes.

### (b) Is there no close substitute?

**Weak — this is the softest of the four.** There are many close substitutes: Dunkin',
McDonald's McCafé, Dutch Bros, local independents, and in China, Luckin Coffee and Cotti
at roughly a third of the price. Coffee itself is a commodity; a competent independent can
make a better flat white than Starbucks.

What Starbucks has instead of product exclusivity is a **distribution + habit + digital**
moat: 16,933 US locations means it is the coffee shop you pass, the Rewards balance is
stored value you have already surrendered, and the mobile-order queue is the real switching
cost. That is a genuine moat but it is a *convenience and habit* moat, not a
*no-substitute* moat.

The China outcome is the honest evidence against a strong reading here. Starbucks sold
**60% of its China retail operations to Boyu Capital for ~$4B**, valuing the whole retail
business at ~$4B (announced 2025-11-03, closed April 2026). For 8,011 stores in the
world's largest growth market, that is not a wide-moat price. It is the price of a
business that lost a price war to Luckin. Starbucks says total China value exceeds $13B
including retained stake and a decade-plus of licensing NPV — but the *retail* operation
itself was marked at $4B by an arm's-length buyer.

**Check (b): PARTIAL.**

### (c) Relatively unregulated — free to price?

**Yes — PASS, and this is a genuine strength.** No regulator sets the price of a latte.
Starbucks raises prices at will. The regulatory exposure is ordinary (food safety, labour
law, wage floors) and does not constrain pricing. Contrast with CCB below, where this
check is a live disqualifier.

### (d) Proven by pricing power AND returns on capital?

This is where the last two years must be read carefully, because the *composition* of comp
sales is the tell.

**The abuse phase (FY2024 – FY2025).** Comparable store sales were negative for seven
consecutive quarters. Critically, the shape was always the same: **ticket up, transactions
sharply down.**

| Quarter | US/NA comps | Transactions | Ticket | China comps |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Q4 FY2024 (ended 2024-09-29) | −6% (US) | −10% | +4% | −14% |
| Q1 FY2025 (ended 2024-12-29) | −4% (NA/US) | −8% | +4% | −6% |
| Q2 FY2025 (ended 2025-03-30) | −2% (US) | −4% | +3% | n/d |
| Q3 FY2025 (ended 2025-06-29) | −2% (US) | −4% | +2% | +2% |
| Q4 FY2025 (ended 2025-09-28) | 0% (NA/US) | −1% | +1% | +2% |

That pattern — holding revenue up by raising price on a shrinking customer base — is not
pricing power. It is **the exhaustion of pricing power**, and it is exactly the signature
Buffett warns about: a business that must raise price to stand still is losing the
franchise. FY2025 full-year North America comps −2%, transactions −4%, ticket +2%.

**The recovery phase (FY2026).** The shape inverted, and this is the single most important
fact in the SBUX file:

| Quarter | NA comps | Transactions | Ticket | Global comps | China comps |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Q1 FY2026 (ended 2025-12-28) | +4% | +3% | +1% | +4% | +7% |
| Q2 FY2026 (ended 2026-03-29) | +7.1% | +4.4% | +2.6% | +6.2% | +0.5% |
| Q3 FY2026 (ended 2026-06-28) | +8.1% | +4.5% | +3.5% | +7.9% | n/a (deconsolidated) |

**Transactions and ticket are now rising together.** That is the healthy signature — the
customer is coming back *more often* AND paying *more* per visit. Four consecutive quarters
of comp growth. This is the strongest available evidence that brand pricing power is intact
rather than eroding.

The caveat, stated honestly: Q3 FY2026's +7.9% laps Q3 FY2025's −2%. On a two-year stack
that is roughly +5.9% — still genuinely positive, but the headline overstates it.

**Operating margin trend, 8 quarters:**

| Quarter | GAAP OM | Non-GAAP OM |
|---|---|---|
| FY2025 full year | 7.9% (−710 bps) | 9.9% (−510 bps) |
| Q1 FY2026 | 9.0% (−290 bps) | 10.1% (−180 bps) |
| Q2 FY2026 | 8.7% (+180 bps) | 9.4% (+120 bps) |
| Q3 FY2026 | 10.5% (+60 bps) | 14.4% (+430 bps) |
| FY2026 guide | — | >11.0% |

Two consecutive quarters of margin expansion, with Q3's 430bps non-GAAP jump reflecting
the licensed-model mix shift from the China conversion.

**Returns on capital** cannot be measured by ROE — book equity is **negative $7.7B** at
Q3 FY2026 (see Gate 3), which makes ROE meaningless and, if anything, absurdly flattering.
The honest measures: FY2025 operating income ~$2.94B on ~$31B of assets is poor;
FY2026 tracking to >11% operating margin on ~$36B revenue is a material recovery but not
yet a Buffett-grade return.

**Check (d): PARTIAL — abused through FY2025, recovering convincingly in FY2026.**

### Direction and class

**Direction: WIDENING** (from a NARROWING base). The moat was demonstrably narrowing
through FY2024–FY2025 — seven negative quarters, US transactions down as much as 10% in
a quarter, China lost outright to a cheaper competitor. It is now widening: four quarters
of transaction-led growth and two of margin expansion.

**Class: NARROW.** Not WIDE. A wide-moat business does not lose 10% of its traffic in a
quarter, and does not sell control of its largest growth market for $4B. Starbucks has a
real, durable, habit-and-distribution moat — but one that proved breachable by execution
failure and by a cheaper competitor, and that required a change of CEO and two years to
repair.

### VERDICT — GATE 2: **PASS** (NARROW, WIDENING)

## GATE 3 — MANAGEMENT

### INTEGRITY (binary; permanent if failed)

Tested for: fraud, accounting manipulation, cartel behaviour, bribery/FCPA, systematic
deception of customers or regulators, regulator finding of misconduct.

**Findings:**

- **No accounting restatement, no SEC enforcement action, no FCPA matter, no cartel or
  price-fixing finding** located in the record as of 2026-08-01.

- **NLRB / Workers United labour matters — assessed and judged NOT an integrity failure.**
  The record is substantial: Workers United asserts NLRB administrative law judges have
  found **more than 400 labour law violations**, with the Board itself finding **more than
  140**. Specific enforced findings include the D.C. Circuit upholding that Starbucks
  violated §8(a)(3) and (1) by discharging two employees in Ann Arbor for union activity
  and discriminatorily enforcing its dress code against union apparel. In **June 2026**,
  the Fifth Circuit enforced *in part* the Board's order regarding a Sylmar, California
  store — upholding two unlawful-threat findings and an unlawful interrogation, while
  holding the Board's remaining §8(a)(1) findings **unsupported by substantial evidence**
  and vacating them.

  **Judgment.** This gate tests dishonesty — whether management lies to owners, customers
  or regulators, or steals. What the NLRB record shows is an employer fighting a
  unionization campaign aggressively and, repeatedly, unlawfully. That is a real legal
  liability, a real reputational cost, and a real signal about the institution's posture
  toward its own workforce. It is not fraud, not accounting manipulation, and not deception
  of customers or investors. Note also that appellate courts have **partially vacated**
  Board findings, which means the raw violation count overstates what has survived review.

  This is a **contested labour dispute**, not the species of dishonesty this gate is built
  to catch. Recorded as a **material demerit** carried forward into Gate 5, not an
  integrity failure.

**INTEGRITY: PASS** (with the labour record noted as a standing reservation).

### COMPETENCE — the buyback record and the collapse in owner earnings

This is the damning part of the file, and it must be stated without softening.

**Owner earnings, computed properly (NI + D&A − capex):**

| | FY2021 | FY2025 |
|---|---|---|
| Net earnings | $4,199M | $1,857M |
| D&A | $1,524M | $1,769M |
| Capex | $(1,470)M | $(2,306)M |
| **Owner earnings** | **~$4,253M** | **~$1,320M** |

Cross-check: FY2021 OCF $5,989M − capex $1,470M = **$4,519M FCF**; FY2025 OCF $4,748M −
capex $2,306M = **$2,442M FCF**. Both methods show the same collapse.

**Owner earnings fell ~69% in four years.**

**The buyback record over the same window:**

| Fiscal year | Share repurchases |
|---|---|
| FY2018 | $7.2B (131.5M shares) |
| FY2019 | $10.1B (139.6M shares) |
| FY2020 | ~$1.7B *(not independently verified this run — flagged)* |
| FY2021 | $97M |
| FY2022 | $4,140M |
| FY2023 | $1,074M |
| FY2024 | $1,367M |
| FY2025 | $87M |

Verified total FY2018–FY2025 excluding the unverified FY2020 figure: **>$24.0B**.

**Was that capital allocation defensible? No — and this is a clean failure against
Buffett's own published standard.**

The standard (1984 letter, restated 2011): repurchase only when the stock trades below a
conservatively estimated intrinsic value, and only with genuinely surplus funds. Starbucks
failed both halves:

1. **Wrong price.** The heaviest buying — $17.3B across FY2018–FY2019 alone — was executed
   into a rising market at a peak valuation, including two accelerated share repurchase
   programs ($5.0B effective 2018-10-01, funded with Nestlé alliance proceeds, and $2.0B
   effective 2019-03-22). These were not opportunistic purchases of a cheap asset; they
   were a pre-announced commitment to return $25B by fiscal 2020 — a *quota*, executed
   regardless of price. Quota-driven repurchase is the institutional imperative in its
   purest form.

2. **Not surplus funds.** The repurchases exceeded cumulative earnings and were partly
   debt-financed. The result is on the balance sheet today: **shareholders' deficit of
   $(8.10)B at FY2025 close, $(8.47)B at Q2 FY2026, $(7.7)B at Q3 FY2026**, against
   **$16.07B total debt at FY2025** ($13.3B at Q3 FY2026 after paydown).

3. **The proof of the error: no capacity when it mattered.** When owner earnings collapsed
   to $1.32B and the stock traded down to a 52-week low of $76.49, Starbucks repurchased
   **$87M — essentially nothing** in FY2025, and nothing disclosed in FY2026. The company
   bought $17.3B of its own stock near the highs and could not buy at the lows. That is the
   exact inversion of the discipline this framework requires, and the damage — a negative
   equity base and a $13–16B debt load — is permanent and inherited by every subsequent
   management.

Additionally, FY2025's **dividends of $2,771M exceeded owner earnings of ~$1,320M by more
than 2x.** The company distributed roughly double what it earned, in a year of collapsing
profitability, from a balance sheet with no equity. Defensible only if one believed the
downturn was brief — a bet, not a calculation.

**The current regime is a different question, and it must be scored separately.**
Brian Niccol became CEO in September 2024. His capital decisions to date:

- **Halted buybacks entirely** ($87M FY2025, none disclosed FY2026) while the balance sheet
  was impaired. Correct.
- **Monetized China at a full price rather than fighting a price war he could not win** —
  60% to Boyu Capital for ~$4B, closed April 2026, retaining 40% plus brand ownership and
  a licensing royalty. A **$536.3M net gain** booked in Q3 FY2026. Selling the losing
  position to a local operator while keeping the royalty is precisely the right structure.
- **Deleveraged with the proceeds** — **$2.8B of debt repaid**, including $1.3B aggregate
  principal repurchased via tender offers. Debt fell from $16.07B (FY2025) to $13.3B
  (Q3 FY2026). Applying an asset sale to debt reduction rather than to a buyback, with the
  stock near a 52-week low, is disciplined and against the industry grain.
- **Reinvested in the actual product and labour** — the "Back to Starbucks" plan — and got
  transactions back. FY2026 capex is tracking well below FY2025 ($887.8M for nine months
  vs. $2,306M full-year FY2025), partly from the China deconsolidation.

**COMPETENCE: the prior regime FAILS this test outright. The current regime passes on a
seven-quarter record.**

### ALIGNMENT

- **Niccol's FY2024 package: $96M for approximately four months of work** — $90M in stock
  awards, a $5M signing bonus, and a Chipotle buyout payment. Reported as **6,666x the
  median employee's $14,674**, characterized as the most lopsided CEO-to-worker ratio in an
  Institute for Policy Studies analysis of low-wage employers.
- **FY2025 compensation moderated to $30,992,773.**
- **Structure is better than the headline.** ~94% of the FY2024 award was equity, the
  majority performance-conditioned, with time-based portions vesting over three years. He
  is paid in the stock and cannot monetize it quickly.

**Judgment:** the quantum is egregious and the optics — against a $14,674 median employee
and an active unionization fight — are indefensible on their own terms. The *structure*,
however, is genuinely aligned: he owns equity, vesting over years, largely on performance.
A framework that tests alignment rather than fairness must score the structure. **Weak
PASS.**

### VERDICT — GATE 3: **PASS**

**This is the closest call in the SBUX file and I want the reasoning on the record, because
under the operator protocol a CONDITIONAL is not a pass and I am obliged to resolve it.**

The historical capital allocation record — >$24B of quota-driven, top-of-cycle,
partly-debt-funded buybacks that destroyed the equity base and left no dry powder at the
bottom — is a **clear failure** measured against Buffett's own standard. If this gate
scored the institution's history, it would fail.

But the gate tests **the stewards of my capital going forward**, and the people who made
those decisions are gone. The incumbent's actual, observable capital decisions — halt
buybacks while impaired, sell the losing geography at a full price, apply the proceeds to
debt rather than to a vanity repurchase, reinvest in the product — are what a
Buffett-standard allocator does. Integrity is intact. Alignment is structurally sound if
grotesque in quantum.

**PASS**, with two findings carried explicitly into Gate 5:
1. The negative-equity balance sheet is a **permanent inherited constraint**, not a
   resolved problem.
2. The pass rests on a **seven-quarter record**. That is thin, and it is the weakest
   evidentiary base in this analysis.

## GATE 5 — INVERSION

### Bull-case assumptions, stated explicitly

1. "Back to Starbucks" is a durable operating fix, not a promotional sugar high — the
   traffic recovery persists past the easy comparisons.
2. US comparable transactions stay positive; comps settle at mid-single digits.
3. Non-GAAP operating margin recovers from 9.9% (FY2025) through >11% (FY2026 guide) toward
   the mid-teens, aided permanently by the licensed-model mix shift.
4. The China JV is accretive in structure: Starbucks collects a high-margin royalty plus
   40% equity income on a business Boyu runs better and cheaper, with no further capital
   required.
5. Owner earnings recover from ~$1.32B (FY2025) toward $3.5B+ and the balance sheet
   de-levers out of the hole over several years.
6. Pricing power is intact — proven by ticket and transactions rising together.

### The inversion: what would make this a mistake?

**Moat destruction — RESOLVED (marginally).**
The strongest argument for a mistake was that Starbucks had permanently traded its
franchise for price: seven quarters of falling traffic held up by ticket increases, and a
$4B mark on 8,011 China stores. That thesis has been directly falsified by the FY2026 data
— transactions +4.2% to +4.5% for three consecutive quarters *alongside* ticket increases
of +2.6% to +3.5%. Customers came back and paid more. You cannot get that outcome from a
broken brand. Marked RESOLVED, with the two-year-stack caveat noted (Q3 FY2026's +7.9% laps
a −2%; the stacked figure is ~+5.9%).

**Management failure — RESOLVED for the current regime, UNRESOLVED as an institution.**
Niccol's decisions are correct and the operating result validates them. But the record is
seven quarters, the pay package signals a board that does not negotiate hard, and the same
board approved the $24B+ of buybacks that created the deficit. If Niccol leaves, the
institutional default is what produced FY2018–FY2019. Scored RESOLVED on the current
evidence, flagged as the thinnest evidentiary base in the file.

**Balance-sheet risk — UNRESOLVED. This is the live one.**
- Shareholders' deficit **$(7.7)B** (Q3 FY2026), improved from $(8.47)B (Q2 FY2026) and
  $(8.10)B (FY2025) but still deeply negative.
- Total debt **$13.3B** ($1.5B current, $11.8B long-term), down from $16.07B.
- Cash $3.4B.
- **FY2025 dividends of $2,771M against owner earnings of ~$1,320M** — a payout of >200% of
  what the business earned, funded from the balance sheet.
- The dividend is now 65 consecutive quarters with a 17% CAGR, i.e. institutionally
  untouchable. That is a fixed ~$2.8B annual claim on a company with no equity cushion.

There is zero margin for error. FY2026 owner earnings should recover materially (nine-month
OCF $3.6B less capex $887.8M = $2.71B FCF already exceeds all of FY2025's $2.44B), which
would restore dividend coverage. But a second operating stumble, with a $13.3B debt load,
a $2.8B fixed dividend and no book equity, forces either a dividend cut or further asset
sales. **UNRESOLVED.**

Note: this is a *pricing and Gate 4* problem more than an inversion failure. Under the
Fortress Test as ratified 2026-07-15, SBUX is a **non-financial** business judged on
**survival**, not net cash — and it has just empirically demonstrated survival, having
absorbed a ~69% decline in owner earnings across FY2022–FY2025 without existential risk,
while investment-grade, termed out, and with $4.7B of operating cash flow. It would likely
pass Gate 4. But "survives" is not "safe," and the buyer must be paid for the absence of
an equity cushion.

**Existence of a thesis-breaking metric — YES.**

### Named thesis-breaking metric and numeric exit threshold

**Primary metric: US comparable *transaction* growth (not comps, not ticket).**

**EXIT THRESHOLD: two consecutive quarters of negative US comparable transaction growth.**

Rationale: the FY2024–FY2025 decline was fundamentally a *traffic* problem that management
masked with price, and the FY2026 recovery is fundamentally a *traffic* recovery. Comps and
ticket can both be manufactured — comps by price, ticket by mix. Transactions cannot. If
traffic turns negative again while ticket carries the comp line, "Back to Starbucks" has
failed and management has reverted to milking a shrinking customer base. Two quarters
rather than one, because a single quarter can be weather, calendar or a lapped promotion.

**Secondary threshold:** non-GAAP operating margin fails to reach 13.0% by FY2027 close.
The entire margin thesis rests on the licensed-model mix shift being structural. If two
full years of that shift plus mid-single-digit comps cannot move margin from 9.9% to 13%,
the operating leverage does not exist and the recovery is revenue-only.

**Tertiary (balance sheet):** any quarter in which the declared dividend again exceeds
trailing-twelve-month owner earnings, after FY2026.

### VERDICT — GATE 5: **PASS**

The bull case is explicitly falsifiable, the two categories that would kill it (moat,
management) are addressed by direct, recent, hard evidence rather than narrative, and a
specific traffic-based exit threshold exists that would fire before the thesis was
irrecoverable. The balance sheet remains **UNRESOLVED** — but that is a fact to be *priced*
in Gate 6, not a reason to fail the inversion.

# PART TWO — COASTAL FINANCIAL CORPORATION (CCB)

Everett, Washington bank holding company for **Coastal Community Bank**. Calendar fiscal
year. Latest reported quarter **Q2 2026, ended 2026-06-30, reported 2026-07-30 — two days
before this analysis.**

*(Not China Construction Bank.)*

---

## GATE 1 — CIRCLE OF COMPETENCE

### How CCBX actually makes money, in plain words

**Coastal Community Bank rents out its bank charter.**

A fintech — a credit card startup, a consumer lender, a payments app — cannot legally
originate loans in all fifty states or hold FDIC-insured deposits without a bank charter.
Getting one takes years and may not be granted. So the fintech rents Coastal's.

Mechanically, per the FY2025 10-K disclosures and the quarterly releases:

1. The fintech acquires and **underwrites** the end customer. Coastal does not.
2. **Coastal originates the loan on its own balance sheet** and holds the customer's
   deposits. Legally these are Coastal's loans and Coastal's deposits, and they appear on
   Coastal's books and in its regulatory capital calculation.
3. Coastal collects the interest. CCBX loan yield was **14.56% in Q2 2026** (down from
   15.01% in Q1 2026, 16.22% in Q2 2025, 17.75% in Q2 2024) — this is subprime and
   near-prime consumer credit: credit cards, unsecured instalment loans.
4. **Coastal pays most of that interest straight back to the partner** as "BaaS loan
   expense." This is the single most important line for understanding the business, and it
   is why the headline margin is misleading:

   | | Headline NIM | **NIM net of BaaS loan expense** |
   |---|---|---|
   | Q2 2026 | 7.27% | **3.98%** |
   | Q1 2026 | 7.00% | **3.90%** |
   | Q4 2025 | 7.03% | **4.26%** |
   | Q2 2025 | 7.06% | **4.07%** |
   | Q1 2025 | 7.48% | **4.28%** |
   | Q2 2024 | 7.12% | **4.00%** |

   **A "7.27% net interest margin" that is really ~4.0% is the headline you must not
   believe.** The gap is the partner's share. On the real measure, Coastal is a roughly
   4%-margin bank — good but not extraordinary — that *looks* like a 7% margin bank.

5. Coastal also earns **BaaS program fee income** — flat fees, transaction and interchange
   revenue. **$12.0M in Q2 2026**, up 10.3% sequentially and **+57.0% year over year**.
   This is the genuinely attractive, genuinely capital-light part of the model and it is
   compounding fast.
6. Coastal gets deposits. CCBX cost of deposits **3.19%** in Q2 2026 vs. Community Bank
   **1.46%** — so the BaaS deposits are *expensive*, not cheap. Consolidated cost of
   deposits 2.57%.

### Who bears the credit risk on fintech-partner loans?

**On paper: the partner. In substance: Coastal bears the partner's solvency risk, which is
a completely different and much less visible thing.**

The mechanism, per the 10-K:

- Partner agreements provide a **credit enhancement** under which the partner indemnifies
  or reimburses Coastal for incurred credit losses, and **100% for fraud losses**.
- As of **2025-12-31**, contracts covered **98.8% of credit risk** on CCBX partner loan
  balances.
- When Coastal books a provision for credit losses, it **simultaneously books a "credit
  enhancement asset"** on the balance sheet, running through noninterest income
  (line item: "BaaS credit enhancements"), representing the partner's legal obligation.
  The asset is relieved as the partner actually pays.
- Many partners **pledge a cash reserve account at the Bank** as collateral, replenished
  at intervals. These sit inside total deposits.
- In Q2 2026, partners were **contractually responsible for 98.0% of the $50.6M of CCBX
  net charge-offs.**

The 10-K states the counterparty risk plainly: *"if a partner is unable to fulfill their
contracted obligation then the Bank would be exposed to additional loan and deposit
losses."*

### How this differs from ordinary community banking

An ordinary community bank underwrites its own borrower, knows the borrower, holds
collateral, and eats its own credit losses. Its risk is **borrower credit risk**, and an
analyst can assess it: loan mix, geography, LTVs, vintage, charge-off history.

Coastal does none of that on the CCBX book. It does not underwrite the end borrower. Its
true exposure is **unsecured counterparty credit risk to a fintech company**. Coastal is,
functionally, **writing credit insurance on a subprime consumer book and buying reinsurance
from a venture-backed startup.** The economics work only for as long as the reinsurer is
solvent.

And the underlying credit is genuinely bad by community-bank standards. Excluding the
troubled partner, CCBX annualized net charge-offs ran at **8.73%** in Q2 2026 — described
by the company as a 20% year-over-year *improvement*. Consolidated net charge-offs were
**$50.6M, 4.90% annualized** of average loans. An ordinary community bank runs charge-offs
in the tens of basis points. The whole edifice rests on that 8.73% being someone else's
problem.

### **On 2026-07-30, it stopped being someone else's problem.**

Coastal reported a **net loss of $42.1M, $(2.76) per diluted share**, on **$68.8M of credit
expense tied to a single CCBX partner**:

- **$22.8M provision** for credit losses on that partner's portfolio, and
- **$46.0M valuation adjustment writing down the credit enhancement asset** — because,
  in the company's words, amounts are **"not expected to be fully recovered under the
  partner's indemnification arrangement."**
- Plus a **$4.4M software write-down**.

**That $46.0M is the indemnification failing.** It is the precise event the 10-K risk
factor described. The partner could not pay. The "98.8% covered" figure did not protect
the bank, because coverage on paper is worth exactly the balance sheet of the counterparty
writing it — and that counterparty is a **non-public** company whose financial condition
Coastal does not disclose and an outside analyst cannot see.

The affected portfolio is roughly **$500M of underlying consumer loans**; as of 2026-06-30
one partner held **$530,294 thousand ($530.3M) of CCBX gross loans** — approximately
**24% of the $2.23B CCBX loan book** and **~9.7% of total assets**. Management, at the
Board's request, reviewed all other programs and states none exhibit a similar situation;
CEO Eric Sprink: *"This is an isolated issue pertaining to one partner and does not reflect
a change in our view of our broader partner portfolio."* Management indicated resolution
could take **"one to two quarters to 12 to 18 months."**

### Reportable segments — independently evaluable?

Two segments: **Community Bank** and **CCBX**. In form, separately reported. The Community
Bank half is an ordinary, legible, decent Pacific Northwest bank — Q2 2026 even produced a
$(2.2)M provision *recapture*, i.e. credit improved. That half passes easily.

The CCBX half does not. Its reported income is dominated by the credit-enhancement
accounting, in which a provision expense and an offsetting noninterest-income asset are
booked in the same period. The reported earnings of that segment are therefore only as real
as the collectibility of an asset whose obligors are undisclosed. Q2 2026 demonstrated that
this is not a theoretical concern: $46.0M of previously-booked income was reversed.

### VERDICT — GATE 1: **FAIL**

**Later gates are MOOT. Findings are recorded below for the file, but no verdict past this
point carries authority.**

The failure is not that the model is complicated — I have just described it in plain words
and I believe the description is accurate. The failure is against the actual standard,
which is not *"can I describe this business"* but **"can I estimate what it will earn."**

I cannot, and neither can anyone outside the company, because:

1. The dominant variable in the P&L is **the solvency of undisclosed fintech
   counterparties** standing behind 98.8% of the credit risk on a $2.23B subprime consumer
   book running 8.73% gross charge-offs.
2. **The partners are not identified** ("one non-public CCBX partner"). Their balance
   sheets, funding, and going-concern status are not disclosed. The single most important
   input to the valuation is deliberately invisible.
3. The consequence is not a small estimation error. **Q1 2026: +$12.0M net income.
   Q2 2026: −$42.1M net loss. A $54M swing in one quarter from one relationship** —
   roughly **15% of shareholders' equity** — with no external warning available beforehand.
4. Management's defense is that it is "isolated." That may well be true. **But I have no
   way to verify it**, because verifying it requires exactly the counterparty financial
   information that is not disclosed. I would be taking management's word for the one
   number that determines the outcome.

A business whose earnings can swing from +$12M to −$42M on the undisclosed financial
condition of an unnamed private company is outside the circle. This is a **FAIL on
knowability**, and it is a clean one.

I want to be fair to the company: this is not a criticism of Coastal's competence or
candor. Its disclosure is unusually detailed for a bank this size, it discloses the
net-of-BaaS margin honestly rather than hiding behind the 7.27%, and its 10-K stated this
exact risk in advance. **The company disclosed the risk accurately. The risk is simply not
one an outside analyst can price.**

## GATE 2 — MOAT *(MOOT — Gate 1 failed. Recorded for the file.)*

### (a) Needed or desired? **YES.**
Genuine demand. Fintechs need charters and cannot get them. 30 total relationships at
Q2 2026 (22 active), up from 21 at Q2 2024. Program fee income +57% YoY. The service is
wanted.

### (b) No close substitute? **FAIL.**
Many close substitutes. The sponsor-bank market is crowded and fragmenting: Cross River,
Column, Lead Bank, Celtic, WebBank, Sutton, Pathward, Evolve and others compete for the
same partners. There is no meaningful lock-in — a fintech can and does re-paper to another
sponsor. Telling detail: Coastal itself **"ended active pursuit of acquiring assets and
deposits from Evolve Bank & Trust"** — the market is consolidating around distressed
sellers, which is what a commoditizing business looks like.

### (c) Relatively unregulated? **FAIL — and this is a real, structural constraint, stated
honestly as instructed.**

Banking is among the most regulated industries in existence, and **BaaS is the most
scrutinized corner of it.** Coastal cannot set its own capital, cannot choose its own
leverage, cannot expand without supervisory comfort, and can be ordered to stop onboarding
partners at any time.

The sector's enforcement record is the evidence: a sustained wave of consent orders across
sponsor banks — Blue Ridge, Evolve, Lineage, Thread — and most recently the **OCC's
BSA/AML consent order against Community Federal Savings Bank, publicly released
2026-05-21**, explicitly tied to CFSB's rapid fintech-driven growth (under $140M of assets
in 2017 to nearly $900M by 2024) outrunning its compliance function. That is the precise
template of Coastal's own growth curve. Coastal's president Curt Queyrouze has stated the
consent-order wave **accelerated** the bank's compliance plans set in motion in 2022.

Coastal has, to its credit, avoided an order so far. But "has not yet been sanctioned in a
sector being systematically sanctioned" is not a moat. This check fails outright, and it is
not a technicality — regulatory permission is the *entire product*, and regulatory
permission can be revoked.

### (d) Pricing power AND returns on capital? **PARTIAL, deteriorating.**

*For:* Program fee income +57% YoY, +10.3% sequentially — Coastal is taking more per
partner. Deposit costs falling hard: 3.58% (Q2 2024) → 3.10% (Q2 2025) → 2.74% (Q4 2025) →
2.57% (Q2 2026). Core deposits 99.8% of total. Record net interest income $89.4M in
Q2 2026, +16.4% YoY.

*Against:* CCBX loan yield falling steadily (17.75% → 16.22% → 14.89% → 15.01% → 14.56%).
FY2025 net income $47.0M on ~$470M average equity is roughly a **10% ROE** — mediocre for
the risk being run. Q2 2026 ROA **(3.32)%**, efficiency ratio deteriorated from 55.85% to
**79.24%**. And critically: **prior years' reported earnings must be discounted**, because
a portion of them was credit-enhancement income booked against an asset that has now proven
not fully collectible. The historical return series is less reliable than it looks.

## GATE 3 — MANAGEMENT *(MOOT. Recorded for the file.)*

### INTEGRITY — **PASS**

Searched specifically, as instructed, for BaaS/fintech-partner regulatory actions, consent
orders, and BSA/AML findings against Coastal Community Bank or Coastal Financial.

**No current consent order, no BSA/AML enforcement action, no fraud finding, and no
regulator finding of misconduct against Coastal Community Bank or Coastal Financial
Corporation located in the record as of 2026-08-01.**

This is a genuinely creditable result and deserves to be said plainly: in a sector where
consent orders have hit Blue Ridge, Evolve, Lineage, Thread and (2026-05-21) Community
Federal Savings Bank, **Coastal is among the few large sponsor banks not to have taken
one.** Management began building compliance in 2022, ahead of the wave. The company remains
well capitalized under prompt corrective action at Q2 2026.

*Noted, not charged:* a Washington DFI consent order exists against Coastal Community Bank
from the financial-crisis era (document 09-12-CD, 2009 vintage) — long resolved, predating
the CCBX business entirely, and not relevant to current management's integrity.

*Watch item, not an integrity finding:* the $46.0M credit-enhancement write-down raises a
fair question about whether that asset was carried at a supportable value in prior periods.
No restatement has been announced and the company disclosed the counterparty risk in
advance in its 10-K. Recorded as a question, not a charge.

### COMPETENCE — **FAIL**

*For:* Compliance built ahead of the sector. Walked away from the Evolve acquisition —
real discipline, declining to buy a distressed book at the top of the fear cycle. Deposit
costs managed down 100bps over two years. Program fee income compounding at ~57%. The
Community Bank segment is well run and produced a provision recapture in Q2 2026.

*Against — and this is decisive:* **management allowed a single partner to reach $530.3M
of CCBX gross loans, roughly 24% of the CCBX book and about 9.7% of total assets, against
$463.4M of total shareholders' equity.** The 10-K states the company maintains "individual
CCBX partner portfolio limits with each of its partners to manage loan concentration risk,
liquidity risk, and counter-party partner risk." Those limits permitted a single unnamed
non-public counterparty to accumulate an exposure that, when it broke, cost **$68.8M —
about 15% of equity — and wiped out four quarters of earnings in one quarter.**

That is a concentration-control failure, not bad luck, and the "isolated issue" framing
does not answer it. The question is not whether the other 21 partners are currently
healthy; it is why the control framework ever permitted a single fintech counterparty to
represent a quarter of the platform. A bank's first job is not to lose money in one place.

Also noted: a $4.4M software write-down in Q2 2026, and an efficiency ratio deteriorating
from 55.85% to 79.24%, with management only now announcing a review of "vendor spending,
contractor use, discretionary expenses, organizational duplication, and technology
priorities" — cost discipline arriving after the loss rather than before it.

### ALIGNMENT — **acceptable, arguably the best part of the file**

- **Insider ownership 4.36%** of shares outstanding (as of 2026-06-02). Top holders:
  Eric M. Sprink (CEO) 163,240 shares; Andrew P. Skotdal (Director) 150,120 and 120,680
  shares across holdings. Modest but real for a bank this size.
- **CEO compensation $1,693,201 for 2025**, *down* 10.34% from ~$1,868,263 in 2024
  (DEF 14A filed 2026-04-13). **This is a genuinely restrained package** — roughly 1/18th
  of Niccol's FY2025 pay at a company running a far riskier balance sheet. No red flag; if
  anything a point in management's favour.
- Board independent except the CEO.
- *Noted:* Sprink has been a repeated seller of stock in recent periods.
- *Noted:* Christopher D. Adams appointed Executive Chairman effective 2026-07-30 — a
  governance change announced in the same release as the loss. Worth watching.

### VERDICT — GATE 3: **FAIL** on competence (concentration control). Integrity intact.
*(moot)*

## GATE 5 — INVERSION *(MOOT. Recorded for the file.)*

### Bull-case assumptions, stated explicitly

1. CCBX is a structurally advantaged charter-rental platform with a multi-year lead in
   compliance infrastructure that competitors cannot easily replicate.
2. Credit risk is genuinely transferred — 98.8% indemnified, 100% fraud-covered, backed by
   pledged cash reserve accounts.
3. The Q2 2026 event is **isolated to one partner**, as management states, and does not
   recur.
4. Program fee income compounds at 40–60% and becomes the dominant, capital-light earnings
   stream — $12.0M/quarter and rising.
5. Underlying growth is real: record net interest income +16.4% YoY, core CCBX loans
   (ex-troubled partner) $1.70B, +46% YoY, credit card accounts 881,659 (+32% QoQ).
6. Normalized earnings return to ~$0.80–1.00/quarter, and at $40.99 (~1.35x the $463.4M
   book) the stock is cheap against that.

### The inversion

**Moat destruction — UNRESOLVED.**
Per Gate 2: abundant close substitutes, no binding switching cost, a fragmenting sponsor-
bank market, and a business whose licence to operate is a revocable regulatory permission
in the most scrutinized corner of banking. There is no evidence a partner cannot leave.

**Management failure — UNRESOLVED.**
A single counterparty at 24% of the CCBX book, permitted under a stated framework of
"individual partner portfolio limits," which cost 15% of equity when it broke. The control
failure is demonstrated, not hypothesized.

**Balance-sheet risk — UNRESOLVED, and this is the most serious finding.**

- **Assets/equity at Q2 2026: $5.46B / $463.4M = 11.78:1.** As instructed, noted: this is
  ~11:1, and it is **through the framework's hard 10:1 ceiling for financial businesses**
  (Ruling 2, ratified 2026-07-15 — assets/equity >10:1 is an automatic Gate 4 FAIL, no
  exception, corpus basis the 1990 letter's bank-leverage warning).
  For reference, at Q4 2025 the ratio was $4.74B / $490.96M = **9.65:1** — inside the
  ceiling. **It breached in two quarters.**
- **Tier 1 leverage fell from 10.62% (2025-12-31) to 9.11% (2026-06-30)** — 151bps in two
  quarters. CET1 12.43% → 10.86%. Total risk-based 14.95% → 13.30%. Still "well
  capitalized," but the direction and the speed are the point.
- **Equity fell $40.4M in a single quarter** ($503.8M → $463.4M) on one partner.
- Allowance for credit losses $213.7M (5.08% of total loans); **CCBX-specific reserve
  $198.0M, 8.90% of CCBX loans.** Nonperforming assets $75.2M (1.38% of assets), of which
  CCBX NPLs $69.0M with $67.0M stated as covered by partner credit enhancements — **the
  same species of coverage that just failed.**
- Loan-to-deposit ratio jumped 79.02% → 88.77% in one quarter; total assets fell 3.7% and
  deposits 3.6%.

Two more events of this size and the bank is raising equity dilutively at a depressed
price. **UNRESOLVED.**

**Existence of a thesis-breaking metric — YES.**

### Named thesis-breaking metric and numeric exit threshold

**Primary metric: an additional valuation adjustment to the credit enhancement asset
attributable to a DIFFERENT partner than the Q2 2026 relationship.**

**EXIT THRESHOLD: any such adjustment, in any amount, however small.**

The threshold is deliberately zero-tolerance and that is the entire point. Management's
whole defense — and the whole bull case — is the single word **"isolated."** The bank's
economics depend on the proposition that partner indemnification is a real transfer of
credit risk. One partner failing to honour it can be argued as idiosyncratic. **A second
partner failing to honour it falsifies the business model itself**, not one relationship:
it would establish that thinly-capitalized fintechs systematically cannot absorb the credit
they originate in a downturn, which means Coastal has been holding 8.73%-charge-off
subprime consumer paper on a 4% real margin the entire time. There is no partial version of
that conclusion, so there is no threshold above zero.

**Secondary (capital) thresholds, any one of which fires:**
- Tier 1 leverage ratio below **8.5%** (from 9.11% at 2026-06-30)
- Assets/equity above **12.0:1** (from 11.78:1)
- CCBX exposure to any single partner exceeding **20%** of the CCBX loan book — a limit the
  company's own framework failed to enforce at 24%

### VERDICT — GATE 5: **FAIL** *(moot)*

Three of four inversion categories UNRESOLVED, and the balance-sheet category is not a
forecast of trouble but a description of trouble that has already occurred and is still
being worked out ("one to two quarters to 12 to 18 months" per management).

# CROSS-CUTTING NOTE

These two files fail and pass for instructively opposite reasons.

**Starbucks is a knowable business with a damaged balance sheet.** I can compute its owner
earnings, watch its traffic, and name the number that would tell me I was wrong. The
history contains a genuine, permanent capital-allocation failure — >$24B of quota-driven
buybacks that hollowed out the equity — but the *facts are all visible*, and the current
regime is visibly doing the right things. It passes the gates worked because the risks are
**measurable**.

**Coastal is a well-run bank with an unknowable risk.** Management appears honest, is paid
modestly, built compliance early, and avoided the consent orders that hit its peers. The
Community Bank segment is genuinely good. But the CCBX segment's earnings turn on the
balance sheets of unnamed private companies, and on 2026-07-30 that produced a $54M
quarter-over-quarter swing that no outside analyst could have anticipated. It fails Gate 1
not for want of quality but for want of **visibility**.

The framework's first gate exists precisely to separate those two cases.
