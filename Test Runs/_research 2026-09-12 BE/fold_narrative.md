## THE FINDING: A COST CURVE THAT WENT TO THE CUSTOMER, AND THEN THE METRIC THAT SHOWED IT WAS DELETED

This is the cleanest **[E4-55]** case the project has run, and the cleanest **[E3-62]** case.

net, **product cost per kilowatt**, installation cost per kilowatt — in the MD&A of every 10-K from
FY2018 to FY2023. Set against the filed product revenue and product cost of revenue for the same
years it reconciles **to within $2 per kilowatt**, which is how you know the series is being read
right. What it says:

| 2017 | 62.2 | — | $3,292 | — |
| 2018 | 80.9 | $4,952 | $3,372 | **$1,475** |

**Cost per kilowatt fell $1,264 over six years. Price per kilowatt fell $1,313. The customer took

[E3-62] asks the question nobody asks — *"how much is going to stay home and how much is just going

**Then the FY2024 10-K deleted all four metrics**, in the registrant's own words: *"We have
determined that the foregoing metrics **no longer reflect key operating metrics** … **we will no
longer report on these metrics** … trends in costs and selling prices per kilowatt are **less
representative** … These factors can **distort** the cost or revenue per kilowatt metrics.
Management relies primarily on revenue, **non-GAAP gross and operating margins**, as well as cash
flows from operating activities to manage operations."* **[E2-49] verbatim** — *"Yardsticks seldom
are discarded while yielding favorable readings … disposition of the yardstick rather than
disposition of the manager"* — and the test [E2-49] sets, whether the switch FOLLOWS deterioration,
is answered by the discarded series' own last reading: **2022 was a $107/kW INCREASE in product
cost, the only increase in seven years.** The yardstick that went was the physical one; the
yardstick that replaced it is the one management is **paid on** and the one that **deletes stock
compensation**.

## THE METRIC THAT SURVIVES THE DELETION, AND IT IS THE ONE THAT CLOSES THE FILE

**PRODUCT gross margin: 35.4% (2023) · 36.8% (2024) · 35.2% (2025) · 35.6% (H1 2026) · 36.5% (Q2
2026). FLAT WITHIN 1.3 POINTS WHILE REVENUE TRIPLED.** A business with pricing power inside a
generational shortage of its own product expands its product margin. Bloom's did not move.

**And the trap this run walked into and out of, which is worth recording as a general lesson:** the
*total* gross margin rose from **14.8% to 32.0%** over the same period, and every bull deck in the
sector cites it. **All of it is mix and the absence of impairments** — the loss-making service,
installation and electricity lines fell from 26.9% of revenue in 2023 to 12.5% in H1 2026, and the
**$130.1M (2023) and $115.3M (2022) PPA impairments** dropped out of cost of revenue. **A blended
gross margin can rise by several hundred basis points without a single price changing. Check the
segment line before scoring pricing power off the total** — this is the same defect class as the
consolidated-series problem at [E2-56], one level down.

## THE ANNUITY IS THE WORST LINE, NOT THE BEST — THE AMAT SHAPE, WORSE THAN AT AMAT

The brief predicted this and the filing over-delivered. **Service gross margin was NEGATIVE for
seven of its first eight years:** −20.9% (2018), −4.6%, −20.7%, −2.8%, −11.6%, −20.7%, −0.7%
(2024), and first turned positive in **2025 at +10.0%**, 16.1% in H1 2026. **Q2 2026: Service 18.7%
GAAP against Product 36.5% — a ratio of 0.51 against AMAT's 0.62 — and Bloom prints both numbers
itself on slide 18 of its own Q2 2026 EX-99.2.** Installation has never been profitable in any
filed year.

**The mechanism is worth carrying forward to any razor-and-blade claim**: Bloom's service contract
is a **warranty on a consumable** — it pays for fuel-cell stack replacement. Bloom has *"increased
the life of our fuel cells by over two and half times"*, which is exactly why the margin finally
turned. **The annuity is only profitable to the extent the product stops needing the annuity.** The
two lines are in direct economic opposition, and a "recurring revenue" claim that rests on a
consumable-replacement obligation should be read as a cost, not a moat, until the filed margin says
otherwise.

**So the DELL lesson applies but not as predicted. At DELL one working-capital line WAS the entire

## TOOLING DEFECTS — TWO, AND ONE IS A SILENT [E5-06] VIOLATION ACROSS A WHOLE POPULATION

**1. BOTH SCREEN BAND ENDS REPRODUCE TO THE DOLLAR AND BOTH ARE WRONG — the INTC shape, plus one

  (−156,994).
- `oe_top_m` **−111** = the **3-year FY2023-25 mean at the D&A end with SBC SET TO ZERO** (−110,936).

Two different windows, which is exactly what the INTC run found on 2026-09-07 — **and additionally
a silent violation of [E5-06]**, *"To say 'stock-based compensation' is not an expense is even more
cavalier."* **The cause is a tagging problem, not an arithmetic one: Bloom tags stock compensation as
`AllocatedShareBasedCompensationExpense` DIMENSIONED by expense line, so an undimensioned annual
XBRL fetch returns NOTHING and the screen substituted zero without saying so.** The omission was
**$70M–$200M a year**; with SBC included the FY2021-25 five-year mean at the capex end is
**−$255,363k, not −$157,000k**. **This is not a BE-specific bug. Any filer that tags SBC by expense
line — and multi-line filers generally must — gets its owner earnings overstated by the whole of its
stock compensation, silently.** The fix is the same shape as `cover_shares.py`'s: when the
undimensioned series is empty but dimensioned facts exist, **fail loudly** rather than default to
zero. A screen that returns a band is trusted; a screen that returns nothing gets read.

**2. THE TIER-3 TRIAGE PUT BE IN THE WRONG SUB-CLASS, and the sub-class carried a cost instruction.**

+$444,206k at the capex end and +$504,275k at the D&A end.** BE belongs two rows down with ACMR,
ALKT and ACVA under **SIGN CHANGE**, whose standing instruction — *"the multi-year mean is averaging

## A NEW Q3 SPECIES: $324.4 MILLION OF STOCK PAID TO A CUSTOMER, AND A $72.3 MILLION INDUCEMENT THAT IS PURE DEADWEIGHT

This has not appeared in seventy prior runs and should be watched for. Bloom issued Oracle a warrant
on 3,531,073 shares at $113.28 in connection with the AI-data-centre partnership. **Total stock
handed over on exercise: $324.4M, of which only $17.9M has yet been charged against revenue —
$306.5M sits on the balance sheet as a "Customer Consideration Asset" that will reduce FUTURE
revenue.** The accounting is correct and the disclosure is excellent. The capital allocation is not:

**Gross settlement would have paid Bloom 3,531,073 × $113.28 = $400.0M IN CASH. Bloom forwent all of
it AND paid Oracle 248,798 additional shares worth $72.3M *"as an inducement for Oracle to elect net
settlement"*, to issue 1.4 million fewer shares worth $400.1M at the implied $290.59.** The
cash-for-shares swap is a **wash by construction** — that is what a cashless exercise is — **so the
$72.3M inducement is the entire economic content of the decision and all of it went out.** The
dilution "saved" reappears in the diluted count anyway (323.3M against 287.3M basic). **[E3-50]**
names what the motive looks like — optimising a reported share count — and **[E5-44]** sharpens it:
measured at intrinsic value rather than quote, Bloom paid with the one currency it should have been
hoarding while handing back $400M of cash, holding $2.67bn it has no filed use for. **Stated with
[E4-13]'s humility clause, which is mandatory: Oracle's negotiating position is not in the filing.**

**The generalisable watch item: a revenue-generating warrant issued to a customer is stock
compensation that does not appear in stock compensation.** It lands in APIC and in a
contra-revenue asset, and no SBC line, no SBC/OCF ratio and no non-GAAP reconciliation will show it.
**Check APIC movements against the sum of SBC plus option exercises on any name whose growth arrived
with a named strategic partner.**
