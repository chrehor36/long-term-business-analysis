## THE FOUNDATIONS (not a gate)
A share of AEP is a share of a regulated monopoly whose cash is set by commissions, and the test is whether one would be content to own it "if the market closed for five years" **[M1997-109]**; nothing below depends on the quotation. Two foundations bear hardest. No macro forecast enters **[M2000-094]**: the filing's case for growth is a forecast of data-center load ("AEP Texas has signed Letters of Agreement for an incremental 36 gigawatts of load by 2030", FY2025 10-K, Item 7; a "$72 billion, five year capital plan", same), and the run does not price that forecast; it prices what the filings show the business has produced. Who is paid to tell you: the plan and the load forecast are the seller's projection of what it sells, "don’t ask the barber whether you need a haircut" **[M2011-083]**, and management's pay is tied to the growth of an adjusted earnings figure (Q4, Q6). **Contrary evidence, written down as found** **[M1997-127]**: (1) owner cash after all capital spending was negative in nine of the last ten years and averaged $(2,670)M in 2021 to 2025 (Step 0); the dividend ($2,008M in 2025) and the growth are paid with new debt, new shares every year 2020 to 2025 and a 19.9% sale of a transmission subsidiary; (2) the SEC found in January 2025 that AEP "formed, fully funded, and controlled" a 501(c)(4) that paid organizations of the Ohio Speaker later convicted of racketeering, and that AEP's July 2020 press release about it was misleading (Release No. 11356; Q5); (3) `tools/run.py` read the wrong capital-spending line and would have shown a 7% owner-cash yield (Step 0); (4) the board pays the chief executive a special $10M award "because they believe his strategy is largely responsible for AEP’s current and future stock price performance" (DEF 14A 2026). ## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND IT? Where its economics will be in ten years, inside my perimeter. STOP.
- **The test.** Understanding is "a reasonable fix on about what the earning power and competitive position will look like in five or 10 years" **[M2012-065]**, found by "trying to identify the key variables in that particular business, and evaluating how predictable they were first" **[M1998-044]**.
- **What the business is, from the filing.** A holding company that "directly owns all of the outstanding common stock" of utilities serving parts of eleven states (Arkansas, Indiana, Kentucky, Louisiana, Michigan, Ohio, Oklahoma, Tennessee, Texas, Virginia, West Virginia), about 5.67 million retail customers by the sum of the 10-K's subsidiary table (FY2025 10-K, Item 1), in four segments: Vertically Integrated Utilities (generation, transmission and distribution at state-approved rates), Transmission and Distribution Utilities (AEP Texas in ERCOT, OPCo in Ohio, wires only), AEP Transmission Holdco (FERC-regulated transmission companies; $17.1B in service, "plans to construct approximately $11.6 billion of additional transmission assets through 2030") and Generation & Marketing (unregulated wholesale and retail supply). Retail sales "are made at rates approved by the state utility commissions [...] and are not subject to competition from other vertically integrated public utilities" (Item 1, Competition).
- **The key variables and whether they are foreseeable.** (1) The rate base, which is the capital put in: construction expenditures rose from $4,781M (2016) to $8,453M (2025), and the filed plan is $72B over 2026 to 2030. (2) The return the commissions allow on the equity part of it: the 10-K's jurisdiction table gives authorized returns on equity of 9.25% to 10.50% (Arkansas 9.65%, FERC transmission 9.85% to 10.50%, Indiana 9.85%, Ohio 9.70%, Oklahoma 9.50%, Texas 9.76% and 9.25%, Virginia 9.75%, West Virginia 9.25%), with the note "Actual ROE varies from authorized ROE." (3) Regulatory lag and disallowances (the specified items of 2023 to 2025 include a $181M West Virginia fuel disallowance, an $80M Turk impairment, a $117M Turk refund provision, an $11M Dolet Hills disallowance). (4) The cost and availability of the capital that must be raised every year (Item 1A: "These projects may require levels of capital that exceed historical utility financing needs"). Variables (1), (2) and (4) are foreseeable in the sense the rows ask: the financial statements of a regulated utility "tell me the information that’s useful to me in making a judgment about what the future financial statements are going to look like" **[M2008-033]**, since earnings are a regulated return on a filed rate base; the speakers call the utility's return "more or less — within a range, anyway — a guaranteed return" **[M2009-104]**. The load forecast (36 GW of letters in Texas) is not foreseeable, but it bears on how much capital goes in (Q3), not on whether the economics of what goes in can be seen.
- **Would the insiders write it down?** **[M2000-105]** Yes: the filer writes a five-year capital plan, eight large-load tariffs with "take-or-pay contractual minimums which can require a customer to pay for as much as 90% of their contracted demand", and integrated resource plans by state. This is not the technology field the row describes.
- **Against understanding, written down.** The rows record a utility case where the forecast failed: "it is difficult to project both earnings and asset values in what was once regarded as among the most stable industries in America" **[L2023-011]**, said of states where wildfire liability raised "the specter of zero profitability or even bankruptcy". AEP's 10-K carries a wildfire risk factor for all registrants ("could cause tremendous loss"), and its territories include the Texas and Oklahoma plains; neither California nor Hawaii is among its states. Slow change in technology is named in Item 1A ("advances in batteries or energy storage, wind turbines and photovoltaic solar cells are reducing costs of new technology to levels that are making them competitive with some central station electricity production"); "slow change can be much harder to perceive" **[M2014-038]**. These are threats to the castle, owned by Q2, not a failure to see the economics.
- **The doubt test** **[M2002-092]**: the doubt in this file is about the people (Q5) and the price of the capital (Q3, Q7), not about how the business makes money.
- **VERDICT: IN.** The economics are a regulated return on capital put in, readable from the filings and from the commissions' own orders **[M2012-065]**, **[M2008-033]**, **[M2009-104]**.

## WHAT IN THE FRAMEWORK WAS WRONG OR UNCLEAR
(1) **Integrity across a change of management.** Q5's STOP asks about "who runs it", and its tests read the people; the rows give no rule for a company whose misconduct was found under a former chief executive and whose board and present management partly continue. This run applied the doubt row to the institution through the culture rows (**[M2000-154]**, **[M2000-152]**) and the present management's own post-order statements; another analyst could read the STOP as confined to the present officers. The framework should say whether an enforcement finding against the company, under prior officers, is read at Q5 through the present board and management's response to it, and for how long. (2) **The regulated return and the floor.** Q2 carries a regulated return to Q3 as a second best, but Q7's floor of about ten percent on the price, applied on the all-equity basis, can never be met by a utility whose commissions allow about ten percent on the equity part of book capital, unless the price is far below book; the run did not need to resolve this because Q5 closed first, but every utility run will meet it. (3) **Tax in a regulated business.** Section E's statutory recomputation does not fit a cost-of-service utility, where tax savings flow to customers; the run confessed a CONVENTION not to apply it. (4) **The serial-acquirer test** divides net purchases by owner cash before purchases; when that figure is negative, as for every capital-heavy grower, the test has no meaning; the framework does not say what to do. (5) **Specific (e) with growth spending far above maintenance**: the text says to charge growth spending against the growth it buys but not at what level to carry the spending for ten years (window average, last year, or the filed plan); the run used the window average ($4,761M), well below the filed plan (about $14.4B a year), and said so. (6) `tools/run.py` picks `PaymentsToAcquireProductiveAssets` as capital spending where a filer also tags `PaymentsForConstructionInProcess`; for AEP that is the acquisitions line, a factor of twenty too small in 2023. The tool should prefer the construction tag or print both.
