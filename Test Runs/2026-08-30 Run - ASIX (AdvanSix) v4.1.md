## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**

- **Unit economics in my own words:** AdvanSix buys cumene (made from benzene and
  propylene), natural gas and sulfur, and runs them through one vertically integrated
  U.S. chain — cumene → phenol/acetone at Frankford PA → caprolactam + ammonium
  sulfate at Hopewell VA (four pounds of ammonium sulfate co-produced per pound of
  caprolactam) → Nylon 6 resin at Chesterfield VA — plus a small amines business.
  It earns **volume × the spread between commodity selling prices and commodity raw
  material costs**. Customer contracts are largely formula-based pass-throughs of raw
  material costs; what is left over is the market spread, which the world sets.
  FY2025: sales $1,522M, NI $49.3M, OCF $122.9M, capex $116.4M. About 86% of sales
  are U.S.; largest customer Shaw (carpet), ~9% of sales.
- **The scarce input this business controls:** honestly — none that is scarce. What it
  has is the world's largest single-site integrated caprolactam/ammonium-sulfate
  complex (its own filing's claim: "world's lowest cost producer of caprolactam"),
  plus U.S. natural gas — a location rent shared with every U.S. producer. Scale here
  is fixed-cost spreading, which is an advantage only while utilization is high;
  that observation is Q2's business.
- **Ten years:** the mechanism will look identical — the same chain, the same
  commodity spreads, the same cycle. The filing's own cyclicality language promises
  it. The LEVEL of earnings in any given year is a supply-demand outcome and not
  predictable; the mechanism is.
- **VERDICT: [x] IN** — a legible, stable-in-character commodity converter. Whether
  legible economics are franchise economics is Q2's question.
