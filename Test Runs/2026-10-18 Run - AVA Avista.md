## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND IT? Where its economics will be in ten years, inside my perimeter. STOP.
- **The test.** Understanding is "a reasonable fix on about what the earning power and competitive position will look like in five or 10 years" **[M2012-065]**, built from "the key variables in that particular business, and evaluating how predictable they were first" **[M1998-044]**.
- **The key variables, from the filing.** (1) Rate base: net utility property $6,319M at 2025-12-31, up from $5,226M at 2021-12-31, about 4.9% a year (10-K balance sheets, accessions `0001193125-26-067872`, `0000950170-23-003546`). (2) The allowed return: Washington ROE 9.8% on a 48.5% equity ratio (order of December 2024), Idaho 9.6% (settlement of August 2025), Oregon 9.5%, Alaska 11.45% (10-K, Regulatory Matters). (3) The gap between the allowed and the earned return, which Item 7 attributes to "Regulatory lag" with "no protection against unexpected inflation and increased interest rates". (4) Load: average hourly load 1,149 aMW in 2025, 1,117 in 2024, 1,115 in 2023; the 2025 IRP forecasts energy demand growth of "0.9 percent per year over the next 20 years" (10-K, Item 1). (5) Event risk: wildfire, Item 1A's first operational risk.
- **Are they foreseeable?** The first four are set in public proceedings years ahead: Washington's multi-year rate plan of January 2026 asks for specified revenue increases for each of 2027 to 2030 (8-K 2026-01-16), and the company files a twenty-year resource plan every two years. The speakers name this exact case as the easy valuation: "the classic case used to be a water or electric utility where the cash could be deployed and the return was more or less guaranteed within a narrow range, and it was very easy to make calculations" **[M1998-058]**; and "when you get an allowed return in the electric utility you’re almost certain of earning it, I mean, if you behave yourself" **[M2010-037]**. The insiders do write the forecast down (test 5, **[M2000-105]**): the IRP and the rate plan are such forecasts. This is a customer-and-regulator forecast, not a technology one (test 7, **[M2023-030]**).
- **Against.** The 2023 letter says that in "a few states" the regulatory climate makes it "difficult to project both earnings and asset values in what was once regarded as among the most stable industries in America" **[L2023-011]**. The filing does not place Washington, Idaho or Oregon among those states: Idaho enacted a Wildfire Standard of Care Act in 2025 and Washington a wildfire-plan law (HB 1522) the same year (10-K, Item 1); the Babb Road fire litigation of 2020 settled for $27M, $21M of it the company's, fully insured (Note 22). Whether that holds is the event risk, which Q9 weighs; it does not make the ten-year economics of a regulated distributor unforeseeable. Test 8, "how far off we can be" **[M2011-084]**: the earned return has stayed inside a band of about 6.5% to 8.6% for ten years (Q3), which is a narrow miss band.
- **Routing.** Not a fast-changing industry; not a financial institution; the segments are read by parts at Q2 (Avista Utilities carries the earnings).
- **VERDICT: IN.** The economics of a regulated electric and gas distributor in the Inland Northwest can be foreseen in rough terms ten years out from the filings and the rate proceedings **[M2012-065]**, **[M1998-058]**; the doubt rule **[M2002-092]** was put to the wildfire question and that question is sent to Q9, where the rows put "what is one event?" **[M2024-021]**.

## Q2 — WHY IS THE CASTLE STILL STANDING, and what keeps it standing ten to twenty years? STOP.
The question: "why is that castle still standing? And what’s going to keep it standing or cause it not to be standing five, 10, 20 years from now." **[M1995-038]**

- **1. The castle questions.** The reason the customer comes is that there is no other wire or main to his house: the 10-K says the distribution business "has historically been recognized as a natural monopoly", with rates set "on a 'cost of service' basis" and service-territory agreements with neighbouring cooperatives and public utility districts (Item 7, Competition; accession `0001193125-26-067872`). 429,000 electric and 386,000 gas customers over 34,000 square miles (Item 1). The reason it lasts is the franchise and the regulatory compact, which the speakers describe from the owner's side as "monopoly service in most cases", in return for which the regulators are asked "to allow us an appropriate return" **[L2009-011]**.
- **2. Would it stand without the lord?** Largely yes: the castle is the franchise, not a person. But the return depends on the standing with the regulators, which the speakers treat as earned by conduct, "we have a deserved good reputation with the regulators" **[M2014-087]**, and on efficiency, since a "sloppy" operation once did fine and "That's all changing." **[L2015-007]**.
- **3. The money test.** Duplicating the distribution grid is the attacker's problem; the 10-K names the attackers that remain: cooperatives and public utility districts for new customers, customer-sited solar and storage, fuel switching away from gas, and large customers who "bypass" or buy their power independently (Item 7, Competition). One did: a large industrial customer "with their own transmission rights" returned to buying power in the market from April 2026, cutting expected 2026 net income by $9M (Item 7, 2026 Customer Load). Item 1A also names "the risk of municipalization or other form of service territory reduction".
- **4. Pricing power and the agony.** Price is set by commissions after proceedings of up to eleven months (Item 7). In Washington's 2024 case the company asked $77M for rate year one and was granted $12M, partly on lower power costs and partly on "a lower approved return on equity than requested"; the WUTC "did not approve our request to modify the ERM", and actual net power supply costs exceeded those in rates by $78M in 2025 (Item 7, Regulatory Matters). Oregon's House Bill 3179 of 2025 bars residential increases from November to March and spaces ROE cases at least eighteen months apart (Item 7). This is the "prayer session" of the test in statutory form **[M2005-020]**; the rows expect it of the industry: "The whole idea behind the utility industry is not to allow extraordinary profits." **[M2000-131]**.

A comparison of Avista's retail rates with its neighbours': no instance found in the 10-K (text search for "average rate" and "lower rates"); not weighed.

## Q3 — HOW MUCH CAPITAL MUST GO IN to get the earnings out, and what does the added capital earn? WEIGHING.
The test as the rows put it for this kind of business: "We expect only that these businesses have reasonable expectations of earning decent returns on the incremental sums they invest." **[L2009-012]**; "cash-consuming businesses, by their nature, are unattractive unless the cash they consume gets to earn a reasonable return" **[M2012-057]**.

- **What it earns on the capital it needs.** Net income over average book equity was 6.5% to 7.3% in each year 2020 to 2025 and 6.9% to 8.6% in 2016 to 2018 (Q2 table; 2019's 10.6% includes the $103M Hydro One termination fee, FY2019 10-K, accession `0000104918-20-000036`). The allowed returns are 9.4% to 9.8% in the three state jurisdictions (Q1). The speakers' own description of the industry is that an allowed return in an electric utility is one "you’re almost certain of earning" **[M2010-037]**; Avista has not earned it in any year read. On total capital, net income plus after-tax interest over equity plus net debt was about 5.2% in 2025 ($193M plus $148M of net interest at 21%, over $2,709M of equity and $3,276M of net debt; 10-K income statement and capitalization table).
- **How much must be reinvested to stand still, and to grow.** Capital spending ran $404M to $570M a year against depreciation of $224M to $289M (2020 to 2025), about twice depreciation (XBRL, cross-checked for 2025 at Step 0). Item 7: "Our annual net cash flows from operating activities usually do not fully support the amount required for annual utility capital expenditures"; the 2026 plan is "up to $230 million of long-term debt and up to $90 million of common stock". Owner cash after all capital spending was negative in every year read (Step 0). The 2025 IRP calls for about 490 MW of new capacity by 2030 and 950 MW by 2035 (Item 1). This is the compulsory kind of reinvestment: "To earn more, their owners must invest more." **[L2011-019]**; and in inflation the depreciation charge understates the need, "your depreciation charges are inadequate and you’re kidding yourself as to your real economic profits" **[M2015-049]**.
- **What the added capital earned.** Averages of three years at each end, so that no single base year decides **[L2005-003]**: net income rose from $129.8M (2016 to 2018) to $181.3M (2023 to 2025), $51.5M more, while average book equity rose from $1,717M to $2,595M, $878M more: about **5.9% on the added equity**, below the 7% the business earns on its old equity, below every allowed return, and close to the long government rate of 5.43%. Net income plus after-tax interest rose by about $100M on about $1,600M more net utility plant over the same ends, about 6.2% (approximate; plant is not rate base). Per share the added capital bought little: diluted EPS $1.97 (2015) to $2.38 (2025), about 1.9% a year (XBRL), while shares outstanding rose from 62.3M (cover, early 2016) to 83.75M (cover, July 2026), about 34%.
- **The growth arithmetic.** "There’s a huge difference in the business that grows and requires a lot of capital to do so, and the business that grows, and doesn’t require capital" **[M1994-081]**. Here the growth is entirely of the first kind, and the speakers' own BHE says what they expect of it: "we like the idea of getting more capital out at reasonable returns", the return "measured by the operating earnings after depreciation" **[M2014-086]**. Earning 6% or less on the incremental dollar, funded partly by new shares sold at about the price the market sets, is not the decent incremental return of **[L2009-012]**; the rows' warning on long holding of low returns applies with some force, "if you have a business that’s earning 5 or 6 percent on equity and you hold it for a long time, you are not going to do well in investing" **[M1998-006]**.
- **WEIGHS AGAINST.** A capital-heavy regulated business may be a second best **[M2018-055]**, but only on decent returns on the added sums **[L2009-012]**; Avista's added capital has earned about 6% on equity, below its allowed returns and its neighbours', with growth funded by new debt and new shares **[L2011-019]**, **[M1998-006]**. (The "little or no debt" criterion is applied at Q9.)
