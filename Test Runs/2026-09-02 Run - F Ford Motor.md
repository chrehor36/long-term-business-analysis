## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**

**The operator's question — "an automaker with a bank attached; if the halves cannot be
cleanly separated from the filings, decide honestly between UNRESEARCHED and UNKNOWABLE" —
is answered first, because it decides the verdict.**

**THEY CAN BE SEPARATED, COMPLETELY, AND FORD PUBLISHES THE SEPARATION ITSELF.** Every 10-K
and 10-Q carries a *"Supplemental Information"* section with a full **three-statement**
split — income statement, balance sheet **and** cash-flow statement — on a **Company
excluding Ford Credit / Ford Credit / Eliminations / Consolidated** basis, with an explicit
`Interest supplements and residual value support to Ford Credit` line reconciling the
intercompany subsidy. Ford Credit is additionally its own SEC registrant. **Judged against
the competitor row, this is the top of the class**: Toyota's Item 5 two-way split is its
equal; **Honda discloses no segment cash flow and no sector balance sheet**; **Stellantis has
no financial-services segment and does not disclose its finance arm's pre-tax income at
all**; Tesla has no captive finance to separate. **No Q1 separation problem exists. The
finding of this run was only possible because Ford published the data, and that is a real
candour credit, recorded again at Q3 [E2-26].**

- **Unit economics in my own words, without management's language.** Ford does two unrelated
  things and calls them one company. **(1) It manufactures.** It buys steel, aluminium,
  chips, batteries and 30,000-odd parts, runs them through owned plants staffed largely by
  UAW labour, and sells **4.39 million vehicles a year wholesale** to franchised dealers for
  **$174.0 billion**. Against that it charges **$185.3 billion** of cost of sales and
  SG&A — *"Cost of sales and Selling, administrative, and other expenses for full year 2025
  were $185.3 billion"* — so in 2025 the manufacturing operation **lost $11.3 billion at the
  operating line.** Its three recurring cost blocks are material and commodity, **$8,973M of
  warranty and field-service accrual**, and **$9,400M of R&D expensed in the year**. It
  capitalises **$8.7 billion a year of tooling and plant** to keep the product line current.
  **(2) It lends.** Ford Credit buys the retail instalment contract or the lease from the
  dealer at origination, funds it with **$141.4 billion of debt at 9.6:1 leverage**, and
  earns the spread — **$13.3 billion of revenue, $2,557M of pre-tax income, 14.9% return on
  its own equity.** The manufacturer pays the lender **$4,011M a year** of interest
  supplements and residual support so the lender can quote a subsidised rate; that payment is
  a *marketing cost of the car business* and Ford's sector statement shows it moving between
  the two columns.
- **The scarce input this business controls.** *Tested rather than asserted, and the honest
  answer is uncomfortable.* Not steel, not chips, not batteries, not labour — all bought in
  competitive markets. Not the dealer — franchised and independent. The candidates are **(i)
  the F-Series/Super Duty position in the US full-size pickup market**, tested specifically
  at Q2, and **(ii) Ford Credit's cost of funds and its captive access to the dealer at the
  point of sale**, which is real and is the reason the finance arm out-earns the factory. The
  10-K's own forward-looking risk list states the dependence without a number: *"Ford's
  results are dependent on sales of **larger, more profitable vehicles**, particularly in the
  United States."*
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** **The demand side, yes**:
  Americans will buy pickup trucks and commercial vans, and Ford will build them. **The cost
  side and the product mix, honestly no** — but the change has just *reversed*, not begun.
  Ford spent roughly $28 billion on electrification in three years, then in December 2025
  cancelled three EV programmes, ended F-150 Lightning production and took **$13.8 billion of
  charges in a single quarter**, with *"about $3 billion"* more expected in H1 2026. That is
  a change in character, and [E3-31] says a business *"subject to constant change"* fails
  here. **It is not scored as a Q1 failure, for two reasons stated openly.** First, [E3-55]
  scopes it: *"If we have a business about which we're extremely confident as to the business
  result, we would prefer that it have high volatility than low volatility"* — and the
  mechanism (build trucks, sell trucks, lend against trucks) is intelligible and stable even
  where the annual figure is violent. Second, and decisively for honesty: **the analyst's
  favourite hypothesis is that Ford fails, and failing it at Q1 on a judgment that properly
  belongs at Q2 and Q4 would be exactly the shortcut [E4-26] warns against.** The doubts here
  are about the *level* and the *durability*, which are the next two questions'.
- **[E4-46] check:** *"if we can't make a decision in five minutes, we can't make it in five
  months."* This business does not require five months. It required one day and one arithmetic
  separation that Ford itself publishes.

- **VERDICT: [x] IN.** How Ford makes money is intelligible, the automotive/finance halves
  separate cleanly from filed statements, and *"the math is not complicated"* [E3-27].
