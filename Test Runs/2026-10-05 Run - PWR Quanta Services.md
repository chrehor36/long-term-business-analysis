- **What the business is, from the filing:** a specialty contractor that sells crews, equipment and project management to electric and gas utilities, power developers and, newly, data-center and industrial owners. "Our operations are decentralized and labor-intensive, and we rely on craft skilled labor personnel" (10-K FY2025, Item 1). Two segments: Electric ($23.0B revenue, segment operating margin 10.3% in FY2025) and Underground and Infrastructure ($5.5B, 7.3%), before $1.15B of corporate and unallocated costs including amortization (10-K FY2025, MD&A, Segment Results). Contract types FY2025: fixed price 60.6%, unit price 23.8%, cost-plus 15.6% (Note 4). About 20% of work is subcontracted (MD&A). 69,500 employees, 55,700 of them hourly; 36% under collective bargaining (Item 1).

(3) What management pays for acquisitions: a question of the people (Q5, Q6). The work itself changes slowly: linework, substations and energized work by "bare hand and hot stick methods" (Item 1) are old trades. The doubt rule **[M2002-092]** was asked: the analyst's doubt is about the durability of the edge (Q2), not about how the business makes its money.

**The castle tests, each with its filing fact.**
1. **The castle questions** **[M1995-038]**. The filing names its key factors: "safety leadership, responsiveness and performance, geographic reach, comprehensive service offerings and financial strength", "preferred service provider status", strategic alliance agreements that "many provide us with preferential bidding procedures" (Item 1). How permanent: the customer relationships are held at the operating companies, where "management at each of our operating companies is primarily responsible for developing and maintaining successful long-term relationships with customers" (Item 1). So part of the advantage sits with the lords, not the castle. The rows' warning is that such a moat "will go when the surgeon goes" **[L2007-006]**.
2. **The attacker with money.** "if I had a hundred million dollars and I wanted to go in and take on See’s Candy, could I do it?" **[M2011-015]**. Here the attackers already exist and are growing in the same markets: MasTec Power Delivery revenue +15.6% in FY2025, Primoris Utilities +10.4%, EMCOR buying Miller Electric, Dycom adding Building Systems (competitor 10-Ks). Quanta itself shows how an attacker enters: by buying contractors ($8.1B cash over five years). The filer's own words: "organizations that have adequate financial resources and access to technical expertise may become a competitor", and its subcontractors "may develop the expertise necessary to compete with us" (Item 1). Against this stands a competitor's own filing, the nearest public answer to the question the rows would put to a competitor **[M1999-130]**. MYR Group writes: "There are a number of barriers to entry into the T&D markets, including the cost of equipment and tooling necessary to perform T&D work, availability of qualified labor" (MYRG 10-K FY2025, Item 1). Split.
3. **Pricing power and the agony before a rise** **[M2005-020]**. No list price exists; prices are bid. Quanta: "price is often an important factor in the award of such agreements. Accordingly, we could be underbid by our competitors" (Item 1). MYR: "most of their work is awarded through a bid process where price is always a principal factor" (MYRG 10-K FY2025). No evidence of pricing power found in the filings read.
4. **Unit volume and share of mind.** Not the test for a contractor; backlog is the nearest measure, and it grew faster than at MYR and MasTec (row above).
5. **The low-cost position** **[L2004-007]**, **[L2000-017]**, **[M1997-010]**. Two facts point toward a cost or scale edge in utility T&D: Quanta's Electric segment margin sits above MYR's T&D segment margin in FY2024 and FY2025 and above MasTec's Power Delivery adjusted-EBITDA margin in FY2023 to FY2025, and its consolidated operating margin sat above MYR's in each of FY2016 to FY2019 and above Primoris's in three of those four years (FY2017: 4.0% against 4.5%) (row above). Two facts point against its being "the low-cost producer" the rows mean: return on total assets ranks fifth of six, and on tangible assets roughly level with MYR and Primoris (STEP 0 and COMPUTATION section), and the filing ties part of the margin to scarce labor ("competition may lessen as industry resources, such as labor supplies, approach capacity", Item 1). The margin lead is shown; that it is a cost advantage, and that it holds when labor is not scarce, is not shown.
6. **The brand in the customer's mind.** The nearest thing is a safety reputation with utility customers (Item 1, Human Capital). Not tested further; no filing fact measures it.
7. **Would the customer still choose it over the low bid?** "it wouldn’t be a question of people buying candy for the low bid" **[M2017-009]**; "people don’t simply just take the low bid" **[M2016-006]**. The filing says both: price is "often an important factor" and "customers often consider other factors ... which we expect to benefit larger service providers such as us" (Item 1). The share of work won by bid against work awarded under alliances and MSAs is not disclosed; contract type (84.4% fixed or unit price) is not award method. Undecided on the filings read.
8. **Ask the competitors** **[M1999-130]**, **[M2025-043]**: MasTec describes its field as "large national firms that service regional and national markets, as well as small independent companies" (MTZ 10-K FY2025, Item 1); none of the competitor 10-Ks read says which competitor it fears. Scuttlebutt was not done (no interviews in this run).
9. **Widening or narrowing** **[M1999-108]**, **[L2005-010]**. Consolidated operating margin rose from 4.2% (FY2016) to 5.7% (FY2025); Electric segment margin from 9.4% (FY2023) to 10.3% (FY2024 and FY2025); backlog grew 27.3% in FY2025 and a further 21.5% in the first half of 2026 to $53.44B (10-Q). On the evidence the position is not narrowing. But the years of widening are the years of the tightest craft-labor market and the largest utility programs, which the filing itself names as the condition under which "competition may lessen". The rows name "a cyclical peak in earnings" as one of the explanations to rule out before a high return is credited **[L1994-009]**.
10. **What could destroy it, five to fifteen years out** **[M2000-014]**: the end of the utility and data-center build, after which price competition returns in a field the filing says has few barriers in parts; utilities taking work in-house ("The in-house service organizations of certain of our existing or prospective customers ... employ personnel who perform some of the same types of services we provide", Item 1); a wildfire liability through the indemnities it gives under its MSAs (Silverado matter, Note 16); fixed-price losses as fixed-price work rises to 60.6% of revenue.

**The returns the moat would protect.** On the capital the business needs (tangible), the returns are high: about $2.1B of FY2025 operating income before amortization (10-K FY2025, $1,612M operating income plus $499M amortization) against tangible capital employed of roughly $5B to $6B (COMPUTATION section), which is what labor-for-hire businesses earn: "The service businesses are generally the better businesses. They require less capital" **[M2009-047]**. On that same tangible basis the two closest T&D peers earn about the same (COMPUTATION section), so the tangible return is the industry's, not evidence of a castle; and the rows prefer "the less labor intensive business" **[M1997-111]**. On all the capital the owners have put in, including $10.2B of goodwill and intangibles paid, the return is ordinary: net income to equity 11.5% and to total assets 4.1% in FY2025 **[M2011-060]**, **[M2010-090]**.

**Reading.** The evidence runs both ways and does not settle the deciding fact. Against a castle: the filer's own statements that barriers are few in parts of its field and that price decides awards often enough to be underbid; a competitor's statement that price is always a principal factor; ordinary returns on all capital; an edge the filer itself links to a tight labor market. For a castle: a margin lead over its T&D peers that is visible in the segment figures for FY2024 and FY2025 (FY2023 against MasTec) and, less consistently, in the consolidated figures back to FY2016; the largest craft workforce, training system (Northwest Lineman College, the Quanta Advanced Training Center) and fleet ("approximately 80,000 units", Item 1) in the field; a competitor naming labor and equipment as T&D barriers. The castle is not shown to be filling in, so OUT is not supported. The rows' test is "how wide the moat is and whether it’s likely to widen further or shrink on you" **[M1999-108]**, and on the record margins and backlog have widened, not narrowed. It is not shown to be standing either: whether the margin lead is a durable low-cost position **[L2004-007]** or the product of a tight labor market and a capital boom **[L1994-009]** is exactly what "what’s going to keep it standing" asks **[M1995-038]**, and the filings read do not answer it. That is a moat "tenuous" on the evidence, which the rows leave alone because "We don’t know how to valuate that" **[M2000-019]**: the box is "too hard" **[M2006-013]**, not OUT.

**Which cause.** The deciding question, whether Quanta's T&D margin and return edge over its closest peers persisted through the slack years before the present boom (FY2014 to FY2020), is important and knowable **[M2006-076]**: it sits in the segment tables of Quanta's, MYR's and MasTec's own 10-Ks for those years, which this run did not read. Industry insiders would write this down; it is history, not a forecast of a fast-changing industry **[M2000-105]**. The cause is therefore the analyst's, "I haven’t done the work" **[M1994-026]**, not the industry's **[L1993-023]**.

- **VERDICT: TOO HARD (WORK).** The castle's durability cannot be judged from the filings read; the deciding question is knowable from the earlier filings and is set out as the research pass below (Part VII). Price does not enter this verdict **[M2000-038]**.
