## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? [E3-31]

- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language.** Landstar is **a clearing house that
  owns almost nothing and employs almost nobody.** In fiscal 2025 it moved **2,004,890 loads** and
  billed shippers **$4,743.8 million** for them, an average of **$2,366 a load**. Out of every
  revenue dollar it paid **77.8 cents to the truck, train, ship or plane that actually hauled the
  freight** and **8.2 cents to the independent salesperson who found it**, keeping **14.1 cents**.
  That 14.1 cents — Landstar calls it *variable contribution* — was **$668.0 million**, and it is
  the only number in the business that matters. Out of it Landstar paid **$159.4 million of
  insurance and claims, $230.5 million of head-office cost, $61.6 million of trailer maintenance and
  bad debts, and $46.4 million of depreciation**, leaving **$151.6 million of operating income** —
  and 2025's figure is after **$32.2 million of impairments**. It did this with **1,294 employees**
  (excluding the Mexican subsidiary held for sale), which is **$3.67 million of revenue per
  employee**, because the 960 salespeople are independent contractors and the 8,514 truck drivers
  are independent contractors too.
- **There are two different businesses inside that one number and the run separates them at Q2.**
  **38% of revenue** is hauled by **BCO Independent Contractors** — owner-operators who lease their
  truck exclusively to Landstar and pull a Landstar trailer. **53%** is hauled by **Truck Brokerage
  Carriers** — 36,852 unrelated trucking companies with no exclusivity at all. Landstar's own
  language, verbatim: *"Historically, variable contribution margin … generated from freight hauled
  by BCO Independent Contractors has been **greater than that from freight hauled by other third
  party capacity providers**."* The arithmetic at Q2 puts the gap at roughly **22.7% versus 8.8%**.
- **The balance sheet is a working-capital machine and nothing else.** Total assets $1,635.9M, of
  which **$670.1M is trade receivables**, $396.7M is cash, $55.5M is investments and only **$261.3M
  is operating property** — 16% of assets, and most of that is 17,426 trailers. There is no
  inventory. There is no goodwill worth speaking of ($34.0M after a $7.5M impairment). **Landstar
  finances shippers for about 51 days and pays its capacity providers faster, and that gap is the
  capital employed.** When revenue grows the gap widens and consumes cash; when revenue falls it
  releases cash. That is Stage 0 leg (d).
- **The scarce input this business controls: the exclusive lease on 8,514 owner-operator trucks,
  and the local shipper relationships of 77 agencies that each bill more than $10 million a year.**
  Not the freight — that is a commodity priced daily in a spot market. Not the software — Landstar
  buys and builds it like everyone else. Not scale in the brokerage leg — 36,852 non-exclusive
  carriers will haul for anybody. **What is genuinely scarce is (i) a captive fleet of drivers who
  have signed away the right to haul for a competitor and who pull equipment Landstar owns, and
  (ii) a distributed sales force whose members have spent decades inside particular shipper
  accounts and, per the filing, "represent the Company exclusively."** Both are contractual,
  both are terminable, and both are counted every year in the 10-K. **That is the whole moat
  question and it is tested at Q2 on those two counts.**
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** **Yes.** Freight will move by truck;
  someone will match a load to a truck; that matcher will keep a single-digit-to-mid-teens
  percentage of the freight bill. Landstar has run this model since its 1993 IPO. The named changes
  are not to the model: they are **(i) whether the matching spread is competed down by software,
  tested at Q2 in the numbers rather than the narrative, and (ii) whether the legal cost of standing
  between a shipper and a truck has permanently risen, which is the Montgomery question at Q4.**
- **[E4-46] check:** this does not need five months. Two segments, one of which is 1% of revenue;
  a 353,000-character 10-K with plain notes; a single revenue line and two cost lines that explain
  90% of the income statement. **It is inside the circle.**
- **What is NOT understood, stated rather than skipped:** the ultimate cost of self-insured
  commercial trucking claims. Landstar retains **$5 million per occurrence** and reserves
  actuarially, and eleven consecutive years of adverse development (Q3) say the estimate has been
  wrong in the same direction every time. **That is an estimate, not an unknowable — it is priced
  into (c) and the death model, not used to fail Q1.**

### FRANCHISE OR OPERATING LEVERAGE? — the operator's distinction, answered

- **OPERATING LEVERAGE is what the variable-cost model buys, and it is a balance-sheet virtue, not a
  moat.** Landstar's own words: *"The nature of the Company's business is such that **a significant
  portion of its operating costs varies directly with revenue**."* When revenue fell 36% from 2022
  to 2025, purchased transportation and commissions fell 36% with it, automatically. **That is why
  Landstar earned $151.6M of operating income in the worst freight market in decades. It is
  arithmetic, not advantage — and the competitor row proves it is not proprietary: CHRW carries net
  property of $116M and RXO $134M. The asset-light structure is shared, not owned.**
- **What it did NOT protect is the return on the spread, where the fixed cost sits.** Landstar
  publishes the ratio itself: **operating income as a percentage of variable contribution was 56.1%
  (2022), 44.6% (2023), 36.5% (2024), 22.7% (2025)** — while **CHRW's equivalent went 19.8% → 24.2%
  → 29.1%.** The variable costs flexed on both sides; only one of the two took cost out of the
  fixed half.
- **THE FRANCHISE, if there is one, is the BCO leg and nothing else.** 8,514 trucks under
  **exclusive** lease pulling **13,784 company-owned trailers**, earning a contractually fixed
  spread no freight cycle can compress because it is a fixed percentage of revenue. **No listed peer
  has this asset.** It is 38% of revenue, 61% of the spread, and 28% smaller than in 2021.

### NAME THE SPECIFIC WAY THIS BUSINESS DIES [E2-27, E3-24, E4-40, E4-51]

**Two mechanisms. The first kills the earnings; the second kills the thesis. Neither kills the

#### DEATH 1 — BROKER LIABILITY AFTER MONTGOMERY. Likelihood: the recurring charge is LIKELY; a single verdict above the insurance tower is A REAL POSSIBILITY.

**The mechanism, from the filing.** Landstar arranges roughly **1,145,750 brokered truckload
movements a year** on non-exclusive carriers (1,943,800 total truck loads less 798,050 hauled by
BCOs). When one of those carriers kills someone, the plaintiff sues the broker for negligent
selection. **Until 14 May 2026 the FAAAA preempted that claim. It no longer does.**

Landstar's Q2 2026 10-Q, Item 1A, verbatim:

> *"On May 14, 2026, the U.S. Supreme Court issued its decision in **Montgomery v. Caribe Transport
> II, LLC**, in which the Company is not a party, holding that the Broker Liability Claim at issue
> fell within the "safety exception" under the FAAAA and therefore is not preempted by the FAAAA.
> The decision **narrowed a freight broker's ability to rely on federal preemption as a defense** to
> Broker Liability Claims and may increase the frequency, severity and potential loss associated
> with such claims. **Following the decision, others in our industry have faced Broker Liability
> Claims resulting in Nuclear Verdicts in excess of $100 million.**"*

**Quantified from filed figures:**
- Landstar **retains $5,000,000 per commercial trucking occurrence**. Between $5M and $10M sits the
  2023 Initial Excess Policy, with an **$18,000,000 aggregate deductible over its 37-month term and
  a $15,000,000 aggregate limit**. Above $10M is third-party excess coverage *"on a per occurrence
  or aggregated basis."*
- **The price of that tower has already moved: *"Since the annual policy year ended April 30, 2020,
  as compared to the annual policy year ending May 31, 2026, the Company experienced an increase of
  approximately $22 million, or approximately 400%, in the premiums charged by third party insurance
  companies to the Company for excess coverage for commercial trucking liabilities in excess of $10
  million."*** **$22 million a year is 14.5% of 2025 operating income, and it was spent before
  Montgomery.**
- **The Cabral matter is the template and it has already run once:** a jury apportioned **15%, or
  $3.42 million**, to Landstar Ranger; the trial court then entered judgment for **100% of the $22.8
  million plus pre-judgment interest**. **A 6.7x multiplication between the verdict and the
  judgment, on a matter where the jury had already found the broker mostly not at fault.**
- **The recurring charge, modelled in [E3-24]'s form.** Consider some arithmetic. Net unfavourable
  development of prior years' claims averaged **$8.6 million a year over 2015–2024**. It was
  **$32.1 million in 2025** and is running at **$15.4 million in the first half of 2026** — roughly
  $31 million annualised, before Montgomery's effect can have reached the reserves. **Suppose the
  post-Montgomery steady state is $25 million a year of incremental claims cost above the historic
  mean.** Against five-year mean owner earnings of **$275 million** that is a **permanent 9% charge**;
  against 2025's operating income of $151.6 million it is **16%**. **We consider that likely, not a
  possibility — it is already in two consecutive filings.**
- **The tail.** Landstar's peers have already suffered verdicts over $100 million since May 2026.
  **A $100 million loss net of insurance is 12.6% of shareholders' equity, 66% of 2025 operating
  income, and about 22% of cash and investments. It would be a very bad year. It would not be a
  solvency event** — which is why this death takes the earnings and not the company.
- **[E4-40] is the reason this section exists in this form.** *"all of us in the industry made a
  fundamental underwriting mistake by focusing on experience, rather than exposure."* **Landstar's
  loss experience is benign. Its exposure is 1.15 million brokered loads a year with the federal
  preemption defence removed four months ago. The experience is useless as a guide and, in the
  corpus's word, dangerous.**

**The mechanism, quantified from Q2's arithmetic.** Landstar's spread is 22.7 cents on BCO revenue
and 8.8 cents on everything else. **The BCO fleet has fallen 28% from its peak. If BCO revenue share

contribution falls from $668.0M to $615.3M — a loss of $52.7 million, 35% of 2025 operating income,

**This is not hypothetical. It has already run once:** BCO revenue share went from **45% (2019) to
38% (2025)**, and on 2025 revenue that shift alone cost about **$46 million of variable

**[E2-27]'s image fits exactly: each participant's decision is individually rational; collectively
they neutralise each other and returns stay anemic.**
