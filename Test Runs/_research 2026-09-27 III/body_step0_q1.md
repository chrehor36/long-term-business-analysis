## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY? **[E3-31]**
- **Unit economics in my own words, no management language.** A large company or a state government about to buy or renegotiate a big outsourcing, cloud, software or telecom contract hires ISG to tell it what the deal should cost and to run the tender. ISG sells the time of experienced advisers, by the hour or for a fixed or capped fee (*"Revenues for time and materials contracts ... are recognized based on the number of hours worked by our advisors at an agreed-upon rate per hour"*), and sometimes a share of the savings it negotiates (contracts *"can be based on the level of savings achieved"*). Around that core it sells research subscriptions and provider ratings (ISG Provider Lens, which technology vendors also buy), events paid for mostly by vendor sponsorships, subscriptions to a supplier-management platform used after the deal is signed (GovernX), and multi-year public-sector contracts; the company calls these *"recurring"* and puts them at *"46 percent of our firmwide total in 2025"*. **Revenue $244.7M in 2025** (Americas $160.9M, Europe $65.5M, Asia Pacific $18.3M), down 1% after the automation unit was sold; **$126.7M in the first half of 2026**, up 5%. The costs are people: *"Direct costs and expenses for advisors"* $139.3M, SG&A $83.1M; operating income $17.8M (7.3%). Profit is set by how many of the advisers' hours are billed: *"Our profitability depends to a large extent on the utilization of our consultants"*. Clients can walk: agreements *"typically allow our clients to terminate early due to breach or for convenience with 30 days’ notice"*.
- **The scarce input this business controls.** By the filer's account, its benchmark data (*"nearly 10 million real-world data points from benchmarking and sourcing contracts"*; *"ISG monitors over 180,000 unique technology service contracts"*), its independence from the vendors whose contracts it negotiates, its brand in sourcing advice (*"over 30 years of benchmarking and sourcing experience"*, the TPI inheritance) and the experience of its advisers (*"an average of more than 20 years"*). Whether any of it is scarce is Q2's question; the advisers are not owned (*"Our voluntary advisor turnover rate has ranged between 11% and 13% over the last three years"*).
- **Will the fundamentals look broadly the same in ten years?** The need for an independent party at the table when a large buyer contracts for technology services runs through every 10-K from fiscal 2007 to 2025; what is being bought has changed (outsourcing, then cloud and automation, now AI), and the company follows it (the automation unit bought in 2016 and sold in 2024; *"Enterprise AI consulting and research ... now represents about 30 percent of our firmwide revenue, up from 10 percent last year"*). The mechanism, adviser hours and research sold to buyers and sellers of technology services, is the same in 2007 and 2025. Whether the change of subject matter makes the advantage one that must be rebuilt is a Q2 question.
- **Relatively simple and stable in character [E3-31]?** Yes: a professional-services firm that bills people's time, plus subscriptions to research and a supplier-management tool.
