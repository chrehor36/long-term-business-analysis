## Q2 — IS IT A FRANCHISE? **[E3-03]**

*Every J & J figure below is read from its filed 10-Ks FY2003-FY2025 and the 10-Q for the quarter to 2026-06-27 (accessions in Step 0), or from SEC companyfacts checked against those statements. Scripts and outputs, all in `Test Runs/_research 2026-09-27 JJSF/`: `xb.py` (`xb_out.txt`), `q2_series.py` (`q2_series.txt`), `seg.py` (`seg_raw.txt`), `units.py` (`units_out.txt`, the ICEE gallon and segment-return series), `peers/row.py` (`peers/row_all.txt`, `peers/row_summary.txt`).*

**The question this file has to answer: does J & J set its prices, or accept them?** It owns well-known names (SUPERPRETZEL, ICEE in its three countries, Dippin' Dots), is the largest maker of soft pretzels in the country, and owns the ovens, warmers and dispensers its customers sell from. **The two hypotheses, stated so they can be attacked [E4-26].** *(a) A franchise of placement and route: the equipment in the outlet and the ICEE service network are what a customer cannot easily replace, the brands carry the consumer, and a quarter-century of unbroken profit shows it.* *(b) A good food manufacturer selling mostly to professional buyers who choose on price: bakery and handhelds are largely private label and contract packing priced off ingredient cost, pretzels and churros are bought by chains and clubs that switch, and the ICEE route is a scale and density advantage, the low-cost operator [E3-43] calls "a business".* Each is tested on the filings below.

**Who the customers are and how they buy, in the filer's words.** *"Our top ten customers accounted for 46%, 45% and 43% of our sales during fiscal years 2025, 2024, and 2023, respectively, with our largest customer accounting for 10% of our sales in fiscal 2025 ... Five of the ten customers in 2025 are food distributors who sell our product to many end users. ... These customers typically do not enter long-term contracts and make purchase decisions based on a combination of price, product quality, consumer demand and customer service performance."* (10-K FY2025, Items 1 and 1A). Competition: *"Snack food and bakery products markets are highly competitive. ... Competitive factors in these markets include product quality, innovation, customer service, taste, price, identity and brand name awareness, method of distribution and sales promotions."* *"there are numerous regional and local manufacturers of food service and retail supermarket soft pretzels as well as several chains of retail pretzel stores."* In frozen beverages, *"There are many other regional frozen beverage competitors throughout the country and one large retail chain which uses its own frozen beverage brand"*, and J & J *"competes with large soft drink manufacturers for counter and floor space for its frozen beverage dispensing machines at retail locations and with products which are more widely known than the ICEE, SLUSH PUPPIE and PARROT ICE frozen beverages."* Risk factor: *"We compete against national and regional manufacturers and distributors on the basis of price, quality, product variety, brand recognition and loyalty, and effective distribution. Many of our major competitors in the market are larger and have greater financial and marketing resources than we do."*

**What price does when costs move: the record in the filer's own sentences.**
- **FY2025, bakery (27% of company sales, the largest product line).** Sales rose *"with the increase attributable to contractual pricing true-up on costing on certain raw material ingredients, as well as some targeted, non-contractual price increases taken to offset the rising costs on certain raw material ingredients, offset somewhat by volume declines, most notably in our first fiscal quarter related to our pie portfolio and the loss of some seasonal business with a declining margin profile that we bid on, but did not retain."* **Price here is set by a cost formula in the contract, and the business is bid for.**
- **FY2025, company-wide.** *"These rising costs pressured margins, primarily during the first half of our fiscal year, as they outweighed pricing actions up to that point in time"*; gross margin fell from 30.9% to 29.7%; Food Service operating income fell 13% *"primarily driven by the impact of gross margin pressures due to rising raw material costs and other inflationary pressures which outweighed the benefits from price increases in the first half of our fiscal year"*; churros to food service fell 14% on *"the lapping of the benefit of limited time offer churro volumes with a major customer in the prior year period which did not recur in the current year period"*.
- **FY2019, retail supermarkets, a year of flat demand.** *"Sales of frozen juices and ices decreased $684,000 or 1% to $73,751,000 as we lost some volume and placements due to price increases."* *"Price increases provided about $4 million of sales for the year and net volume decreased about $5.5 million for the year."* The FY2020 comparison, as the FY2021 10-K repeats it: *"sales in the year ago periods were impacted by lost volume and placements due to the price increases implemented in last year’s first quarter"* (the FY2020 10-K itself reads *"due the price increases"*, recorded as filed). The FY2019 sentences are quoted from the FY2019 comparison in the 10-K for FY2020. **A price rise under slack demand cost more volume than it gained in price.**
- **FY2011-FY2012.** Food Service operating income fell *"from $50,220,000 in 2010 to $46,171,000 in 2011 primarily as a result of higher ingredients and packaging costs of about $16 million and increased freight and distribution costs ... which were partially offset by the benefit of higher pricing"*; in 2012 it rose on *"increased sales volume and price increases which offset higher ingredient and packaging cost increases of about $9 million"*.
- **FY2022.** *"Pricing actions that were implemented during fiscal 2022 helped to offset some of these significant cost pressures"*; gross margin 26.8%, operating margin 4.5%, Food Service segment operating income $18.5M on $872.7M of segment sales (2.1%).
- **The risk factor the filer has carried since at least FY2008:** *"If commodity price changes result in increases in raw materials and energy costs, we may not be able to increase our prices to offset these increased costs without suffering reduced volume, revenue and operating income"* (10-K FY2008; the FY2025 wording drops "volume"); and *"The increased buying power of large supermarket chains, other retail outlets and wholesale food vendors could result in greater resistance to price increases."*

**[E3-03], criterion by criterion.** *"An economic franchise arises from a product or service that: (1) is needed or desired; (2) is thought by its customers to have no close substitute and; (3) is not subject to price regulation."*
- **(1) Needed or desired [x]: passes.** Snacks at the point of sale are desired; 132,000 dispensers and *"a large majority of supermarkets in the United States"* carry the products.
- **(3) Not subject to price regulation [x]: passes.** Food safety and labelling are regulated; price is not (the USDA school programme makes some pretzels and juice cups eligible for reimbursement; it does not set their price).
- **(2) Thought by its customers to have no close substitute: not met.** The customers are professional buyers (chains, clubs, distributors, schools, supermarkets; ten of them 46% of sales) who *"make purchase decisions based on a combination of price, product quality, consumer demand and customer service performance"* and *"typically do not enter long-term contracts"*. The largest product line, bakery, is priced by a *"contractual pricing true-up on costing"* and loses business *"that we bid on, but did not retain"*. Churro volume with *"a major customer"* came as a limited-time offer and left. In the one retail year the filer quantified, a price rise lost *"volume and placements"*. The consumer brands exist; the buyer who stocks them treats J & J's products as substitutable at a price.

**[E3-43]'s demonstration, *"a company’s ability to regularly price its product or service aggressively and thereby to earn high rates of return on capital"*, and its other half: *"In contrast, “a business” earns exceptional profits only if it is the low-cost operator or if supply of its product or service is tight."***
- **The return half: not met at the level the test means, and falling.** After-tax return on equity 8.5-14.4% across twenty-five years ([E2-01] reads it as the primary test; [E3-46] asks for *"very high returns on capital employed over time"*); pre-tax on operating capital 23-31% for a decade and 10-13% since FY2021; the ICEE segment 11-15% on its assets. The IPAR file of this morning read 27-31% on capital as good, not exceptional; this is lower and has fallen by half.
- **The pricing half: absent.** In every inflationary year the filer discusses (FY2011-FY2012, FY2021-FY2022, FY2025) price follows cost and lags it, and the bakery contracts are priced by formula off ingredient cost.
- **[E2-44], the two characteristics.** The first (*"an ability to increase prices rather easily (even when product demand is flat and capacity is not fully utilized) without fear of significant loss of either market share or unit volume"*): **failed on the record** in FY2019 retail, the flat-demand case the test names. The second (*"an ability to accommodate large dollar volume increases in business ... with only minor additional investment of capital"*): **not met**: about 67 cents of operating capital, 41 cents tangible, for each added dollar of sales since FY2010, and plant spending above depreciation in every year FY2017-FY2025.
- **[E3-43]'s other door, the low-cost operator.** The largest soft-pretzel maker and the only national frozen-beverage route may well be the low-cost operator in their niches, and that is **"a business"** by the passage's own words, which *"can be killed by poor management"*: the FY2020-FY2022 margins (1.7%, 6.2%, 4.5%) and the three plant closures of Project Apollo in 2025 (*"we recorded plant closure costs of approximately $24 million in the fourth quarter of fiscal 2025"*) show a result that moves with execution and input costs.

### THE STRONGEST EVIDENCE AGAINST THIS VERDICT, stated as its holders would state it [E4-51, E4-26, E3-47]
The verdict below is OUT, so the case for IN is set out first, at full strength:
1. **Placement and route are a moat the returns understate.** J & J owns the oven, warmer or dispenser at the counter and services 132,000 dispensers from 172 depots; no rival has that national route, and a customer who takes the equipment takes the product. ICEE is a national consumer brand, and SUPERPRETZEL and Dippin' Dots lead their niches.
2. **The record.** Profitable in every year FY2000-FY2025, including the pandemic year (net income $18.3M on sales down 14%), essentially debt-free, dividend raised through it: a business that stands shocks and some mismanagement [E5-18].
3. **Pricing held at ICEE.** Revenue per gallon rose about a third between FY2019 and FY2024 and was not given back when gallons fell in FY2024-FY2025.
4. **The returns before the pandemic were high.** 29-31% pre-tax on operating capital FY2010-FY2016, 45-56% on tangible operating capital; the post-2020 dip is input-cost inflation and pandemic volume, now being repaired by Project Apollo (*"at least $20 million of run-rate operating income"*).
5. **Dippin' Dots is a unique product** (cryogenically beaded ice cream) with a brand no rival can copy.

**Answered.** (1) is accepted as the best asset in the file and weighed at the segment where it lives: the ICEE segment earns 11-15% pre-tax on its own assets in every normal year FY2009-FY2025, which is what a well-run route earns, [E3-43]'s low-cost operator; and the placement programme has not stopped buyers moving on price (the FY2019 retail placements, the bid bakery business, the churro offer that did not recur). (2) is accepted and belongs at Q4: persistence of modest profit is not the high return on capital [E3-43] and [E3-46] demand, and [E5-18] describes a business that tolerates mismanagement because its economics are strong, which the 1.7-6.2% operating margins of FY2020-FY2022 do not show. (3) is weighed against its timing: flat for seven years FY2012-FY2019, then risen in the inflation years while the segment's return on assets stayed at 13.6-15.3%; price followed cost. (4) is weighed with its base: the high pre-pandemic figures exclude $117-343M of cash and securities, after-tax return on equity never passed 14.4%, and the decline since FY2021 began before Project Apollo, whose savings are a management target, not a filed result ([E3-48], a prompt beneath the close). (5) is accepted as a real product advantage inside one line (frozen novelties are 16% of sales, of which Dippin' Dots is part), bought for $222M in 2022, and not large enough to carry the company's class. **[E3-47]** (a wrongly closed file inside the circle is the costliest error) was weighed, and is why the reversal conditions below are written in terms of segment returns and price conduct, so the file reopens on evidence rather than on price.
