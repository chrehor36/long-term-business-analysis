# I. THE FOUR VERDICTS

> "There's just — there's just games that are too tough. Charlie says, you know, **'We've
> got three boxes at the company: in, out, and too hard.'** (Laughter) And a lot of things
> end up in the 'too hard' pile, and it doesn't bother us." — **[E4-19]**, 2006 meeting
> *(the line is Munger's, quoted by Buffett)*

| Verdict | Meaning | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **IN** | The evidence is here and it clears. | Continue. |
| **OUT** | The evidence is here and the business fails. | **Stop.** Permanent. |
| **UNRESEARCHED** | The evidence exists and I have not got it. | **A work order.** Name the artifact and where it lives. Never an answer. |
| **UNKNOWABLE** | The evidence is in and the future is still indeterminate. | **Close, without prejudice.** No finding about the business. |

**The separating test, asked aloud on every non-IN verdict: "Can I name the document that
would resolve this?"** Yes → UNRESEARCHED, go and get it. No → UNKNOWABLE, close it.
*(CONVENTION: splitting "too hard" in two is ours. The corpus has one box. We split it
because a 2026 analyst can retrieve in minutes what took Buffett a letter to the company,
and calling an unperformed task "too hard" dignifies laziness as judgment.)*

**And UNRESEARCHED is for documents, never for competence [E4-46]:** *"if we can't make a
decision in five minutes, **we can't make it in five months**. We're not going to learn
enough in the following five months to make up for the fact that we went in deficient in
the first place."* A named filing inside an understood business is a work order; a business
that would take months of study is outside the circle, and the verdict is Q1 OUT — no
fetch repairs it.

**A thin-evidence gate is never IN.** A gate marked IN carrying "unverified", "general
knowledge" or "provisional" is UNRESEARCHED. **CONVENTION** *(labelled 2026-09-20; the rule
had its rationale and not its label)*: this rule exists because runs that did otherwise put
unverified names into a real portfolio — operational history, not a corpus passage. The
nearest corpus is the four-verdict split itself, which is a confessed convention in section VI.

> "there's **no degree of difficulty factor**… we get paid, not for jumping over 7-foot
> bars, but for **stepping over 1-foot bars**… I'd rather have the universe be a little
> smaller than it really is, than being interpreted as larger than it is." — **[E4-18]**, 2005

**A conclusion that required fighting for it is worth less, not more.** Gathering more
evidence is legitimate. Narrowing assumptions until the answer appears is not.

**And the closed file has a price [E3-47]:** *"Typically, our most egregious mistakes fall
in the omission, rather than the commission, category… their invisibility does not reduce
their cost."* A wrongly-closed file inside the circle of competence is the error class the
corpus rates most expensive. UNKNOWABLE remains a verdict; writing one on a name you
understood is the one place to hesitate.

# II. BEFORE ANY QUESTION — READ THE FILING

> "**There are no answers in the financial statements.** There are guidelines to enable you
> to figure out the answer. And to figure out that answer, **you have to understand
> something about business.** You don't have to understand a lot about mathematics. I mean,
> **the math is not complicated.**" — **[E3-27]**, 1994 meeting

Tagged data (XBRL) is transcription and screening. It may generate candidates. It may not
produce a verdict. Before Question 5, the run records that the **MD&A, the cash-flow
statement including its detail lines, and the footnotes** were read, with the document date
and accession number. Any figure that drives a verdict is checked against the filed
statement.

**Evidence ladder** *(CONVENTION — a procedure, not a threshold)*: SEC XBRL → SEC EDGAR
primary documents → company IR site (English) → exchange filings (TDnet, RNS, SEDAR+) →
EDINET *(blocked: paid key)* → competitor filings. Where a rung is blocked, name the rung
and the obstacle.

# III. THE SIX QUESTIONS

## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND HOW THIS MAKES MONEY?

> "we try to stick to businesses we believe we understand. That means they must be
> **relatively simple and stable in character.** If a business is complex or subject to
> constant change, we're not smart enough to predict future cash flows. […] What counts for
> most people in investing is not how much they know, but rather **how realistically they
> define what they don't know.**" — **[E3-31]**, 1992 letter

Write the unit economics in your own words, without management's language. Name **the
scarce input the business controls** *(CONVENTION, labelled 2026-10-04: an analyst's instruction with no ledger
row; the nearest corpus is the unit-economics reading above [E3-31] and the Q2 tests of what competitors cannot
take. Found unlabelled by the v5 reconciliation, part 1.)*. State whether the fundamentals will look broadly the
same in ten years.

> "Our satisfactory results have been the product of **about a dozen truly good decisions —
> that would be about one every five years**" — **[E5-13]**, 2022 letter

Twelve decisions in fifty-eight years is the corpus's own answer to why a small universe is
correct. Most names should end here, and that is the system working.

**VERDICT: IN / OUT / UNRESEARCHED / UNKNOWABLE**

## Q2 — IS IT A FRANCHISE?

> a franchise is a product or service that "(1) is needed or desired; (2) is thought by its
> customers to have **no close substitute** and; (3) is not subject to price regulation."
> — **[E3-03]**, 1991 letter

> "A moat that must be **continuously rebuilt** will eventually be no moat at all.
> Additionally, this criterion eliminates the business whose success depends on having a
> great manager." — **[E4-04]**, 2007 letter

**Scope of [E4-04], set by the corpus itself (batch 2):** what "enduring" excludes is the
moat whose **basis must be periodically replaced** — rapid-change industries, depleting
assets. It does not exclude moats that need continuous *defence*: *"certainly should be
working at improving your own moat and defending your own moat **all of the time**"*
**[E5-23]** is prescribed for every moat, and the canonical franchise was designed with
maintenance as *"a permanent obsession"* **[E3-49]**. The test: **does a lapse in spending
destroy the structure, or merely narrow it — and does the spending defend the same
advantage, or buy its replacement?** (Mitsui's Rhodes Ridge buys a replacement deposit;
Coca-Cola's advertising defends the same trademark.) The excluded class's mechanism has a
name — Munger's **competitive destruction** — and the long runs it does permit are
**surfing runs**: *"when a surfer gets up and catches the wave and just stays there, he can
go a long, long time. But if he gets off the wave, he becomes mired in shallows"*
**[E3-51]**. A surfing run is not a moat; the advantage lives in the wave, not the surfer.
Ask which of the **four causes of extreme success [E4-36]** the record actually comes from
— extreme max/min of one or two variables; a nonlinear combination; extreme performance
over many factors; or wave-riding — because only some of them are ownable.

**The verdict form [E4-04] takes — ruled 2026-09-20 from the corpus, after the TSM run applied it as
OUT on the business.** The rule's own reason is epistemic — creative destruction *"precludes investment
certainty"* — and the 1999 letter says it plainly: *"we have no insights into which participants in the
tech field possess a truly durable competitive advantage"* **[E4-57]**; predicting those economics *"is
simply far beyond our perimeter"* **[E4-58]**; the 1987 acquisition criteria put the reason inside the list
itself, *"if there's lots of technology, we won't understand it"* **[E2-75]**; and Munger names it: *"we
tend to avoid that stuff, based on our personal inadequacies"* **[E3-72]**, adding that staying out
*"doesn't mean that it's irrational for you to do it"* **[E3-73]**. The purchase record qualifies the rule
on the record — BYD, *"we have always bragged about avoiding that [...] And yet here we are"* **[E5-51]**;
Apple, bought for *"the value of an ecosystem and how permanent that ecosystem could be"* and not *"because
it was a tech stock in the least"* **[E5-52]**, against the 1991 letter's *"We will never develop the
competence to spot such businesses early"* **[E3-75]**; and the only TSMC passage on the shelf calls it
*"one of the best managed companies and important companies in the world"* with *"nobody in the chip
industry that's in their league"*, sold because *"I don't like its location"* **[E5-53]**. **So [E4-04]
is applied as a competence limit, never as a fourth franchise criterion.** A name that passes [E3-03] and
whose durability cannot be judged from filings closes **UNKNOWABLE at Q2**, without prejudice — the
perimeter close — never *OUT on the business*. A name whose advantage the filings show must be **rebuilt
from zero** each generation fails [E4-04] on the business; a lead **maintained** through the generations
does not, which is the same distinction the scope paragraph above draws between replacing a moat's basis
and defending it. The unit of the test is the company, not the industry **[E4-08]**; the have-to-stay-smart
class is, in Buffett's own example, retailing **[E3-74]**.

**The commodity end has its own doctrine (batch 5).** The equation **[E2-58]**: *"persistent
over-capacity without administered prices (or costs) equals poor profitability"*, long-term
profitability set by *"the ratio of supply-tight to supply-ample years"*, and prosperity
breeding the next glut (*"nothing fails like success"*). The one exception is *"a cost
advantage that is both **wide and sustainable** … By definition such exceptions are few."*
Capex in this class faces **the second step [E3-62]**: vendor projections show the savings;
none ever asks *"how much is going to stay home and how much is just going to flow through
to the customer"* — in a commodity business the gains flow to buyers (the textile-loom
lesson: *"Nothing was going to stick to our ribs as owners"*), in the one-paper town they
stick.
And the rescue is not a franchise **[E2-59]**: administered pricing (regulation, cartel)
can floor a commodity business's profits — pre-1970s insurers *"could legally price their
way to profitability even in the face of substantial over-capacity"* — but the moat belongs
to the **regime**, and *"That day is gone"* is how it ends. Regulation *caps* a franchise
([E3-03] criterion 3) and *floors* a commodity business; neither creates the class.

**A bank is on the business side, and the low-cost door is its only door — ruled 2026-09-20 from the
corpus, after five bank runs reached it by four routes.** The product is a commodity in Buffett's own
words: banks are *"dealing in what is basically a commodity — money"*, and their high returns *"would be
self-neutralizing"* **[E4-59]**; the 1982 letter names *"deposit costs for financial institutions"* among
administered costs, inside the commodity frame [E2-58] itself comes from **[E2-59]**. The door has two
filed forms: the cost of funding — *"very little risk on the asset side and very cheap money on the deposit
side"* **[E4-60]** — and the cost of operations — the Bank of Granite's *"2.58 percent on assets [...] and
[...] a 33 percent efficiency ratio"*, *"There's no magic to it. You just have to stay away from doing
something foolish"* **[E3-76]**. Both are management-made, which is why the advantage is perishable and why
Q3 decides a bank's file: *"leverage of 20:1 magnifies the effects of managerial strengths and weaknesses"*
**[E3-29]**, *"you should know something about the culture of the management [...] and that's hard to do
for 99 percent of the banks"* **[E4-63]**, the economics rest on cheap money, *"the implicit federal
guarantee"* and leverage **[E5-54]**, and *"You can change the math of banking, and the attractiveness of
banking, totally, by capital requirements"* **[E5-55]**. **So a bank's Q2 IN is NARROW by construction, and
a bank with neither form of the door is OUT.** The text refuses the opposite reading too — *"I would not
characterize all banks as the same"* **[E4-61]**; *"Warren and I have failed to properly diagnose banking"*
**[E4-62]** — and treats a deposit base as a non-wasting asset: *"Yet core deposits regularly increase"*
**[E5-56]**. **[E3-43] supplies the test a bank is measured against; it is said of newspapers, television
and magazines, and does not classify banks.**

**The competitor row is required.**

> "**I can't be an intelligent owner of a business unless I know what all the other
> businesses in that industry are doing.**" — **[E3-28]**, 1996 meeting

A moat is a claim about *relative* position and cannot be evidenced from one company's
numbers. Name the closest competitors and give the **same metric, same window,
filing-sourced**. Buffett says eight; take as many as the industry actually has and say how
many you took. Where a competitor's data is unavailable, say so and hold the moat class
**PROVISIONAL**. **And state the row's limit [E3-61]:** identical structures produce
opposite outcomes — *"In some businesses, the participants behave like a demented Kellogg.
In other businesses, they don't … **I think you'd have to know the people involved.**"*
The row shows position; it cannot show conduct, and the corpus says even Munger had no
model for predicting it.

**And look for the moat that has not yet shown up in the numbers:**

> "There are actually businesses that you will find a few times in a lifetime where any
> manager could raise the return enormously just by raising prices, and yet they haven't
> done it. So they have **huge untapped pricing power** that they're not using. That is the
> ultimate no-brainer." — **[E3-33]**, Munger, 1994

A screen on realised returns alone misses this class entirely. **Scope (batch 3):**
*"If you name some business that has incredible pricing power, you're talking about a
business that's **a monopoly or a near monopoly**"* **[E5-28]** — claiming the class for a
name is claiming near-monopoly, and the competitor row must then support it. **And the
inverse metric [E4-37]:** *"you can almost measure the strength of a business over time by
**the agony they go through in determining whether a price increase can be sustained**"* —
*"it's not a great business when you have to have a prayer session before you raise your
prices a penny."* The same answer runs newspapers from yawn-pricing to agony-pricing:
the metric detects a moat downgrade in real time, which is its Q6 monitoring use.

**More Q2 tests, added 2026-08-28 (register batches 1 and 3):**
- **The dominance class [E2-53]** — the strongest reading of franchise: *"Once dominant,
  the newspaper itself, not the marketplace, determines just how good or how bad the paper
  will be. **Good or bad, it will prosper.**"* Position, not execution, sets the economics
  — the ancestor of [E5-18]'s stand-a-little-mismanagement test. (Quality may be crucial
  to *achieving* dominance; afterwards the position carries it.)
- **The second question about the business is a number**: *"the best businesses, by
  definition, are going to be businesses that earn very high returns on capital employed
  over time"* — asked about the business, before the manager **[E3-46]**.
- **The two-characteristic test [E2-44]**: can it raise prices *"even when product demand
  is flat and capacity is not fully utilized"*, and grow dollar volume *"with only minor
  additional investment of capital"*?
- **The attacker's test [E2-45]**: *"how I would like, assuming I had ample capital and
  skilled personnel, to compete with it"* — the shelf's one forward-looking moat test.
- **Direction outranks existence [E4-32]**: the moat *widened every year* is *"the primary
  criterion of a great business"* — *"that does not necessarily mean that the profit is
  more this year than last year."* This is also Q6's monitoring metric. **The widener is
  the manager**: *"a great knight in charge of that castle… He's the one that throws the
  crocodiles into the moat and widens the moat over time"* **[E4-33]** — the knight sets
  the moat's *direction*, never its *existence*; asked about after the castle, at Q3.
  **Where units exist, monitor units [E4-55]:** Precision Steel's pounds fell 69M → 46M
  while price rises held dollar revenue level — *"a serious reverse, not likely to
  disappear in some 'bounce back' effect."* Dollar revenue flattered by pricing is how a
  shrinking franchise hides; the physical series is the honest one.

**Growth is not automatically good** — see Q4's great/good/gruesome test, which is a
question about the franchise as much as about survival.

**VERDICT: IN / OUT / UNRESEARCHED / UNKNOWABLE**

## Q3 — ARE THEY HONEST, AND ARE THEY RATIONAL?

**Full standard: `Framework/v4/THE MANAGER STANDARD - Q3.md`** — the whole citation shelf
read for this question. What follows is binding; the annex carries the evidence and the
worked calibration cases.

### First, declare how much Q3 weighs. The business sets it.

Three determinants, each independently sourced, all measuring one thing: **how much damage
can this manager do before you can react?**

| raises Q3 to a **gate** | source |
|---|---|
| **Daily execution** — have-to-be-smart-**every-day**, not have-to-be-smart-once | **[E3-38]**, its 1991 original **[E3-43]**, and the 1977 root **[E2-70]**: an undifferentiated product (*"their only products are promises"*) **magnifies** the manager |
| **Control** — you own the whole thing and cannot exit | **[E1-16]** |
| **Leverage** — small asset errors destroy equity | **[E3-29]** |

*Test D traced this rule to its original form, 1991: "franchises can tolerate mis-management…
**a business, unlike a franchise, can be killed by poor management**" **[E3-43]**. Franchise →
overlay; "a business" → gate. [E5-18] (2009) is the same doctrine restated; [E3-38] (1995) is
its worked example.*

> "**Because leverage of 20:1 magnifies the effects of managerial strengths and weaknesses,
> we have no interest in purchasing shares of a poorly-managed bank at a 'cheap' price.**
> Instead, our only interest is in buying into well-managed banks at fair prices."
> — **[E3-29]**, 1990 letter

**All three low** → Q3 is a qualitative overlay; a mediocre manager is survivable and price
does the work. **Any one high** → Q3 is a binary gate and **no price compensates** — the only
place in the framework where cheapness is ruled out as a remedy, stated twice
**[E1-16, E3-29]** and as a law in the Kraft Heinz post-mortem **[E5-35]**: *"You can turn
any investment into a bad deal by paying too much. **What you can't do is turn any
investment into a good deal by paying little.**"* **A run that does not declare its weight
case has not done Q3.**

### The binary

> "Disappointments are inevitable. **We are understanding about business mistakes; our
> tolerance for personal misconduct is zero.**" — **[E5-16]**, 2022 letter

Competence errors are priced; integrity failures are refused. Not two ends of one scale.
Munger runs both as agency costs **[E3-57]** — self-dealing and folly are *"either way,
very costly to you"* — but the treatments differ: dishonesty is refused outright, while
folly is priced through the business's capacity to stand it (*"some businesses so good that
they'll easily stand a lot of folly in the managerial suite"*), and even the
perfect-people preference was a preference: *"I don't think we've always invested with
them."*

> "**We've never succeeded in making a good deal with a bad person.**" — **[E2-31]**, 1989

The veto runs **one way**. Note the ordering in the same passage: bad people void good
economics; good people do not rescue bad economics.

### The primary test — and it is a number

> "**The primary test of managerial economic performance is the achievement of a high
> earnings rate on equity capital employed** (without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry,
> etc.) **and not the achievement of consistent gains in earnings per share.**"
> — **[E2-01]**, 1979 letter

Run it as a multi-year series, balance sheet before income statement. It is defined *in
opposition to the metric managements game*, which is why it pairs with the flags below.
**The whole of Q3 fits in two yardsticks [E3-59]:** *"One is how well they run the business"*
— judged against *"the hand they were dealt"*, read against competitors' reports — *"And
then the second thing … is how well that they treat their owners."* (The same answer notes
the correlation: the poor operators are usually also the ones who *"don't think that much
about the shareholders. The two often go hand in hand."*) And the numbers are not the whole
read **[E3-56]**: Wells Fargo — *"if we hadn't gone a little further … than just looking at
numbers, we would not have been able to make that decision."*
**Scoped as its sources scope it (batch 1):** the 1977 original carves out unusual
debt-equity ratios and mis-stated asset values **[E2-47]**, and for acquisitive or leveraged
filers the denominator is **unleveraged net tangible assets** — *"the best guide to the
economic attractiveness of the operation"* **[E2-43]** — with the goodwill wedge reported
separately, never hidden in book equity. **A third denominator for judging the operators
themselves [E2-73]:** *"the managers of the units should be judged by the returns they
achieve on the underlying assets; **what we pay for a business does not affect the amount
of capital its manager has to work with.**"* Pick the denominator by the question asked —
and adjust for *"tailwinds not of their own making"* and *"the hand they have been dealt."*

### The flags — accounting and disclosure, not litigation. *An open list, not a closed one.*

> "**First, beware of companies displaying weak accounting** … **There is seldom just one
> cockroach in the kitchen.** […] **Second, unintelligible footnotes usually indicate
> untrustworthy management.** […] **Finally, be suspicious of companies that trumpet earnings
> projections and growth expectations.** […] Managers that always promise to 'make the
> numbers' will at some point be tempted to make up the numbers." — **[E4-22]**, 2002 letter

**Fifth — from the same 2002 passage, previously dropped by v4's own ellipsis, and the
single most-repeated disclosure tell in the shelf:**

> "**Trumpeting EBITDA … is a particularly pernicious practice.** Doing so implies that
> depreciation is not truly an expense, given that it is a 'non-cash' charge. **That's
> nonsense.**" — **[E4-29]**; twelve-plus restatements 2000–2024, ending at *"a banned
> measurement"*

The mechanism, in one inversion **[E5-41]**: *"float is where we get the money first and we
have the expense later. Depreciation is where you spend the money first … and record the
expense later. And it's **reverse float**."* The worst kind of expense — already paid — is
exactly the one EBITDA deletes, *"in the interests of Wall Street, enormously … higher
borrowing power, higher valuations."*

Plus: **serial share issuance**, *"one of the surest indicators of a promotion-minded
management, weak accounting, a stock that is overpriced and — all too often — outright
dishonesty"* **[E5-15]**; and the **filed-figure fraud tells** from the corpus's one fully
worked fraud case — reported growth engineered to be unnaturally smooth, and **cash taxes
falling as a share of reported pretax income** (*"This plainly increased their chances of
attracting undesired questions"* — **[E4-30]**), both computable from the multi-year series
the run already builds.

**The action on the projections flag [E3-48]:** pull the company's own past guidance and
set it against outturn — Buffett's stated base rate is that *"about nine cases out of ten"*
of projections exist to justify a decided course, and his stated remedy is to demand *"the
record of the people who made the projections."* **And on any conduct matter: penalty size
is not seriousness, in either direction [E5-22]** — the Wells Fargo error was reading a
$185M fine as small; the failure that counts is *"they didn't act when they learned."*

**Sixth — metric-switching (batch 2):** *"Yardsticks seldom are discarded while yielding
favorable readings. But when results deteriorate, most managers favor **disposition of the
yardstick rather than disposition of the manager**"* — demand *"pre-set, long-lived and
small bullseyes"* **[E2-49]**. Operational form: compare the headline metric across
successive filings; a switch that *follows* deterioration fires; one announced ahead with
reasons (as Berkshire's own 1982 switch was) is the candor case.

**The auditor's-eye test [E4-34]:** the four questions whose answers should be *"recorded
and reported"* — would the auditor have prepared the statements differently? would he, as
an investor, have received the essential information in plain English? is internal audit
run as if the auditor were CEO? and is he aware of any action with *"the purpose and
effect of moving revenues or expenses from one reporting period to another"*? The fourth
is the period-shifting test the fraud tells **[E4-30]** lack. **And scope the prior by the
business [E2-50]:** *"Where 'earnings' can be created by the stroke of a pen, the
dishonest will gather"* — in reserve- and estimate-driven sectors the conduct record and
the reserve-development table carry the Q3 weight.

**Flags that converge are a different event [E4-52]:** the lollapalooza — *"extreme
consequences from **confluences** of psychological tendencies acting in favor of a
particular outcome … it dominates life."* Several flags firing toward one outcome (weak
accounting + trumpeted projections + serial issuance + price-targeting) are not a sum of
prompts; they are one reinforcing system, and the read treats them as such.

**Seventh — dividends funded by issuance [E2-52]:** *"Other companies sell newly issued
shares to Peter in order to pay dividends to Paul. **Beware of 'dividends' that can be paid
out only if someone promises to replace the capital distributed.**"* Set the cash dividend
against net issuance over the same window; a payout that only exists because the capital
is replaced fires. **Eighth — stock-price targeting [E3-50]:** managers whose premise is
*"that their job at all times is to encourage **the highest stock price possible** (a
premise with which we adamantly disagree)"* — the passage names the next step,
*"unadmirable accounting stratagems."* The positive doctrine is 1988's: a price *"in a
narrow range centered at intrinsic business value"*, with overvaluation as unwelcome as
undervaluation.

**Ninth — the except-for flag [E2-57]:** *"'except for' should be excised from the lexicon.
If you are going to play the game, **you must count the runs scored against you in all nine
innings.** Any manager who consistently says 'except for' … may be missing the only
important lesson — namely, that **the real mistake is not the act, but the actor.**"*
**Tenth — the restructuring charge [E3-53]:** *"a large chunk of costs that should properly
be attributed to a number of years is **dumped into a single quarter** … In some cases, the
purpose of the charge is to clean up earnings misrepresentations of the past, and in others
it is to prepare the ground for future misrepresentations."* The acquisition variant boosts
loss reserves at deal time so "earnings" flow into income later; the charges are real costs
and belong in the owner-earnings mean **[E5-33]** — *"to tell owners year after year, 'Don't
count this' … is misleading"*; even a disclosed one-time rebasing is *explained for years*,
never annualized away, and Berkshire's own restructurings were *"borne by shareholders"*
every time.

**Why the projections flag matters so much — the behavior is irreversible [E5-30]:**
*"once you start it, it's all over. You can't quit. It's like taking $5 out of the cash
register … **do it once and you probably never stop** … And forecasting earnings, I can't
imagine anything more destructive."* A guidance culture is not this year's fact; it is a
ratchet.

Each flag is **a prompt to read, never a verdict** — for two corpus-stated reasons. Screens
and ratios do not decide **[E5-36]**: *"We don't know how to buy stocks just by looking at
financial figures … anything a computer could be functioned to do, in terms of screening —
**I know I never do it.**"* And a fired flag is not a venality finding **[E5-38]**: people
Buffett would trust with his wallet *"would play games with any number that came to them"* —
the flag reads the accounting, the binary [E5-16] judges the person on conduct, and the two
tests stay distinct. Each flag is dated to when it became
**public**, so the test stays point-in-time honest. Litigation and regulatory matters are a
source to read, not the primary checklist. **The list above is open, not closed** — a
closed "the checklist is exactly N" claim was itself one of v4's confirmed errors. **And a
checklist it must remain [E3-68]:** in Munger's pilot system, *"Checklist routines are
always mandatory"* — checklists are for **coverage** (never miss a hazard class), formulas
are for decisions and are refused [E5-43]; the two rules are one doctrine. The same system
imposes the maintenance obligation: *"regular use of the aircraft simulator to prevent
atrophy"* — the worked calibration cases exist to be re-flown, not framed.

### Candor

> "Our guideline is to tell you **the business facts that we would want to know if our
> positions were reversed.** […] We also believe candor benefits us as managers: **the CEO
> who misleads others in public may eventually mislead himself in private.**"
> — **[E2-26]**, 1983 letter

That last clause is why honesty and rationality are one question, not two. **The test:** does
this company's reporting tell you what you would want to know if the positions were reversed
**[E2-26]**? A one-time item quantified separately at every line passes; the same item buried
in an adjusted figure does not. **The positive pole is on record [E2-67]:** Berkshire
published a table of its own reserving errors *"so you can … judge whether we may have some
systemic bias"*, naming the direction (*"always presented a better underwriting picture than
was truly the case"*) — in a reserve-driven filer [E2-50], the presence or absence of that
table IS the candor read. **And authorship is a tell [E2-72]:** *"owners are entitled to
hear directly from the CEO … A once-a-year report of stewardship should not be turned over
to a staff specialist or public relations consultant."* **Judge deviations by direction
[E2-69]:** Berkshire once accepted an auditor's scope disclaimer to report current reality
over stale audited figures — a deviation toward candor is not the weak-accounting flag; the
corpse filing its own death certificate generously is.

**Rationality is capital allocation.** The skill is scarce and it dominates:

> "the heads of many companies are **not skilled in capital allocation.** Their inadequacy
> is not surprising. Most bosses rise to the top because they have excelled in an area such
> as marketing, production, engineering, administration or, sometimes, institutional
> politics." — **[E2-29]**, 1987 letter

**The stakes are tenure-weighted, and delegation is a flag [E3-58]:** a CEO ten years in
the job with a 12% ROE business paying out a third has allocated an enormous share of all
the capital — yet CEOs are neither trained nor selected for it (*"like somebody playing the
piano all their life, and then getting to Carnegie Hall and they hand him a violin"*), and
solving it *"by either having a staff that does it, or by hiring consultants"* is *"a
terrible mistake."* Visibly outsourced allocation — serial banker-led M&A,
consultant-driven programs — fires the prompt.

**Three tests make it readable (batch 4).** The consolidated series lies **[E2-56]**:
*"Their marvelous core businesses … **camouflage repeated failures in capital allocation
elsewhere**"* — the Pro-Am effect; judge retention segment-by-segment, incrementally, never
on the blended return. The corpus's own scored test is **retention [E3-54]**: at least $1 of
market value per $1 retained, five-year rolling — carried with its **published 2009
self-correction** (book-value gain vs the S&P, plus a consistent premium to book), which is
itself the candor standard demonstrated. And the rare-positive tell **[E4-39]**: a candid
acquisition post-mortem is *"almost never witnessed"* — the Washington Post reviewed every
deal three years after; a filer that revisits its own deals against the announcement case
earns weight the way beaten guidance does **[E3-48]**.

**Score the four observable behaviours of the institutional imperative** — this is a
checklist, not a slogan:

> "(1) …an institution will **resist any change in its current direction**; (2) …corporate
> projects or acquisitions will materialize to **soak up available funds**; (3) Any business
> craving of the leader, however foolish, will be **quickly supported by detailed
> rate-of-return and strategic studies** prepared by his troops; and (4) The behavior of
> peer companies… will be **mindlessly imitated.** […] **Institutional dynamics, not venality
> or stupidity**, set businesses on these courses" — **[E2-30]**, 1989 letter

The last clause governs: **this is not a fraud test.**

**Buybacks — the two conditions:**

> "first, a company has ample funds to take care of the operational and liquidity needs of
> its business; second, its stock is selling at a **material discount** to the company's
> intrinsic business value, conservatively calculated." — **[E5-08]**, 2011 letter

**The earliest full statement has a THIRD condition [E4-31]:** *"Shareholders should have
been supplied all the information they need for estimating that value"* — a buyback made
against an under-informed register fails even at a discount. (The 1999 wording is also
*"below its intrinsic value, conservatively-calculated"*; "material discount" is the 2011
restatement.) The conditions sit under **the first law of capital allocation** — *"whether
the money is slated for acquisitions or share repurchases … **what is smart at one price
is dumb at another**"* **[E5-24]** — and the corpus shows what compliance looks like when
real: Berkshire published **both conditions as numbers in advance** (the 110%-of-book
limit; the $20bn liquidity floor — *"financial strength that is unquestionable takes
precedence over all else"*) **[E5-25]**. At a true discount the rational course is
aggressive **[E4-50]**: Munger's wiser board buys *"very aggressively, using up all cash on
hand **and also borrowing funds**"* — the discount does the licensing, never the borrowing —
while stating the order and the real test
**[E5-31]**: business needs first, then acquisitions vs repurchases by **per-share value
added at the price**; the book multiple was a signaling device pinned to a tracked gap
(*"book value has got nothing to do with the price at which you should purchase your
shares; intrinsic business value does"*). **Stock deals run the same law in shares
[E5-44]:** *"The intrinsic value of the shares you give in an acquisition **must not be
greater than the intrinsic value of the business you receive**"* — measure the acquirer's
paper at IV, not at quote; a premium paid in undervalued shares is larger than it looks. **The refusal is a tell too [E2-51]:** *"A manager
who consistently turns his back on repurchases, when these clearly are in the interests of
owners, **reveals more than he knows of his motivations**."* A failure on the second
condition is a **capital-allocation flag**, and it must be stated with the humility clause:

> "it is natural for CEOs to be optimistic about their own businesses. **They also know a
> whole lot more about them than I do.**" — **[E4-13]** · and "infractions, even serious
> ones, are innocent; many CEOs never stop believing their stock is cheap" — **[E5-08]**

The flag binds **position size**, never the discount rate. *Two halves, two warrants
(2026-09-20):* **"never the discount rate" is the corpus** — *"not try to have the whole
panoply with all different kinds of risk rates … that'd just be playing games with numbers"*
**[E3-42]**, and *"We don't formally have discount rates"* **[E4-21]**. **"Binds position
size" is a CONVENTION**: the corpus gives sizing a direction and several numbers but leaves it
a stated judgment (section VI), so a Q3 flag enters that judgment as a stated reason to size
down — it moves no rate and no value.

### THE GUARDRAIL — Q3 can stop a run. It can never start one.

Six independent statements across five decades and three source classes — **carried with the
hedges their sources carry** (Test D, 2026-08-28): the 1980 original reads *"with **few
exceptions**, when a management with a reputation for brilliance…"* and the 1985 restatement
adds *"(though intelligence and effort help considerably, of course, in any business, good or
bad)"*. The guardrail is a strong tendency the corpus acted on for decades, not a law without
exceptions — the corpus's own exception classes are the excisable-cancer case **[E2-35,
E2-36]** and the rare superstar, whom the corpus **names rather than denies**: Heineman,
Singleton, Zaban, Murphy — four in a generation, and their tell is behavioral: *"these
champs have made very few deals in recent years, and often have found repurchase of their
own shares to be the most sensible employment of corporate capital"* **[E2-48]**; Buffett
disqualifies *himself* from the class in the next sentence. Munger holds both halves in one
passage **[E3-63]**: *averaged out* bet the business [E3-39] — and when a wonderful business
and a wonderful manager align, *"**If you don't load up when you get those opportunities,
it's a big mistake**"*, because the superstars are *"in many cases … not all that hard to
identify."* The sharpest:

> "**a textile company that allocates capital brilliantly within its industry is a remarkable
> textile company — but not a remarkable business.** … a good managerial record (measured by
> economic returns) is **far more a function of what business boat you get into than it is of
> how effectively you row**." — **[E2-37]**, 1985 letter

> "**Good jockeys will do well on good horses, but not on broken-down nags.** Both Berkshire's
> textile business and Hochschild, Kohn had able and honest people running them… **they were
> never going to make any progress while running in quicksand.**" — **[E2-38]**, 1989 letter

Munger states it as a ranking rule: *"averaged out, **betting on the quality of a business is
better than betting on the quality of management**"* **[E3-39]**.

**Therefore a strong Q3 cannot promote a name, repair Q2, or substitute for Q4.**

**But note what the guardrail does not say.** A weak manager does not wreck a great business
*through incompetence* — tolerating one is part of what makes a business great: *"if it won't
stand a little mismanagement it's not much of a business… **We're not looking for
mismanagement. We like the capacity to stand it, if we stumble into it.**"* — **[E5-18]**.
**Two vectors do the damage, and neither is operating skill:**

> "A far more serious problem occurs when the management of a great company **gets sidetracked
> and neglects its wonderful base business while purchasing other businesses that are so-so or
> worse** … **Loss of focus is what most worries Charlie and me when we contemplate investing
> in businesses that in general look outstanding.** All too often, we've seen value stagnate in
> the presence of **hubris or of boredom** that caused the attention of managers to wander."
> — **[E3-40]**, 1996 letter

The second is integrity **[E2-31]**. **And the slow third is the ABCs [E5-45]:**
*"arrogance, bureaucracy and complacency. When these corporate cancers metastasize, **even
the strongest of companies can falter**"* — GM, IBM, Sears and U.S. Steel named, whose
*"one-time financial strength and their historical earning power proved no defense."* Its
tells are the [E2-30] imperative behaviours aging in place, and it is a Q6 monitoring item
as much as a Q3 one. **Test the manager against the base business and the
cash, not against operating polish.** A loss-of-focus finding is an **exit trigger at Q6**,
not an engagement plan — see **[E4-24]**, whose base rate the corpus states **[E4-49]**:
the history of persuading managements to change course is *"poor"* (Munger: *"Worse than
poor"*) — *"you'll probably do better to get out and get in with people … going to do
sensible things with it."* Engagement stays an option; its odds are on the record. The happy side of the same doctrine
**[E2-66]**: SAFECO, *"a much better insurance operation than our own"*, bought passively
under book — *"if one controlled a company run as well as SAFECO, **the proper policy also
would be to sit back and let management do its job**."* Passive participation in excellent
management loses only *"excitement and prestige."* **Conduct across an information
asymmetry is the sharpest read [E2-68]:** the seller who stays on has *"dozens of
opportunities to mislead … through omissions, ambiguities, and misdirection"* — the clean
case *"laid all negative factors face up on the table"*; a management's disclosure conduct
wherever *it* holds the information advantage is the same test. (The NFM zero-verification
purchase **[E2-71]** marks the private-deal branch — $55 million against a word, licensed
only by years of directly observed conduct; it opens nothing for public-market runs, where
the filing stays mandatory [E3-27].)

**"Requires a great manager" is a Q2 finding, not a Q3 finding:**

> "**if a business requires a superstar to produce great results, the business itself cannot
> be deemed great.** … The partnership's moat will go when the surgeon goes. You can count,
> though, on the moat of the Mayo Clinic to endure, **even though you can't name its CEO.**"
> — **[E4-23]**, 2007 letter

Buffett wrote that inside the durability criterion. **Record key-person dependence at Q2 as a
moat defect**, or a warning about the business will be read as a compliment to the person.

**The one exception, with its boundary:** a great manager justifies action only where the
franchise is already intact and the damage is local —

> "extraordinary business franchises with a **localized excisable cancer** (needing, to be
> sure, a skilled surgeon), should be distinguished from the true 'turnaround' situation in
> which the managers expect — and need — to pull off a **corporate Pygmalion.**"
> — **[E2-36]**, 1980 letter, worked against GEICO 1976 at **[E2-35]**

Is the moat intact and the damage excisable, or **is the manager the plan**? Only the first
is buyable.

### The humility clause — this test has a known failure mode

> "Never deal with a rascal under the expectation that you can prevent him from cheating
> you." … **People are not that easy to read. Sincerity and empathy can easily be faked.**
> — **[E5-17]**, 2023 letter

Munger on their own screen missing a reserve fraud: *"If it can happen at Royal Dutch,
believe me, it can happen a lot of other places."* Buffett: *"Charlie and I would not have
spotted it."* And the Sokol calibration **[E5-26]**: *"it's generally a mistake to assume
that rationality is going to be perfect, **even in very able people** … I think hubris
contributes to it"* — a decade of strong record preceded the failure, and a trusted answer
went unverified (*"I obviously made a big mistake by not saying, 'Well, when did you buy
it?'"*). **A Q3 pass is the absence of found disqualifiers, not a finding that the
managers are honest. Write it that way — and verify even trusted answers.**

**And the filed statement itself is not bedrock [E5-32]:** Salomon's books carried a daily
invented number — *"just put in there to **make assets equal liabilities**"* — signed by
the largest audit firm in the country for twelve years (Munger: *"the floating plug"*).
Audited does not mean true. This is why the cross-checks exist (equity recomputed from
A−L, the cash-tax tell, counterparty marks where obtainable), and why every filing-based
test in this framework carries [E5-17]'s cap.

## Q4 — WILL IT SURVIVE?

### Owner earnings — the one number

> "(a) reported earnings plus (b) depreciation, depletion, amortization, and certain other
> non-cash charges … less **(c) the average annual amount of capitalized expenditures for
> plant and equipment, etc. that the business requires to fully maintain its long-term
> competitive position and its unit volume.** (If the business requires additional working
> capital to maintain its competitive position and unit volume, **the increment also should
> be included in (c)**.)" … "**(c) must be a guess** — and one sometimes very difficult to
> make." … "**I would rather be vaguely right than precisely wrong.**" — **[E2-23]**, 1986

Four constraints, all binding:
1. **"the average annual amount"** — a multi-year figure. A single year is not it.
2. **"requires to fully maintain"** — maintenance capex, not total capex.
3. **The working-capital increment is required where the business requires it** — the source's
   own carve-out, restored 2026-08-28: *"businesses following the LIFO inventory method
   usually do not require additional working capital if unit volume does not change"*
   **[E2-23]**, same parenthetical.
4. **"must be a guess"** — (c) is a **disclosed judgment**, never a computed output.
5. **Maintenance has a third dimension [E2-60]:** restricted earnings are those whose payout
   costs the business *"its ability to maintain its unit volume of sales, its long-term
   competitive position, **its financial strength**"* — where leverage rises to fund the
   payout, (c) was understated, and *"a company that consistently distributes restricted
   earnings is destined for oblivion."*

**The corpus supplies a DEFAULT for (c), and an exception class — added 2026-08-28 (Test D);
the prior claim that it gave "the definition, not a computation" was wrong:**

> "by and large, **the depreciation charge is not inappropriate in most companies to use as a
> proxy for required capital expenditures**" — **[E3-44]**, 1998 meeting, answering the direct
> question; *"At **95% of American businesses**, capital expenditures that over time roughly
> approximate depreciation are a necessity"* — **[E2-41]**, 1989 letter

> "in the case of all railroads, **merely spending their depreciation expense will not keep
> them in the same place** … the true maintenance capex … is **higher than 60 percent** of
> [total capex]" — **[E5-20]**, 2016 meeting; airlines and textiles named the same way
> **[E3-44]**

**So: D&A is the default guess for (c). Where the business is capital-intensive — railroads,
airlines, utilities, anything whose own filing says depreciation understates renewal — the
D&A end of any owner-earnings range is INVALID, not merely optimistic, and (c) is judged
upward from total capex.** The band survives only as a display of the guess, never as two
equally legitimate answers. **The default is also inflation-conditioned [E4-47]:** *"inflation
destroys value, but it destroys it very unequally"* — under high inflation, replacement
capex in current dollars outruns depreciation charged in old dollars, so the D&A default
weakens for asset-heavy filers and the exception class widens; the immune class *"retains
its earning power in real dollars without commensurate investment."*

*(CONVENTION, narrowed 2026-08-28: we compute owner earnings as a multi-year mean of
operating cash flow less share-based compensation, less the (c) guess above, because
operating cash flow nets the working-capital change from one audited line — applying
constraint 3's carve-out where it bites. For companies with material equity-method or
unconsolidated minority stakes, add the look-through share of undistributed investee
earnings **[E3-04]** — eleven consecutive letters compute it — or the yield is understated.)*

### THE WINDOW: the corpus's default is FIVE YEARS; the spread is still part of the range

**Test D corrected the prior claim that "the corpus does not name a window." It does:**

> "we recommend **not less than a five-year test** as a rough yardstick of economic
> performance. **Red lights should start flashing if the five-year average annual gain falls
> much below the return on equity earned over the period by American industry in
> aggregate.**" — **[E2-42]**, 1983 letter; "five-year rolling" stated twice more, 1983–84,
> matching Ground Rule 5's *"I much prefer a five-year test"* **[E1-03]**

**Five years is the default window.** Where a distorted year forces a spread across windows,
the range rule still governs:

> "Using precise numbers is, in fact, **foolish; working with a range of possibilities is the
> better approach.** **Usually, the range must be so wide that no useful conclusion can be
> reached.** Occasionally, though, even very conservative estimates … reveal that the price
> quoted is **startlingly low** in relation to value." — **[E4-25]**, 2000 letter

**So run more than one window and carry the spread as part of the range, alongside the capex
band.** The two together are the range **[E4-25]** — not an inconvenience to be resolved before
ranking. **The corpus names the disease and the cure [E4-38]:** *"growth-rate presentations
can be significantly distorted by **a calculated selection of either initial or terminal
dates**"* — and its remedy, worked on Berkshire's own record, is to publish **every** window
(*"a series of calculations is presented so that you can decide for yourself which period is
most meaningful"*, base years spaced five apart, 2003 letter).

**Do not pick a window and justify it.** The corpus asks for a range, not a defence of a point
estimate. *"(c) must be a guess"* and *"I would rather be vaguely right than precisely wrong"*
**[E2-09]**.

**If the combined range is too wide to reach a conclusion, that IS the conclusion** — the
verdict is that no useful conclusion can be reached **[E4-25]**, which closes the file honestly
rather than resolving it by preference.

**Why this rule is here at all.** Two independent analysts ran the same company from the same
filings under the reproducibility test, agreed on every verdict, and produced owner-earnings
figures roughly a quarter apart purely by choosing different windows — silently moving the
ranking. A single reported window conceals a spread the corpus says belongs in the open. Full
record: `Framework/v4/PROOF - tests A, B and C, and what they found.md`.

**A wide spread is also a Q4 finding in its own right**, not just a valuation width: it means a
distorted year sits in the window, which bears directly on whether earnings are reliable
**[E5-11]**. **Scope it honestly [E3-55]:** volatility with a certain endgame is not a defect —
*"If we have a business about which we're extremely confident as to the business result, **we
would prefer that it have high volatility** than low volatility."* The spread measures
distortion and uncertainty about the *level*; where the mechanism is certain and only the
year-to-year figure bounces (See's losing money eight months a year), the bounce is noise,
not width. **And normalize the mean DOWN for luck [E4-41]:** the corpus's only pro-forma that
ever disclosed earnings too *high* is Berkshire's own — a no-megacat year and a bond tailwind
stripped out as *"about $500 million less than we actually reported."* Favourable exogenous
breaks in the window are named and removed before the mean is trusted.

Stock compensation is subtracted in full. The corpus is stricter than any threshold:

> "To say 'stock-based compensation' is not an expense is even more cavalier." — **[E5-06]**

**The measure is market value, not the accounting charge [E3-70]:** subtract *"an amount
equal to what the company could have realized by **publicly selling options of like quantity
and structure**"* — repricing offsets the forfeiture and tax arguments, the revisions ran
5–10% of reported EPS, and they sometimes changed the decision itself. Where SBC is
material, the reported charge is the floor of the subtraction, not the measure.

**Sector method, where the business is float [E2-61, E3-69]:** the insurer's one number is
**cost of float** — underwriting loss against float developed, over years (*"a low cost of
funds signifies a good business; a high cost translates into a poor business"*), read beside
the sovereign exactly as owner earnings are; quarterly and even annual figures are *"too
heavily based on estimates to be much good."* The float-bearing holding company has its own
worked form **[E3-71]**: liquidating value plus the deferred-tax advantage valued as an
interest-free loan — never at face, never at zero — with Munger printing his own
intrinsic-value estimate against his own quote.

### The great, the good and the gruesome

> "The worst sort of business is one that **grows rapidly, requires significant capital to
> engender the growth, and then earns little or no money.** Think airlines. […] Investors
> have poured money into a bottomless pit, **attracted by growth when they should have been
> repelled by it.** […] think of three types of 'savings accounts.' **The great one** pays an
> extraordinarily high interest rate that will rise as the years pass. **The good one** pays
> an attractive rate of interest that will be earned also on deposits that are added.
> Finally, **the gruesome account** both pays an inadequate interest rate and requires you to
> keep adding money at those disappointing returns." — **[E4-20]**, 2007 letter

Say which of the three this is. It is expressed as a yield, so it carries straight into Q5.
**And do not over-read it [E4-43]:** the *good* class **passes** — capital-hungry growth
*"may well prove to be a satisfactory investment … nothing shabby about earning $82 million
pre-tax on $400 million of net tangible assets."* Only the gruesome fails Q4; good ranks
below great at Q5, and that is all. The good class even has a number **[E5-40]**: ~12% on
retained utility capital is *"quite satisfactory"* — a return-on-retention judgment about an
owned business, distinct from the [E4-28] entry-expectancy floor. What makes cash-consuming
gruesome is only *"unless the cash they consume gets to earn a reasonable return."*

### Staying power

> "Financial staying power requires a company to maintain three strengths under all
> circumstances: **(1) a large and reliable stream of earnings; (2) massive liquid assets and
> (3) no significant near-term cash requirements.** **Ignoring that last necessity is what
> usually leads companies to experience unexpected problems**" — **[E5-11]**, 2014

Score all three. The third is the killer and is the one most often skipped. The reason,
in the corpus's own image **[E5-39]**: *"We will never be dependent on **the kindness of
strangers** … cash is a lot like oxygen: you don't notice it 99.9 percent of the time. But
if it's absent, it's the only thing you notice"* — no bank lines counted, no commercial
paper, nothing depended on. **The metric set itself is selected by business type first
[E5-37]** — *"different numbers are of different importance … depending on the kind of
business"; "there is not one-size-fits-all."* **Strength 2 is
sector-scoped [E2-61]:** for insurers and float businesses, *"you can be broke but flush"*
— cash arrives at inception and losses pay later, so the walking dead *"don't run out of
cash until long after they have run out of net worth"* and *redouble their writing* to keep
it flowing. Read reserve adequacy and net worth there, never cash on hand. **And strength
is an offensive asset [E2-64]:** Berkshire borrowed *before* the need, because *"the most
attractive opportunities may present themselves at a time when credit is extremely
expensive — or even unavailable."* A balance sheet built for the storm is also built to buy
in it. **The design principle behind all three [E2-55]:** *"we do not wish it to be only likely that we can meet
our obligations; **we wish that to be certain** … acceptable long-term results under
**extraordinarily adverse conditions**, rather than optimal results under a normal range of
conditions."* Score the worst case, not the expected one. And **risk here means impairment,
never price movement [E5-29]:** *"Volatility is far from synonymous with risk. Popular
formulas that equate the two terms lead students, investors and CEOs astray"* — a volatile
earnings stream with certain coverage passes; a smooth one with near-term cash requirements
does not.

> "Whenever a bright person, a really bright person, goes broke that has a lot of money,
> **it's because of leverage.**" — **[E4-16]**, 1999 meeting

> "We will **never play financial Russian roulette** with the funds you've entrusted to us,
> even if the metaphorical gun has 100 chambers and only one bullet. In our view, it is
> **madness to risk losing what you need in pursuing what you simply desire.**" — **[E5-12]**, 2014

**There is no leverage ratio in this framework for judging subject companies, and the
corpus does not supply one.** On banks it says twenty to one is *"a common ratio in this
industry"* and concludes about management, not a threshold — then buys a bank at that
leverage **[E3-29]**. *(Scope corrected 2026-08-28: the corpus DOES contain one leverage
number — the operator's own: "My self-imposed standard limit regarding borrowing is **25%
of partnership net worth**" **[E1-18]**, and even that borrowing ran only against hedged
work-outs. A ceiling for the analyst's account, never a screen for companies.)* Leverage is
judged, named and quantified, not screened. **What it supplies instead is a coverage test
[E2-54]:** *"whenever someone creates a capital structure that does not allow **all
interest, both payable and accrued, to be comfortably met out of current cash flow net of
ample capital expenditures — zip up your wallet.**"* Accrued interest counts, cash flow is
the source, and capex comes out first — the solvency read EBITDA-based covenants are built
to avoid. **And read the terms, not just the quantity [E3-52]:** float and deferred taxes
are *"liabilities **without covenants or due dates** attached to them … the benefit of debt
… but saddle us with none of its drawbacks"* — covenant-free, long-dated,
customer-prepaid liabilities are a different animal from covenanted bank debt due next year.
**For foreign names, add jurisdiction [E3-66]:** the US system is *"especially favorable to
shareholder interests … there are many other countries where any good going to public
shareholders has a very low priority and **almost every other constituency stands higher in
line**"* — under-weighed precisely because it resists quantification, and *"some important
factor doesn't lose share of force just because some 'expert' can better measure other
types of force."* A non-US run states where its shareholders stand in the queue.

### Name the specific way this business dies

> "Viewed individually, each company's capital investment decision appeared cost-effective
> and rational; **viewed collectively, the decisions neutralized each other and were
> irrational** … After each round of investment, all the players had more money in the game
> and returns remained anemic." — **[E2-27]**, 1985 letter

And model it, quantified, with a stated likelihood — the corpus's own worked example:

> "**Consider some mathematics:** … If 10% of all $48 billion of the bank's loans … produced
> losses averaging 30% of principal, the company would roughly break even. A year like that
> — **which we consider only a low-level possibility, not a likelihood** — **would not
> distress us.**" — **[E3-24]**, 1990 letter

**Name the mechanism. Quantify it from filed figures. State the likelihood** in that
vocabulary: *likely / a real possibility / a low-level possibility.* If no mechanism can be
named, consider UNKNOWABLE. **The bar for this section is the iron prescription [E4-51]:**
*"I'm not entitled to have an opinion unless I can **state the arguments against my position
better than the people who are in opposition**."* The named-death section is that
prescription's written artifact — a bear case its holders would accept as fairly stated. **And model exposure, not experience [E4-40]** — the corpus's
named failure mode for exactly this exercise: *"all of us in the industry made a fundamental
underwriting mistake by **focusing on experience, rather than exposure**, thereby assuming a
huge terrorism risk for which we received no premium."* A benign loss history late in a good
cycle is *"not only useless, but actually dangerous"* as a guide; the death list comes from
what the filing shows the business is exposed to, never from what has recently happened.

**VERDICT: IN / OUT / UNRESEARCHED / UNKNOWABLE**

## Q5 — WHAT IS IT WORTH, AGAINST A GOVERNMENT BOND?

**Why this question is arithmetic at all [E4-42]:** *"What's needed is an antidote, and in
my opinion that's **quantification**. If you quantify, you won't necessarily rise to
brilliance, but **neither will you sink into craziness.**"*

**And the corpus states the whole routine in one paragraph [E5-34]:** *"We first have to
decide whether we can sensibly estimate an earnings range for **five years out, or more**.
If the answer is yes, we will buy the stock (or business) if it sells at a reasonable price
in relation to **the bottom boundary of our estimate**. If, however, we lack the ability to
estimate future earnings — **which is usually the case** — we simply move on."* Q4's range,
Q5's conservative-end pricing, and the UNRESEARCHED/UNKNOWABLE verdicts are that paragraph
operationalized — most names *should* close unresolved [E5-13].

### Why the bond is the reference at all

> "interest rates … **act on financial valuations the way gravity acts on matter: The higher
> the rate, the greater the downward pull.** … every time the risk-free rate moves by one
> basis point — by 0.01% — **the value of every investment in the country changes.** …
> Nonetheless, the effect — like the invisible pull of gravity — is constantly there."
> — **[E4-15]**, 1999 Fortune essay

**And immediately, the guardrail:**

> "**So I do not have to have a view on interest rates — and I don't have a view on interest
> rates —** to make a decision" — **[E3-32]**, 1994 meeting

The sovereign enters as the **currently observed rate for the currency the business earns
in**. Never as a forecast.

### The three questions

> "How certain are you that there are indeed birds in the bush? When will they emerge and
> how many will there be? What is **the risk-free interest rate (which we consider to be the
> yield on long-term U.S. bonds)?**" — **[E4-01]**, 2000 letter

### ONE BOOK. Owner earnings against the bond.

Every worked valuation the corpus *reports* is a **yield or a multiple** — Wells Fargo at
*"less than five times after-tax earnings"* **[E3-24]**, Coca-Cola at roughly fifteen times,
See's at $25m against ~$2m of earnings. The corpus's *definition* of the target quantity is
nonetheless a discounted value — the Owner's Manual and the 1983/1992/1994 letters define
intrinsic value as "the discounted value of the cash that can be taken out of a business
during its remaining life" — and Buffett endorsed a questioner's staged DCF outright in 1999
(*"you've stated the approach — I couldn't state it better myself"*). What the corpus refuses
is the **artifact**: formal projections, prepared or read (*"I've never prepared a
spreadsheet"* **[E4-21]**; investment-banker projections turned over *"as if it were a
diseased carcass"*).

> "Warren talks about these discounted cash flows. **I've never seen him do one.**"
> — **[E3-34]**, Munger, 1996

So: the DCF is the *definition*; the yield and the multiple are the *practice*; the formal
projection document is the *refusal*. A discounted cash flow may be run as an **engine** — to
convert growth into a rate you can set beside a bond — but it casts no vote and it is never
the reported decision artifact. The reason is stated **[E5-43]**: *"If I had a group under
me, they would try and figure out what I wanted the answer to be … if the hurdle rate is 15
percent or something, having them all come out at **15.1 or 15.2**, because that's what's
going to happen."* Formal apparatus manufactures the wanted output; the analyst's own
arithmetic after the understanding hurdle does not. **And keep the two judgments separate
[E5-42]:** business quality is *"the capital actually needed in the business"*; *"whether
it's a good investment for us depends on **how much we pay for that in the end**"* — which
is why Q2–Q4 judge the business and only Q5 judges the price. Report three things *(CONVENTION, labelled 2026-10-04:
the three-figure report is our format; the corpus compares the return with the bond [E4-21, E5-43] and states no
report form. Found unlabelled by the v5 reconciliation, part 3, row 12.)*:

1. **The yield** — owner earnings ÷ market cap, beside the sovereign.
2. **What the price already assumes** — the year-1 growth needed to justify the quote,
   against what the business has actually done.
3. **What you are paid** — the return at the current price, expressed as **points over the
   sovereign**.

### The floor, then the ranking — REWRITTEN 2026-08-28 (Test D)

*The prior version of this section — "There is no hurdle. There is a ranking." — was built on
a cut of [E4-21] that removed the floor stated two sentences above the quoted fragment, and
removed Munger's clause "not that we don't have one, in a sense." The re-cut row carries the
full passage. The floor and the ranking coexist in the corpus; v4 had kept only the ranking.*

**First, the floor.** The corpus states one, four times across a decade, and it does not move
with the sovereign:

> "The question on opportunity costs and the 10 percent we mention. You know, basically
> **that's the figure we quit on** … we don't want to buy equities where our real expectancy
> is below 10 percent. Now, **that's true whether short rates are 6 percent or whether short
> rates are 1 percent.**" — **[E4-28]**, 2003 meeting; same 10% at **[E3-13]** (1994), the
> 2002 letter, and Munger's 2004 Wesco letter

Its basis is **guessed future opportunity cost**, not today's rate — Munger, in the same
answer: *"we have hurdles in our mind which are basically — well, they involve, implicitly,
future opportunity cost,"* revisable only if rates *"were to more or less permanently
settle"* lower **[E4-28]**. Buffett calls the number arbitrary in the same breath; it is a
judgment, not a formula. **A candidate whose honest pre-tax expectancy sits below roughly 10%
is not ranked — it is quit on**, however it compares with the bond of the day.

**The growth belief that carries a name over the floor must face the corpus's base rate
[E4-35]:** *"I would wager you a very significant sum that **fewer than 10 of the 200 most
profitable companies** in 2000 will attain **15% annual growth in earnings-per-share over
the next 20 years**."* Sustained double-digit growth is a fewer-than-one-in-twenty event
among the *best* businesses; a floor case that needs it carries the burden of proof, in
writing, against those odds. (The same passage names the cost of pretending otherwise:
lofty targets *"corrode CEO behavior"* — the flag at Q3.) **The second bound [E4-44]:**
*"the value of an asset, whatever its character, **cannot over the long term grow faster
than its earnings do**"* — multiple expansion is not a perpetual term, and the assumptions
must be stated (*"The Tinker Bell approach — clap if you believe — just won't cut it"*).
**State the ceiling too [E2-63]:** the WPPSS analysis capped its own upside at face value;
most operating businesses are also capped *"unless more capital is continuously invested,"*
because average ROE barely moves. A Q5 verdict names what bounds the upside, not just the
yield.

**Then, above the floor, the ranking.** The government bond is *"the yardstick at a base"*
**[E4-21]**; the decision above the floor is **comparative** — rank by what each candidate
pays over its own sovereign, and deploy to the top of the list:

> "**Diversification is a protection against ignorance** … to have some super-wonderful
> business and then put money in number 30 or 35 on your list of attractiveness and forego
> putting more money into number one, just strikes Charlie and me as **madness**."
> — **[E3-45]**, 1996 meeting

**This is what the ranking is *for*** — capital goes to rank #1, not pro-rata down the list **[E3-45]**.
**The nothing-option has a location [E2-74]:** between opportunities the money is *parked* —
permanent holdings, arbitrage, bonds (*"our major parking place for money is medium-term
tax-exempt bonds"*) — liquid and waiting, because *"Mr. Market will offer us opportunities —
you can be sure of that."* The box exists so cash pressure never bends the standards.

**The switch itself has a threshold [E4-45]:** in the hurdle answer's own words, *"even if
you had something you were really familiar with and were very sure on the 8 percent, **8
1/2 wouldn't tempt you** if somebody came along, as a practical matter."* A ranking
inversion of half a point over a well-understood holding moves nothing — the better-idea
trigger needs a material gap, before the tax friction [E2-46] is even counted.

**Concentration carries a license condition [E2-62]:** it *"makes sense only because"* the
concentrating owner's own position has *"exceptional financial strength"* — for capital
*"not strong enough to withstand a big error"* it *"would be totally inappropriate, no
matter how attractive an investment opportunity might appear when analyzed on the basis of
probabilities."* Sizing is a function of the holder's strength as well as the idea's rank.
**And the standard runs on the operator too [E4-53]:** *"Proper allocation of capital is an
investor's number one job"* — highest use measured by next-best use, heavy bets only when
the odds are greatly favorable. Every test this framework runs on a CEO's allocation
applies to the portfolio decisions made with it.

### Where certainty is priced — REWRITTEN 2026-08-28 (Test D)

*The prior "+3% certainty spread over the long bond, wider for less certainty" read [E3-13]'s
10-minus-7 arithmetic as a spread and stopped before the row's final sentence. The corpus,
1996–1999, four independent times, says the opposite:*

> "If you say I'm going to stick an extra 6 percent in on the interest rate to allow for the
> fact — I tend to think that's kind of nonsense. … It may look mathematical. But it's
> **mathematical gibberish** in my view. You better just stick with businesses that you can
> understand, **use the government bond rate**. And when you can buy them — something you
> understand well — at **a significant discount**, then, you should start getting excited."
> — **[E3-42]**, 1996 meeting; restated 1997, 1998, 1999

**Certainty is never priced in the rate.** It is handled twice, in the places the corpus
names **[E3-42]**: at the **understanding gate** (a business you cannot be certain about fails Q1 —
the go/no-go valve), and in the **discount to value demanded at the end** (Bar 1's margin,
spent once **[E4-11]**). Any
per-name risk premium in the discount rate is the named error. Certainty is still priced
**once** — the margin — and may not be stacked. **The source form of the whole rule
[E4-48]:** *"don't try and put too much windage in at every level … try to be as realistic
as you can on those numbers, but with any errors being on the conservative side. **And then
when you get all through, you apply the margin of safety.**"* **And that end margin is
graded, not fixed [E3-60]:** perfect understanding needs *"very little in the way of a
margin of safety"*; a bridge over the Grand Canyon needs more than one six inches above a
creek — *"It depends on the nature of the underlying risk. The biggest thing to do is
understand the business."*

### Report the value as a range, in round numbers

> "Using precise numbers is, in fact, foolish; **working with a range of possibilities is the
> better approach.**" — **[E4-01]**

A figure like "$71.84" claims a precision this method cannot support **[E4-01]**; "roughly
$70 to $85" is the honest form.

## Q6 — WHAT WOULD PROVE ME WRONG, AND WHEN DO I SELL?

**The exit metric is pre-committed, before entry:**

> "I believe in establishing yardsticks **prior to the act**" — **[E1-02]**

**The sell rule, complete:**

> "Sometimes, of course, **the market may judge a business to be more valuable than the
> underlying facts** would indicate it is. In such a case, we will sell our holdings.
> Sometimes, also, we will sell a security that is fairly valued or even undervalued because
> **we require funds for a still more undervalued investment or one we believe we understand
> better.** We need to emphasize, however, that **we do not sell holdings just because they
> have appreciated or because we have held them for a long time.** … We are quite content to
> hold any security indefinitely, **so long as the prospective return on equity capital of
> the underlying business is satisfactory, management is competent and honest, and the market
> does not overvalue the business.**" — **[E2-28]**, 1987 letter

Two triggers. Three hold conditions, which are Q2, Q3 and Q5 restated. Price appreciation
and holding period are **explicitly rejected** as reasons.

**When the second hold condition fails — good business, management misallocating — the
corpus says exit, not engage [E4-24].** Asked exactly this by a shareholder holding a
durable-advantage business whose managers began *"doing things which are far from
intelligent"*:

> **BUFFETT:** "the history that Charlie and I have had of persuading decent, intelligent
> people, who we thought were doing unintelligent things, to change their course of action
> has been poor. Would you agree with that, Charlie, or no?"
> **MUNGER:** "**Worse than poor.**"
> **BUFFETT:** "if you really think you're in with people that have got a good business, but
> **they're going to keep doing dumb things with your money, you'll probably do better to get
> out** and get in with people who've got a good business and you think they're going to do
> sensible things with it." — **[E4-24]**, 2001 meeting

The damage vector to watch for is **loss of focus** — capital wandering away from the base
business **[E3-40]** — not operating polish. **Engagement is not a plan the corpus endorses:
it rates its own record at it as worse than poor.**

**The real trigger is a moat downgrade, and it is slow:**

> "we sell — really when we … **reevaluat[e] the economic characteristics of the business.**
> … That doesn't mean we think that the company is going into some disastrous period …
> **And those beliefs change quite gradually.**" — **[E4-17]**, 2002 meeting

**The monitoring question, in one sentence:**

> "The question is whether this erosion is just part of an **aberrational cycle** … or whether
> the business has **slipped in a way that permanently reduces intrinsic business values.**"
> — **[E3-30]**, 1990 letter

That is a question, not a tripwire. "Beliefs change quite gradually" is direct evidence
against any short-window mechanical exit.

**Do not trim winners:**

> "**The weeds wither away in significance as the flowers bloom.** Over time, it takes just a
> few winners to work wonders." — **[E5-14]**, 2022 letter

**Inactivity is a mechanism, not a mood:**

> "**In allocating capital, activity does not correlate with achievement.** Indeed … frenetic
> behavior is often counterproductive. Therefore, Charlie and I mainly just wait for the
> phone to ring." — **[E3-35]**, 1998 letter

**The switching penalty binds trigger 2 [E2-46]:** the corpus quantifies what a
sell-to-switch costs a taxable holder — annual realization versus a single compounder over
twenty years came to **$25,250 versus $692,000** after tax. Munger's per-annum form
**[E3-64]**: taxed once at the end, a 15%/30-year compounder keeps 13.3%; taxed annually,
9.75% — *"over 3.5 percent"* a year from sitting still. Friction stacks on top **[E3-67]**:
management plus in-and-out costs *"can easily reach 3 percent per annum"*, invisible to
accounting. So the full switching bar is **tax + friction + a material gap [E4-45]**,
computed for the account that holds the position. **And the counterweight, from the same
passage [E3-64]:** *"trying to minimize taxes too much is one of the great standard causes
of really dumb mistakes"* — tax never vetoes a right decision; the corpus's own Apple trim
paid the tax when the view crystallized [E2-40].

**The scope structure — CORRECTED 2026-08-28 (Test D).** The prior warning here said
Owner's Manual 11's never-sell applies to controlled businesses only, with E2-28 governing
all securities. The 1987 letter itself says more, nine lines below E2-28:

> "our insurance companies own three marketable common stocks that **we would not sell even
> though they became far overpriced** … we view these investments **exactly like our
> successful controlled businesses** — a permanent part of Berkshire … To that, I will add
> one qualifier: … we would, **if absolutely necessary**, sell portions" — **[E2-39]**, 1987

So Q6's full structure: **E2-28's three hold conditions govern every marketable security.** The 1987 letter
treated three marketable stocks as permanent **[E2-39]**; the 2016 letter withdrew that treatment for all marketable
securities and amended the Owner's Manual to say the never-sell principle covers controlled businesses only: *"we have
made no commitment that Berkshire will hold any of its marketable securities forever. [...] That principle covers
controlled businesses, not marketable securities. This year I've added a final sentence to #11 to ensure that our owners
understand that we regard any marketable security as available for sale, however unlikely such a sale now seems."*
**[E5-64]**, 2016 letter. **No marketable holding is exempt from [E2-28]'s overvaluation condition on the corpus's
authority.** A holder who nonetheless designates a stock permanent does so as a CONVENTION of his own, confessed in
writing and in advance per **[E1-02]**, and it still yields to [E2-40]'s crystallized view. *(Corrected 2026-10-05 from
the PRIME RULE 5 case of that date, approved by the operator. The paragraph had read, since the Test D correction of

**And the counter-trigger the gradualism rule was missing:**

> "It was a mistake to buy fifteen-year bonds, and yet we did; **we made an even more serious
> mistake in not selling them (at losses, if necessary) when our present views began to
> crystallize.**" — **[E2-40]**, 1979 letter

**[E4-17]**'s *"beliefs change quite gradually"* governs how a moat belief *forms*. Once the
view **has crystallized, delay is the graver error** — the 2020 airline exit (complete,
weeks, no half measures) is the same doctrine practised. Q6 carries both: slow to conclude,
fast once concluded.

# IV. THE TWO BARS

The corpus supports two different standards for two different situations. **Keep them
separate; never apply both to the same number.** **CONVENTION** *(labelled 2026-09-20)*: the
two bars are each corpus; the rule that they may never touch the same number is this
document's own reconciliation of the two passages, made so that conservatism is spent once
**[E4-11]** rather than at both bars.

### Bar 1 — the normal method
> "you take all of the variables and calculate them reasonably conservatively. **But you
> don't try and put too much windage in at every level.** … don't focus too much on taking it
> on each variable in terms of **the discount rate and the growth rate** … And then when you
> get all through, **you apply the margin of safety.**" — **[E4-11]**, 2004 meeting

**Realistic inputs, then one margin at the end.** Conservatism is spent **once**. If a run
applies it in more than one place, it says so and justifies it.

**How large a margin?** The corpus scales it and gives illustrations, not a rule:
- *"if you understood a business perfectly … you would need **very little** in the way of a
  margin of safety. So **the more volatile the business** is … the larger the margin"* — **[E3-26]**
- the bridge: a 9,800lb load, a 15,000lb bridge — **~35%** — **[E3-25]**
- the Grand Canyon variant: a 4,000lb truck on a 10,000lb bridge — **60%**, the widest figure
  the corpus states — **[E3-26]**
- for a business you genuinely understand: *"we don't think we need a huge margin of safety
  … we will buy those as much **closer to a dollar on the dollar**"* — **[E4-12]**, 2007
- *"If you can repurchase your shares at a significant discount from intrinsic value, it like **buying dollar
  bills at 90 cents or 80 cents** or whatever it may be, and it's a very sure way of improving per-share value."*
  — **[E5-63]**, 2013 meeting *(corrected 2026-10-04: until then this line read "it's hard to go wrong when you're
  buying dollar bills for 80 cents or less", cited to [E5-09], whose text says that in repurchase decisions price is
  all-important. Those words are not on the shelf; they were a paraphrase wearing a citation. The row E5-63 was
  added first, then this line. Found by the v5 reconciliation, part 3, row 49.)*

**And the remedy for uncertainty is not a bigger margin:**
> "we don't really try to compensate for that sort of thing by having some extra large
> margin of safety. **We really want to try to go on to something that we understand
> better.**" — **[E4-12]**

State the margin you used and which illustration it sits nearest. **60% is the ceiling the
corpus states [E3-26]**; beyond it, an earlier question should have failed.

### Bar 2 — the screamer test
> "Usually, the range must be so wide that **no useful conclusion can be reached.**
> Occasionally, though, **even very conservative estimates** … reveal that the price quoted is
> **startlingly low in relation to value.**" — **[E4-01]**, 2000 letter

Take the conservative end of the range and ask whether the price already clears it. **No
margin is added on top — "startlingly low" is what you observe, not what you subtract.**
Three outcomes, and the middle one is the usual one and is a finished answer:

| | |
|---|---|
| price below the conservative case | the rare case; act |
| price inside the range | **no useful conclusion — move on** |
| price above the whole range | no |

> "it ought to just kind of **scream at you**" — **[E3-25]**, 1996 meeting

**What a screamer actually looked like [E3-65]:** the Washington Post at *"about 20 percent
of the value to a private owner … and, in addition … the top hand in a game that was clearly
going to end up with one winner and a management with a lot of integrity and intelligence.
That one was a real dream."* The 60% ceiling is the outer bound of buyable; the record was
built at one-fifth of obvious value with the structure and managers aligned. **The
long-horizon qualifier [E3-17]:** over decades the business return converges on the owner —
*"If the business earns 6 percent on capital over 40 years … you're not going to make much
different than a 6 percent return, even if you originally buy it at a huge discount"* — so
the entry discount dominates short horizons, and business quality dominates long ones. Both
tests bind; neither excuses the other.

# V. WHAT THIS FRAMEWORK DOES NOT CLAIM

**The market-beating claim is unproven.** Every backtest that beat the market ran through a
screen with a look-ahead bug and was withdrawn on 2026-07-25. The only uncontaminated
full-gate test **underperformed** over 32 years. Of the clean tests since: the basket beat
the index at **2 of 8** anchors, mean **−0.81 points a year**; the mechanical layer won
**49.6%** of 944 one-year holdings, with the median pass trailing by 1.45 points.

**What is demonstrated** is narrower and all of it is exclusion on filed facts: the honesty
test catches the same companies at independent dates years before the famous scandals; the
full owner-earnings formula catches businesses with positive net income and negative owner
earnings; the mechanical layer's passes lose money less often and less deeply than the names
it rejects (the loss leg, Test E, thin and pre-registered); and the project has repeatedly
found and published its own errors, including the bug that voided four of its own best
results.

**And the corpus itself says the record would not transfer even if the method were applied
perfectly.** Munger's own accounting of why Berkshire worked:

> "Only four large factors occur to me: (1) The constructive peculiarities of Buffett, (2)
> The constructive peculiarities of the Berkshire system, (3) Good luck, and (4) The weirdly
> intense, contagious devotion of some shareholders and other admirers … **the heavy freight
> was carried by the constructive peculiarities, the weird devotion, and their
> interactions.**" — **[E5-19]**, 2014

The selection method alone is not on that list. The *system* — insurance float, permanent
capital, near-zero friction, inbound deal flow, *"no department of acquisitions under
pressure to buy"* — carried freight that no private investor applying these six questions
possesses. This is not modesty; it is the corpus's own attribution, and it is the deepest
reason the line below exists. What the framework CAN inherit is the promise structure of
Ground Rule 7 **[E1-17]**: value-based selection, the attempt at permanent-loss
minimization, and a common destiny — none of which is a promise of results.

**Never present this framework as proven to beat the market.**
