# 1. OWNER EARNINGS, THE FULL (c) CLAUSE
`Shareholder Letters/1986 Letter.txt:1650` · [E2-23]

> "These represent (a) reported earnings plus (b) depreciation, depletion, amortization,
> and certain other non-cash charges [...] less **(c) the average annual amount of
> capitalized expenditures for plant and equipment, etc. that the business requires to
> fully maintain its long-term competitive position and its unit volume. (If the business
> requires additional working capital to maintain its competitive position and unit
> volume, the increment also should be included in (c).** However, businesses following
> the LIFO inventory method usually do not require additional working capital if unit
> volume does not change.)"
>
> "Our owner-earnings equation does not yield the deceptively precise figures provided by
> GAAP, since **(c) must be a guess — and one sometimes very difficult to make.** [...]
> We agree with Keynes's observation: **'I would rather be vaguely right than precisely
> wrong.'**"

And, thirty lines later, the failure mode named directly:

> "All of this points up **the absurdity of the 'cash flow' numbers** that are often set
> forth in Wall Street reports. These numbers routinely include (a) plus (b) — **but do
> not subtract (c).**"

## SYNOPSIS
One sentence carries four constraints, and the framework has broken three of them at
different times:
1. **"average annual amount"** — a multi-year figure. A single year is not it. *(This is
   the exact clause I violated this morning on TBTC.)*
2. **"requires to fully maintain"** — maintenance capex, not total capex. Total capex
   includes growth and understates owner earnings for any expanding business.
3. **"the increment also should be included in (c)"** — the working-capital term is
   mandatory, not optional. *(Omitted entirely from the 2026-07-31 TJX table.)*
4. **"must be a guess"** — (c) is a judgment, and Buffett says so in the same breath he
   defines it. It is not derivable from the statements.

The Keynes line is the framing for all of it: **the answer is meant to be roughly right
and openly approximate.**

## RULING
- **Rulings 10 and 11 stand, and the "average annual amount" clause is elevated to the
  operative test.** Owner earnings are computed on a multi-year mean. Any single-year OE
  figure is a diagnostic, never an anchor.
- **The maintenance-capex band is a DISCLOSED JUDGMENT, never a computed output.**
  "Must be a guess" forecloses any construction that presents it as arithmetic.
- **NEW — the Keynes clause is turned against our own machinery.** "Vaguely right rather
  than precisely wrong" is an argument against the twelve-cell sensitivity grid and the
  two-decimal IV. **Gate 6 should report intrinsic value as a RANGE in round numbers, not
  a point estimate to the cent.** Reporting TJX's fair value as "$71.84" implies a
  precision the method cannot support; "roughly $70 to $85" is the honest form and is
  what the corpus asks for.

# 2. "NO ANSWERS IN THE FINANCIAL STATEMENTS"
`Annual Meetings/1994 Annual Meeting.txt:1233` · [E3-27]

> "**The numbers in any accounting report mean nothing, per se, as to economic value.
> They are guidelines to tell you something about how to get at economic value. But they
> don't tell you anything. There are no answers in the financial statements. There are
> guidelines to enable you to figure out the answer. And to figure out that answer, you
> have to understand something about business. You don't have to understand a lot about
> mathematics. I mean, the math is not complicated.** But you do have to understand
> something about the business."

From the same answer, two sentences earlier:

> "**we are more concerned with the certainty of those numbers than we are with getting
> the one that looks absolutely the cheapest**, but based upon numbers that we don't have
> any — we don't have great confidence in."

## SYNOPSIS
Two distinct claims, both load-bearing.

**First: statements are inputs, not outputs.** A verdict cannot be read off a line item.
This is the direct answer to "does the corpus use XBRL" — XBRL delivers precisely and
only "the numbers in the accounting report," which he says mean *nothing per se*.

**Second, and easy to miss: "the math is not complicated."** The framework's most
elaborate component is its mathematics. He is saying that is not where the difficulty
lives, and by implication not where the edge lives either.

**Third: certainty beats cheapness.** This independently confirms Ruling 4-C from a
different passage than the one 4-C was built on.

## RULING
- **Ruling 11 is CONFIRMED and should be ratified.** XBRL is transcription and screening.
  No name reaches Gate 6 without the filing read and recorded.
- **NEW — "the math is not complicated" is corpus authority for the audit's Part 2.**
  The elaboration of Book Two, the sensitivity grid and the diagnostics is our invention
  and the corpus does not merely fail to support it, it says the difficulty lies
  elsewhere. **This strengthens the case for deleting Gate 8 and demoting Book One.**
- **Ruling 4-C gains a second independent source.** "More concerned with the certainty of
  those numbers than with getting the one that looks absolutely the cheapest" is the same
  ruling stated in 1994 that 4-C derived from Munger in 1996.

# 3. WHAT HE READS ANNUAL REPORTS *FOR*
`Annual Meetings/1996 Annual Meeting.txt:681` · [E3-28]

> "I like to know as much as I can about **the person that's running it and how they think
> about the business and what's really going on in the business.** In other words, I would
> like to have a report that would be identical to what — **if I owned half of a company
> but was away for a year, and I had a partner who owned the other half** — when I came
> back, that he would tell me about what had taken place during the past year and what he
> foresaw coming up."
>
> "If we own stock in a company and in an industry, and there are eight other companies
> that are in the same industry, **I want to own or be on the mailing list for the reports
> for the other eight, because I can't understand how my company is doing unless I
> understand what the other eight are doing.** I want to have the perspective of, in terms
> of market share, what's going on in the business or their margins or the trend of
> margins [...] **I can't be an intelligent owner of a business unless I know what all the
> other businesses in that industry are doing.**"

## SYNOPSIS
He reads for three things, in this order: **the person**, **what is really happening**,
and **the competitors**. The third is stated as a hard precondition, twice, in the
strongest language he uses anywhere: *cannot* understand, *cannot* be an intelligent
owner.

The half-owner-away-for-a-year test is also the cleanest statement of the candor standard
in the corpus, and it belongs to Gate 3 as much as the Owner's Manual does.

## RULING
- **Ruling 11's COMPETITOR ROW is CONFIRMED and should be ratified.** Gate 2 currently
  asks for one company's moat metric and trend. A moat is a claim about *relative*
  position; the corpus says it cannot be evidenced from one company's numbers. Every moat
  verdict in this project was issued without it.
- **Scope it honestly so it is actually done.** "The other eight" is not literal. The
  requirement: **name the two or three closest competitors and give the SAME primary moat
  metric over the SAME window, filing-sourced.** Where unavailable (private, foreign,
  unsegmented), say so and hold the moat class **PROVISIONAL**, which carries the wider
  NARROW spread until resolved.
- **NEW — the half-owner test moves into Gate 3** as the written candor standard,
  replacing the vaguer "integrity (binary)" line. The question becomes: *does this
  company's reporting tell me what a 50% partner would have told me?* TJX's separate
  quantification of the tariff benefit at every line passes it; a company burying the
  same item in an adjusted figure does not.
- **Concrete first application:** TJX's WIDE moat is currently asserted against Ross and
  Burlington rather than measured. Until Marmaxx's +1% comp is set beside theirs, we
  cannot say whether that is a TJX problem or an off-price sector problem.

# 4. "IN, OUT, AND TOO HARD"
`Annual Meetings/2006 Annual Meeting.txt:215`

## ⚠ ATTRIBUTION CORRECTION
I gave this in chat as Buffett's line. **It is Munger's, quoted by Buffett.** The text:
"**Charlie says**, you know, 'We've got three boxes at the company: in, out, and too
hard.'" Prime Rule 1 requires the correction; the substance is unaffected.

> "There's just — there's just games that are too tough. Charlie says, you know, **'We've
> got three boxes at the company: in, out, and too hard.'** And **a lot of things end up
> in the 'too hard' pile, and it doesn't bother us.** You know, we don't have to be able
> to do everything well. If you go to the Olympics, you know, if you run the hundred meter
> well, you don't have to throw the shotput."
>
> "What I've learned is I know enough not — **to know that I don't know enough** to make
> an investment decision."

Munger, later the same meeting (`:821`):
> "we have a wonderful way of coping with a lot of these things. We have this 'too hard'
> pile. [...] **If something is too hard to do, we look for something that isn't too hard
> to do. What could be more obvious than that?**"

And the sharpest version, 2005 (`Annual Meetings/2005 Annual Meeting.txt:977`):
> "one of the interesting things about investment is that **there's no degree of
> difficulty factor.** [...] **we get paid, not for jumping over 7-foot bars, but for
> stepping over 1-foot bars.** [...] Now maybe we cast out too many things as being too
> hard and thereby narrow our universe. **But I'd rather have the universe be a little
> smaller than it really is, than being interpreted as larger than it is.**"

## SYNOPSIS
**This says something more precise than "have fewer gates," and I proposed the wrong
simplification off it.**

The corpus does not complain about the number of tests. It insists on a **third outcome**.
Their filter has three boxes and ours has two: every gate in this framework returns PASS
or FAIL. But **FAIL and TOO HARD are completely different findings.** FAIL says the
business is bad. TOO HARD says the business may well be excellent and I cannot tell.
Collapsing the second into the first produces false confidence in both directions.

"No degree of difficulty factor" is the other half: **hard analysis earns no premium.**
A conclusion reached after fighting through a twelve-cell grid is worth no more than an
obvious one, and is usually worth less. And the final line is an explicit instruction to
err toward casting things out.

## RULING
- **ADD A THIRD VERDICT: `TOO HARD`, available at every gate.** It is not a FAIL, records
  no judgment about the business, and closes the file without prejudice. Any gate may
  return it, and a name may be re-opened later if better evidence appears.
- **TOO HARD becomes the DEFAULT when evidence is thin**, replacing the current practice
  of forcing a PASS on weak evidence and papering it with a caveat. This directly fixes
  the pattern that produced the Japan runs, which recorded PASS verdicts marked
  "unverified" and "general-knowledge" — those are TOO HARD verdicts wearing a PASS
  label, and treating them as passes is what put unverified names in the portfolio.
- **This SUPERSEDES my "eight gates to six" proposal as the primary simplification.**
  Deleting Gate 8 still stands on its own evidence (computed in 10 of 121 runs). But the
  corpus's actual instruction is not to have fewer questions; it is to be far more willing
  to stop answering. **A framework with eight gates and a real TOO HARD box is simpler in
  use than a six-gate framework that forces every name to a verdict.**
- **"No degree of difficulty" is the disposal authority for the valuation apparatus.**
  Where a verdict requires the sensitivity grid to adjudicate it, that is a 7-foot bar,
  and the ruling is TOO HARD rather than a narrowly-argued PASS.

# 5. WELLS FARGO: VALUATION VERSUS THE STRESS CASE
`Shareholder Letters/1990 Letter.txt:326-332` · [E3-24]

> "Aided by their flight from bank stocks, we purchased our 10% interest in Wells Fargo
> for $290 million, **less than five times after-tax earnings**, and less than three times
> pre-tax earnings."
>
> "**Consider some mathematics:** Wells Fargo **currently earns** well over $1 billion
> pre-tax annually after expensing more than $300 million for loan losses. **If 10% of all
> $48 billion of the bank's loans — not just its real estate loans — were hit by problems
> in 1991, and these produced losses (including foregone interest) averaging 30% of
> principal, the company would roughly break even.** A year like that — **which we consider
> only a low-level possibility, not a likelihood** — **would not distress us.** In fact, at
> Berkshire we would love to acquire businesses or invest in capital projects that produced
> no return for a year, but that could then be expected to earn 20% on growing equity."

## SYNOPSIS
Three moves, cleanly separated, and the separation is the whole lesson.

1. **He values off CURRENT earnings.** "Currently earns." Five times earnings, not five
   times some worst-case figure.
2. **The catastrophe is a SEPARATE, QUANTIFIED, COMPANY-SPECIFIC scenario.** Not a generic
   haircut: 10% of the loan book impaired, 30% loss severity, and he names the resulting
   outcome (break-even). He also assigns it a probability: "a low-level possibility, not
   a likelihood."
3. **Surviving it is the test, and passing costs the valuation nothing.** A zero-return
   year "would not distress us" for a business that can then earn 20% on growing equity.

The framework had been doing all three at once and in the wrong place: stress-testing at
Gate 4, then *also* anchoring the valuation to the stress year at Gate 6.

## RULING
- **Ruling 9 is CONFIRMED.** The trough anchor stays retired. Book One reads normal
  earning power; the trough is a Gate 4 survival input.
- **NEW — RULING 9-A is now settled, in favour of the CURRENT tier.** The open question
  from this morning (current tier versus 5-year median) is answered by "currently earns."
  **The anchor is the most recent verified tier**, with the 5-year median demoted to a
  consistency cross-check. Where the two diverge materially and the divergence cannot be
  explained, the verdict is **TOO HARD**, not an average.
- **NEW — Gate 4's stress test is upgraded from generic to specific.** "A 50% owner-
  earnings decline for two years" is our invention and is the same test for a retailer, a
  bank and a software company. The corpus models **the actual mechanism of failure for
  the actual business**, quantifies it, and states the probability. **Gate 4 now requires:
  name the specific way this business gets hurt, quantify it from filed figures, and state
  the outcome.** For TJX that is closeout supply drying up, not a 50% haircut. The generic
  test remains the floor for names where a specific mechanism cannot be quantified — and
  where it cannot, that is a signal to consider TOO HARD.
- **NEW — probability is stated, not implied.** "A low-level possibility, not a
  likelihood" is a written probability judgment attached to the downside. Gate 5's
  inversions currently score D/DS/P/U with no likelihood attached. **Each inversion should
  carry a one-line probability characterisation** in his language: likely / a real
  possibility / a low-level possibility.
