# THE MANAGER STANDARD — Q3
**2026-08-26.** Built by reading the whole citation shelf for what Buffett and Munger say
about judging a manager. Every rule below carries its verbatim source. Nothing here is
invented; where the corpus is silent, this file says so instead of filling the gap.

**Read this first, before any of the criteria.** The people who built this test say it
fails, and they say so in the latest letter in the shelf:

> "Never deal with a rascal under the expectation that you can prevent him from cheating
> you." Many bankers who thought they could "manage" the rascal problem have learned the
> wisdom of Mr. McCulloch's advice — and I have as well. **People are not that easy to read.
> Sincerity and empathy can easily be faked.** That is as true now as it was in 1863. **[E5-17]**

Munger, on their own screen missing a reserve fraud at a company he would have ranked near
the top for culture: *"If it can happen at Royal Dutch, believe me, it can happen a lot of
other places."* Buffett, in the same answer: *"Charlie and I would not have spotted it."*
And asked how he ranks managers at all: *"you have to understand that **we cheat**. We buy
businesses with good managers."*

**A Q3 pass is therefore never a clearance. It is the absence of found disqualifiers.**
Write it that way in the run file.

---

# 1. THE WEIGHT RULE — decide this before anything else

**How much Q3 counts is not fixed. The business sets it.** Three determinants, each stated
independently in the corpus, all measuring the same thing: **how much damage can this
manager do before you can react?**

| raises Q3 to a **gate** | the test | source |
|---|---|---|
| **Daily execution** — the business must be run well every day | Is this a have-to-be-smart-**once** business or a have-to-be-smart-**every-day** business? | **[E3-38]** |
| **Control** — you buy the whole thing and cannot exit | Would you still buy it at a cheaper price with a run-of-the-mill manager? | **[E1-16]** |
| **Leverage** — small asset errors destroy equity | Does the balance sheet magnify every managerial act? | **[E3-29]** |

Buffett on the first: *"if you were smart enough to buy a network TV station very early in
the game, you could put in a shiftless and backward nephew to run things, and the business
would still do well for decades… **For a retailer, hiring that nephew would be an express
ticket to bankruptcy.**"* **[E3-38]**

On the third, and this is the sentence that governs: *"Because leverage of 20:1 magnifies
the effects of managerial strengths and weaknesses, **we have no interest in purchasing
shares of a poorly-managed bank at a 'cheap' price.** Instead, our only interest is in buying
into well-managed banks at fair prices."* **[E3-29]**

## The operative consequence

- **All three low** → Q3 is a **qualitative overlay**. A mediocre manager is survivable and
  price does the work. Record the finding; do not stop the run on manager quality alone.
- **Any one high** → Q3 is a **binary gate**, and **no price compensates**. This is the only
  place in the framework where cheapness is explicitly ruled out as a remedy, and the corpus
  states it twice — at **[E1-16]** for control and **[E3-29]** for leverage.

**State which case you are in, and why, at the top of every Q3.** A run that does not
declare its weight case has not done Q3.

# 2. THE BINARY — what disqualifies, at any price, in any weight case

**The cleanest statement in the shelf:**

> "Disappointments are inevitable. **We are understanding about business mistakes; our
> tolerance for personal misconduct is zero.**" **[E5-16]**

That sentence separates the two things a manager test must never merge. **Competence errors
are priced. Integrity failures are refused.** They are not two ends of one scale.

**The veto, and its limit, in one passage:**

> "we do not wish to join with managers who lack admirable qualities, no matter how
> attractive the prospects of their business. **We've never succeeded in making a good deal
> with a bad person.**" **[E2-31]**

Read the whole row: the same paragraph says liking the managers *"will not ensure success: A
second-class textile or department-store company won't prosper simply because its managers
are men that you would be pleased to see your daughter marry."* **The veto runs one way.**
Bad people disqualify a good business; good people do not rescue a bad one.

**Disqualifying, therefore:** conduct that would fail the front-page test; a matter that
became public and was not acted on; conduct the manager concealed. **Not disqualifying on
its own:** an operating loss, a bad acquisition, a missed forecast, a fine for something
that is not dishonesty toward owners.

> **Worked precedent.** TJX's 2022 CPSC penalty is real and was ruled a **product-safety**
> failure, not financial dishonesty toward owners. It is not a Q3 failure. No keyword can
> make that distinction, which is why the tool that tried was withdrawn.

# 3. THE PRIMARY TEST — and it is a number

The corpus names one measurable test and calls it primary:

> "**The primary test of managerial economic performance is the achievement of a high
> earnings rate on equity capital employed** (without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry,
> etc.) **and not the achievement of consistent gains in earnings per share.**" **[E2-01]**

The reason sits four lines above it in the same letter: *"even a 'stopped clock' can look
like a growth stock if the dividend payout ratio is low."*

**Why this test and not another:** it is defined *in opposition to the metric managements
game*. That is why it pairs with §4 — §3 measures the performance, §4 catches the gaming.

**Run it as a multi-year series, never a snapshot.** Munger's reason:

> "When a man's steps are consecutively taken toward disaster, with each step being very
> small, the brain's contrast-misreaction tendency will often let the man go too far toward
> disaster to be able to avoid it… **Cognition, misled by tiny changes involving low
> contrast, will often miss a trend that is destiny.**"
> — *The Psychology of Human Misjudgment*, Talk 11

**And read the balance sheet first.** *"I spend more time looking at
balance sheets than I do income statements… I like to look at balance sheets over an 8 or 10
year period before I even look at the income account because **there are certain things it's
harder to hide or play games with on the balance sheet** than with the income statement."*
— **[E5-27]**, 2025 meeting. The 8-or-10-year window attaches to the **balance-sheet** read;
the income series is separately "multi-year, never a snapshot."

# 4. THE FOUR READABLE RED FLAGS — the corpus's own checklist

**This is the finding that voided the 2026-08-26 integrity tool.** That tool scanned Item 3
Legal Proceedings for regulatory keywords. **Buffett's own checklist never looks there.** It
looks at accounting and disclosure choices, and it is stated as advice to investors:

> "**First, beware of companies displaying weak accounting.** If a company still does not
> expense options, or if its pension assumptions are fanciful, watch out. When managements
> take the low road in aspects that are visible, it is likely they are following a similar
> path behind the scenes. **There is seldom just one cockroach in the kitchen.** […]
> **Second, unintelligible footnotes usually indicate untrustworthy management.** If you
> can't understand a footnote or other managerial explanation, it's usually because the CEO
> doesn't want you to. […] **Finally, be suspicious of companies that trumpet earnings
> projections and growth expectations.** […] Managers that always promise to 'make the
> numbers' will at some point be tempted to make up the numbers." **[E4-22]**

Plus a fourth, which compresses four diagnoses into one observable:

> "we have never invested in companies that are **hell-bent on issuing shares**. That
> behavior is one of the surest indicators of a promotion-minded management, weak
> accounting, a stock that is overpriced and — all too often — outright dishonesty." **[E5-15]**

| flag | where it is read | dated to |
|---|---|---|
| Weak accounting — comp not expensed, fanciful pension assumptions | the filed statements | filing date |
| Unintelligible footnotes | the notes | filing date |
| Trumpeted projections and growth targets | MD&A, releases, calls | publication date |
| Serial share issuance | share count across years | filing date |

**Each flag is a prompt to read, never a verdict.** A fired flag obliges you to go look; it
does not close the question. **[E4-13]** applies: an outside judgment about a management
decision is made without the facts the manager had.

**Point-in-time honesty.** Every flag is dated to when it became **public**, so a run
anchored at any date can cut the list there and see only what was knowable then.

# 5. THE AFFIRMATIVE CRITERIA — what good looks like

The corpus states the positive attributes once, compactly, for two management pairs at once
(Reichardt/Hazen at Wells Fargo, Murphy/Burke at Capital Cities):

> "First, each pair is stronger than the sum of its parts because each partner understands,
> trusts and admires the other. Second, both managerial teams **pay able people well, but
> abhor having a bigger head count than is needed**. Third, both **attack costs as vigorously
> when profits are at record levels as when they are under pressure**. Finally, both **stick
> with what they understand and let their abilities, not their egos, determine what they
> attempt**." **[E3-37]**

**The third is the only one observable from filings across a cycle, and it is the sharpest.**
Anyone cuts costs in the bad year. Cutting them in the record year is the test.

Beyond those four, in descending order of corpus support:

1. **Capital allocation, which dominates the rest.** Why it is scarce and why it dominates:
   **[E2-29]**. Buffett's image for the defect: a CEO reaches the top through sales or law,
   *"it's like getting to Carnegie Hall playing the violin, and then you walk out on the
   stage and they hand you a piano."*
2. **The retention test.** Does a retained dollar become more than a dollar of value? Applied
   through the two buyback conditions **[E5-01, E5-08]**, with **[E4-13]**'s humility clause
   attached. Buffett: buybacks are *"a pretty good test for some people, in terms of
   managements, how they think about something."*
3. **Candor.** The reason honesty and rationality are one question: **[E2-26]**. The
   operative form — *"The CEO who misleads others in public may eventually mislead himself in
   private"* (Owner's Manual, Principle 12). Test it by whether the CEO wrote the letter, and
   whether the most important operating number appears in the report at all.
4. **Resistance to the institutional imperative**, enumerated as four observable behaviours:
   **[E2-30]**, compact definition at **[E3-02]**. *"Institutional dynamics, **not venality or
   stupidity**, set businesses on these courses."*
5. **Owner-orientation with the manager's own money.** *"who ever washes a rental car?"*
   Shares **bought**, not granted.
6. **Latency to bad news.** The Wells Fargo fault is located precisely: not the incentive
   design but the failure to act after learning. *"An ounce of prevention is worth more than
   a pound of cure. And I would say a pound of cure, promptly applied, is worth a ton of cure
   that's delayed."*

# 6. THE GUARDRAILS — what Q3 does **not** require

**This section exists so the manager test does not become a list of virtues.** Every item
below is a place where the corpus explicitly refuses a criterion a careful analyst would
otherwise apply. Six independent statements across five decades and three source classes.

## 6.1 A great manager cannot make a bad business good

> "**a textile company that allocates capital brilliantly within its industry is a remarkable
> textile company — but not a remarkable business.** … a good managerial record (measured by
> economic returns) is **far more a function of what business boat you get into than it is of
> how effectively you row**." **[E2-37]**

> "**Good jockeys will do well on good horses, but not on broken-down nags.** Both
> Berkshire's textile business and Hochschild, Kohn had able and honest people running them…
> **they were never going to make any progress while running in quicksand.**" **[E2-38]**

> "when a management with a reputation for brilliance tackles a business with a reputation
> for bad economics, **it is the reputation of the business that remains intact**." **[E2-17]**

Munger states it as a ranking rule: *"averaged out, **betting on the quality of a business is
better than betting on the quality of management**. In other words, if you have to choose
one, bet on the business momentum, not the brilliance of the manager."* **[E3-39]**

**Consequence: a strong Q3 can never promote a name.** It cannot repair Q2, and it cannot
substitute for Q4. Q3 can only stop a run or let it continue.

### 6.1a … and the reverse question, answered precisely

*"But surely a bad manager can wreck a wonderful business?"* — **not through incompetence.**
The corpus denies that directly and repeatedly:

> "**The really great business is one that doesn't require good management.** … And the poor
> business is one that can only succeed, or even survive, with great management."
> — Buffett, 1996 meeting, answering *"how do you decide what good management is"*

> "**if it won't stand a little mismanagement it's not much of a business.** Of course, you
> prefer a business that will prosper pretty well, even if it's not managed very well.
> **We're not looking for mismanagement. We like the capacity to stand it, if we stumble into
> it.**" — **[E5-18]**, Munger, 2009 meeting

Tolerating a weak manager is part of what *makes* a business great. **Two vectors do the
damage instead, and neither is operating skill:**

**(i) Loss of focus — capital misallocation and neglect of the base business.** Their own
stated biggest worry about outstanding businesses:

> "A far more serious problem occurs when the management of a great company **gets
> sidetracked and neglects its wonderful base business while purchasing other businesses that
> are so-so or worse** … (Would you believe that a few decades back they were **growing shrimp
> at Coke and exploring for oil at Gillette**?) **Loss of focus is what most worries Charlie
> and me when we contemplate investing in businesses that in general look outstanding.** All
> too often, we've seen value stagnate in the presence of **hubris or of boredom** that caused
> the attention of managers to wander." — **[E3-40]**, 1996 letter

**(ii) Integrity** — *"no matter how attractive the prospects of their business"* **[E2-31]**.

**So test the manager against the base business and the cash, not against operating polish.**
The question is never *are they impressive*; it is *are they wandering, and are they honest*.

**And the corpus gives the remedy, not just the diagnosis.** Asked what to do when a business
has a durable advantage but management starts doing unintelligent things — the transcript's
own section header is *"Can a good business make up for bad management?"*:

> **BUFFETT:** "the history that Charlie and I have had of persuading decent, intelligent
> people, who we thought were doing unintelligent things, to change their course of action has
> been poor. Would you agree with that, Charlie, or no?"
> **MUNGER:** "**Worse than poor.**"
> **BUFFETT:** "if you really think you're in with people that have got a good business, but
> **they're going to keep doing dumb things with your money, you'll probably do better to get
> out** and get in with people who've got a good business and you think they're going to do
> sensible things with it." — **[E4-24]**, 2001 meeting

**A loss-of-focus finding is therefore a Q6 exit trigger, not an engagement plan.** The
corpus rates its own record at persuading managements as *worse than poor*.

## 6.2 "Requires a great manager" is a **Q2** finding, not a Q3 finding

> "**if a business requires a superstar to produce great results, the business itself cannot
> be deemed great.** A medical partnership led by your area's premier brain surgeon may enjoy
> outsized and growing earnings, but that tells little about its future. The partnership's
> moat will go when the surgeon goes. You can count, though, on the moat of the Mayo Clinic
> to endure, **even though you can't name its CEO**." **[E4-23]**

Buffett wrote that inside the *durability* criterion, not the management section. **Record
key-person dependence at Q2 as a moat defect.** A run that records it at Q3 will read a
warning about the business as a compliment to the person.

## 6.3 The exception, and its exact boundary

A great manager **does** justify action in one named case, and Buffett worked it against his
own rule using GEICO:

> "It certainly is true that managerial brilliance was needed for its resuscitation… **But it
> also is true that the fundamental business advantage that GEICO had enjoyed… was still
> intact within the company**, although submerged in a sea of financial and operating
> troubles." **[E2-35]**

> "extraordinary business franchises with a **localized excisable cancer** (needing, to be
> sure, a skilled surgeon), should be distinguished from the true 'turnaround' situation in
> which the managers expect — and need — to pull off a **corporate Pygmalion**." **[E2-36]**

**The test:** is the moat intact and the damage excisable, or **is the manager the plan**?
The first is buyable. The second is not — and Munger's version concedes the exception is
rare: *"very rarely, you find a manager who's so good that you're wise to follow him into
what looks like a mediocre business."* **[E3-39]**

## 6.4 Not required, stated explicitly

| not required | the corpus's words |
|---|---|
| **Meeting management** | *"What's the sense of talking to management? … if you talk to management of almost every company, they'll say they think their stock is a wonderful buy"* (2008 mtg). Most of a quarter's purchases: *"I never met the management, never talked to them."* |
| **Due diligence checklists** | *"We have not found a due diligence list that gets at what we think are the real risks."* Munger: *"we have lower due diligence expenses than anybody else in America — and… we have had less trouble because we had less expense."* |
| **Credentials or schooling** | *"I never look at where a candidate has gone to school. **Never!**"* (2024 letter). Ajit Jain hired with *"None"* insurance experience. Ben Rosner: fourth grade. Mrs. B: no schooling at all. |
| **High IQ** | *"we see very smart people do very stupid things."* Munger: *"it's great to have a manager with a 160 IQ — unless he thinks it's 180."* |
| **Low pay** | *"if they take nothing and they're the wrong CEO, they're still a disaster. **So low pay itself is not the criteria.**"* (2006 mtg) |
| **A clean compensation scheme** | *"There are more problems with having the wrong manager than with having the wrong compensation system… any compensation sins are generally of minor importance compared to the sin of having somebody that's mediocre running a huge company."* (2007 mtg) |
| **Governance checkboxes** | *"they have little checklists and they can issue grades and all that. It isn't going to do anything."* The "independent director" is deflated at length; Munger: *"He's 'independent' the way a slave is independent."* |
| **Absence of a corporate jet** | Munger: *"we're solidly in favor of private jets."* Explicitly refused as a proxy. |
| **A formula** | *"I wish I could give you some precise formulas, but there aren't any."* |

## 6.5 The hard case is mediocrity, not dishonesty

> "The real problem that you can face, and may often face, is when you're dealing with
> **mediocrity**… In business, the tough part is to get rid of something a notch or two above
> mediocrity, but not the best one that could be found." (2005 mtg)

And the admission that judging a successor inside a good business is close to impossible:
*"if they don't show up at the office, it'll keep working for a long time. And maybe that
lack of useful input may show itself in five years… I've been on the board of 20 companies:
It's very hard."* **A Q3 that only catches crooks catches the rarer problem.**

# 7. THE METHOD — how to actually run it

1. **Declare the weight case first** (§1). Everything downstream depends on it.
2. **Overweight the record; underweight the presentation.** Munger, on hiring: *"The correct
   antidote to this sort of folly is to **underweigh face-to-face impressions and overweigh
   the applicant's past record**."* The written analogue: weight the cash-flow statement over
   the MD&A. What they **did** with cash outranks what they **said** about it.
3. **Read the filing, not the tags.** *"there are no answers in the financial statements.
   There are guidelines to enable you to figure out the answer. And to figure out that
   answer, you have to understand something about the business."* **[E3-27]** Tagged data is
   transcription and screening; no name reaches Q5 on XBRL alone. Record the document, date
   and accession number, and cross-check one figure against the filed statement.
4. **Read the competitor's report too** — mandatory, not optional **[E3-28]**.
5. **Balance sheets over an 8-or-10-year window first [E5-27], then the §3 income series
   (multi-year, never a snapshot).** *(Corrected 2026-08-28: the prior wording transposed
   the corpus's balance-sheet window onto the income series.)*
6. **Check the four flags** (§4) and follow every one that fires into the actual text.
7. **Ask the ownership question.** *"What would you do different if this was a hundred percent
   owned by you and your family?"* — a list means the answers are being made for someone else.
8. **Watch behaviour in the bad year, and cost discipline in the record year** **[E3-37]**.
9. **Write the verdict as IN · OUT · UNRESEARCHED · UNKNOWABLE**, and on any non-IN verdict
   ask aloud: *can I name the document that would resolve this?* Manager questions resolve to
   documents more often than they feel like they do — proxy statements, the litigation
   history, the eight-year filing series. **Name the document or close the file.**

|---|---|

| **GEICO 1976** | The exception, and its boundary: intact franchise, excisable damage **[E2-35, E2-36]**. |
| **Berkshire textiles** | A manager Buffett admired, in a business that could not be saved. *"one of the reasons I stuck with the textile business as long as I did was that I liked Ken Chase so much."* Affection for a manager as a **cost**, not a criterion. |
| **Citigroup 2007** | Fails Q3 on its own filed Legal Proceedings — Enron $2.01bn settled 2005-06-10, Adelphia $250M, WorldCom, Parmalat, twelve named actions. Worked in full at `Framework/v4/VERIFICATION - …`. |

# 9. WHAT THIS STANDARD CANNOT DO

Stated plainly, because a test that cannot fail is not a test.

- **It cannot detect a competent fraud.** **[E5-17]**, and Royal Dutch.
- **It cannot detect intent.** Munger's gallbladder surgeon believed he was helping his
  patients: *"in lesser strength, the cognitive drift of that surgeon is present in every
  profession and in every human being."* A test that hunts for a guilty mind finds nothing.
  **Every disqualifier above is behavioural for this reason.**
- **It cannot rank managers in advance of a record.** *"we cheat. We buy businesses with good
  managers."* Track record, or no verdict.
- **It cannot promote a name.** §6.1. Q3 stops runs; it never starts them.
- **It has no tooling.** The attempt was built, tested on ten companies and rejected the same
  day — see `Framework/v4/TOOL TEST - integrity check, NOT ADMITTED.md`. §4 is the corrected
  design brief for the next attempt: read accounting and disclosure choices, not Item 3.

**A Q3 verdict of IN means: I read the filed record, I ran the four flags, I ran the primary
test as a series, and I found no disqualifier. It does not mean the managers are honest.**
