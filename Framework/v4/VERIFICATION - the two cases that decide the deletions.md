# VERIFICATION — the two cases that decide v4's deletions
**2026-08-26.** `INVENTIONS - deleted and why.md` recorded two debts. Both are now paid,
and both came out in favour of the deletion. Neither was a foregone conclusion.

---

# CASE 1 — CITIGROUP, mid-2007, without the leverage ceiling

**The question.** The 10:1 assets/equity ceiling was the framework's best-performing
mechanical rule: it rejected Citigroup automatically. v4 deleted it because it cited a
ledger row that does not contain its number. **Does v4 still reject Citigroup — and if so,
where?**

**The evidence.** Citigroup's FY2006 Form 10-K, filed **2007-02-23**, accession
`0001193125-07-038505` — the annual report a mid-2007 analyst would have had. Retrieved
in full from EDGAR; the Legal Proceedings section is present and was read.

## Finding 1 — the corpus's own number would NOT have caught Citigroup

From the filed balance sheet at 2006-12-31:

| | |
|---|---|
| Total assets | **$1,884,318M** |
| Total stockholders' equity | **$119,783M** |
| **Assets ÷ equity** | **15.7 : 1** |

The corpus observes that *"assets are twenty times equity — a common ratio in this
industry"* **[E3-29]**. **At 15.7:1 Citigroup was not merely under twenty, it was
unremarkable by the corpus's own description of a normal bank.**

So the choice was never "our 10:1 versus Buffett's 20:1." **Had v4 adopted the corpus
figure as a threshold, it would have waved Citigroup straight through.** The old rule
worked only because 10 is an arbitrary number that happened to sit below Citigroup's
actual leverage. That is curve-fitting, not a principle, and it is exactly what the
deletion was for.

## Finding 2 — v4 rejects Citigroup one gate EARLIER, on the company's own disclosure

Q3 tests honesty as binary, permanent, filings-based, and **dated to when each matter
became public**. Citigroup's own Legal Proceedings section, in the filing above, discloses:

- **Enron.** *"Citigroup agreed to settle this action on June 10, 2005… Citigroup will make
  a pretax payment of **$2.01 billion** to the settlement class"* — approved by the District
  Court **2006-05-24**.
- **Adelphia.** *"CGMI and numerous other financial institution defendants settled… for a
  total of **$250 million**."*
- **WorldCom**, **Parmalat**, and the **research-analyst** matters, all disclosed in the
  same section.
- Twelve distinct named securities actions, including *LOS ANGELES CITY EMPLOYEES
  RETIREMENT ASSOCIATION v. CITIGROUP* and *CARROLL v. WEILL*.

Every one of these was public **before mid-2007**, and every one is in the company's own
annual report. Under Q3 this is not a close call.

## Verdict

**v4 rejects Citigroup at Q3. The run stops there and never reaches the balance sheet.**

This is a better outcome than the rule it replaces, on three counts:
1. It fires **one question earlier**, on conduct rather than on a ratio.
2. It rests on the company's **own filed disclosure**, not on a threshold we chose.
3. The corpus's actual number **would have failed to catch it** — so the deletion removed
   a rule that was right by accident and replaced it with one that is right by evidence.

**The ceiling is deleted and there is no hole where it used to be.**

# CASE 2 — COCA-COLA, 1988, without the Coca-Cola clause

**The question.** The invented clause let a generational moat pass at 70% of the hurdle.
It was the only thing keeping the wonderful-business-at-a-fair-price path open. **Without
it, can v4 still buy the purchase the corpus is proudest of?**

**The arithmetic.** ~15× earnings — a **6.7%** earnings yield — against a long bond of
**~9%**.

**On a bare static yield it fails by −2.3 points.** That is the v3.x result, and it is
precisely why the clause had to be invented: the static test rejected Coca-Cola, so a
patch was written to un-reject it.

**Under v4, the decision number is an IRR — growth converted into a rate [E4-21]:**

| growth | points over the 9% bond | |
|---|---|---|
| 6% | **+1.41** | clears |
| 8% | **+2.04** | clears |
| 10% | **+2.70** | clears |
| 12% | **+3.39** | clears |
| 15% | **+4.47** | clears |

**It clears at every growth rate tested**, including rates far below what Coca-Cola
actually delivered. And the growth the 1988 price already assumed was **1.2%** — the
market was pricing Coca-Cola for almost no growth at all.

## Verdict

**The Coca-Cola clause was never needed. It was compensating for a defect somewhere else.**

The defect was the static yield test. Once the question becomes "what does this pay me,
compared with the bond and with everything else available," a wonderful business at a fair
price clears on its own merits, with no special pleading.

**This also closes the oldest open question in the project.** `CLAUDE.md` had carried, since
2026-08-01, the unresolved note that *"Book One is a static yield test and is therefore
structurally hostile to compounders."* It was, and the fix was never a clause for
exceptional companies — it was to stop using a static test. Coca-Cola 1988 is the worked
case that proves it.

# WHAT BOTH CASES HAVE IN COMMON

Each invented rule was **a patch for a defect elsewhere in the framework**, not a rule in
its own right:

- The 10:1 ceiling patched a Q3 that was not being applied hard enough on filed conduct.
- The Coca-Cola clause patched a valuation test that could not see growth.

Removing the patch and fixing the defect leaves the framework **shorter and stronger in
both cases**. That is the general lesson for anything else on the deleted list: before
mourning a rule, check whether it was covering for something.
