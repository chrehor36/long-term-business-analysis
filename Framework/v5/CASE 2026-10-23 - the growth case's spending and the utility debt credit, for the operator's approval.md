## Gap 1: what happens to the growth spending in the growth case
**What the audit found.** Specific (e) charges "the growth spending against the growth it buys" and never says what the
spending does over time. The runs of 2026-10-18 to 2026-10-23 did three things: held it flat for ever (ED), held it flat
and stopped it at year ten (D, DTE, ETR, EVRG), grew it with the growth (ES, FTS), and in two runs charged none (DUK,
ELPC). The fair price moves 2% to 9% between the readings.

**What the rows say.**
- Growth is bought by spending above depreciation, and is judged by what that spending earns: "So we’re putting $130
  million above depreciation into that business. Now that can be good or bad. I mean, it’s growth." and "whether that’s
  good or bad depends on what we earn on that incremental $130 million over time" **[M2001-019]**; the second-best business
  "takes more money, but the rate at which you invest — reinvest — the money to get that growth is a very satisfactory
  rate." **[M1998-081]**.
- At a steady return, earnings grow only as fast as the capital put in: "we’re not earning a higher rate of return on
  capital than we were when we started. We just put way more capital into the business" **[M2023-081]**. So a growth rate
  held for ten years at a steady return needs spending that grows at the same rate.
- The spending has to come back as cash: "at some day, a financial asset has to give you back cash to justify you laying
  out cash for it now." **[M1998-082]**; and growth above the discount rate cannot last, "it shouldn’t work for an extended
  period of time" **[M1997-095]**.
- Q7 already stops the spending with the growth for purchases, "a business that buys no growth spends nothing to buy it"
  (specific (c), 2026-10-10).

**Proposed reading** (CONVENTION, ours in method; the principle is the rows'). In the growth case, the growth spending
(capital spending above the maintenance guess, as the window shows it, after any regulated-utility debt credit) grows at
the growth rate carried for the ten years, so that the return on the added capital stays where the window shows it
**[M2023-081]**, **[M2001-019]**; after year ten, when growth stops, it stops, and owner cash is carried at no growth after
maintenance only, as for purchases under (c). The maintenance guess is charged in every year of every case. The no-growth
case charges maintenance only. A run states the growth spending it charged in year one and year ten.

*Alternatives the operator may prefer:* (b) held flat for ten years, then stopped (D's reading): simpler, and it credits
the growth slightly more generously than the rows' steady-return picture; (c) held flat for ever (ED's): rejected, since it
charges spending for growth after the growth has stopped.

## Gap 2: the regulated-utility debt credit
**What the audit found.** "The regulated utility" credits "the long-term debt raised over the window to fund the fixed
assets [...] against the capital spending it funded, up to the debt share of the capital structure the regulator allows".
Runs read three parts of that differently:
1. *Which debt:* the rise in net debt with cash and commercial paper (BKH, ETR) or the rise in long-term debt (the rest).
2. *Against which spending:* all capital spending, maintenance included (HE, KEP, ES), or only the spending above
   maintenance (IDA, MGEE, EVRG, ETR, FTS).
3. *Whose debt share:* a non-deciding subsidiary's (ES used PSNH), a rate-base weighting across every utility (FTS, ETR), the
   deciding part's own (IDA, MGEE), or the consolidated share (EVRG, FE).

**What the rows say.**
- The exception is for long-term debt dedicated to fixed assets: "debt dedicated [...] to the fixed-asset commitments at our
  regulated utilities" **[L2017-012]**; the assets are "funded by large amounts of long-term debt" **[L2010-012]**; "Here, we
  will favor long-term, fixed-rate loans." **[R2009-001]**. Commercial paper and cash fund the working capital, not the
  plant.
- Depreciation is a real cost the equity bears: "depreciation is an economic cost every bit as real as wages, materials, or
  taxes" **[R1996-023]**; "Depreciation charges are a more complicated subject but are almost always true costs."
  **[L2015-004]**; of the return on the added capital, "It’s measured by the operating earnings after depreciation." **[M2014-086]**.
  The maintenance guess is at least the depreciation charge, and the growth is what is spent beyond it: "Every year we
  spend amounts equal to our depreciation charge simply to stay in the same economic place [...] then spend additional
  sums to grow." **[L2000-035]**. What new debt funds is the growth of the plant, the spending "above depreciation"
  **[M2001-019]**.
- The business is judged by its deciding part (Q2, a business of parts), and "The regulated utility" ties the basis to that
  part's allowed return **[M2010-037]**.

**Proposed reading** (CONVENTION, ours in method; the exception is **[L2017-012]**'s).
1. *The debt credited* is the increase over the window in long-term debt (borrowings due after a year, current maturities
   of them, and finance leases), averaged per year; commercial paper, other short-term borrowing and cash are left out.
   Securitization bonds repaid from a dedicated customer charge are left out with their charge, and the run says so.
2. *It is credited against the growth spending only*: capital spending above the maintenance guess. The maintenance guess
   is charged in full to the equity in every case.
3. *The cap* is the allowed debt share of the deciding part, as the filing states it; where the deciding part is several
   regulated companies, their allowed shares weighted by rate base where the filing states both; where the filing states
   an allowed share for less than half of the deciding part's rate base, the business's own long-term debt share of
   long-term capital (long-term debt over long-term debt plus equity) at the latest filed date. A subsidiary outside the
   deciding part never sets the cap.
4. In the growth case the credit grows with the growth spending and stops with it (gap 1).
