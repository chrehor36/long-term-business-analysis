## Q11 — HAVING BOUGHT: HAS THE BUSINESS CHANGED, OR ONLY ITS PRICE? WEIGHING.

The question is asked of something already owned. The rows split what happened since the purchase into two things, what the quotation did and what the business did, and only the second is a reason to act.

> "But we continue to think that it is **usually foolish to part with an interest in a business that is both understandable and durably wonderful.** Business interests of that kind are **simply too hard to replace.** [...] An investor should ordinarily hold a small piece of an outstanding business **with the same tenacity that an owner would exhibit if he owned all of that business.**" — **[L1993-014]**, 1993 letter

> "if we **lose confidence in the management**, if we **lose confidence in the durability of the competitive advantage**, if we **recognize we made a mistake** when we went into it — we sell plenty of times. So it’s not unheard of. On the other hand, if you really get a wonderful business with outstanding management — but mostly the wonderful business part of it — **when in doubt, keep holding. But it’s no inviolable rule.**" — **[M2009-040]**, 2009 annual meeting, AM

> "But the truth is, if you owned the businesses you liked prior to the virus arriving — **it changes prices** and it changes — but **nobody’s forcing you to sell.** And if you really like the business, and you like the management you’re in with, and **the business hasn’t fundamentally changed** —" — **[M2020-008]**, 2020 annual meeting, AM

The default is to keep. "the real thing to do with a great business is just hang on for dear life." **[M1998-012]**; "the best thing to do is buy a stock that you don’t ever want to sell." **[M1998-011]**. The holding is judged as the businesses are judged, by two tests the 2007 letter names: "The first test is improvement in earnings, with our making due allowance for industry conditions. The second test, more subjective, is whether their "moats" [...] have widened during the year." **[L2007-016]**. Munger states the same in 2009: "I wouldn’t get too excited about the fact that the stock goes down. [...] What matters are things like this" **[M2009-026]**.

### The three reasons the rows give for selling

M2009-040 names three, and the rows around it supply the cases.

**1. Confidence in the management is lost.** The Tesco sale is the worked case, and its lesson is speed: "In 2013, I soured somewhat on the company's then-management and sold 114 million shares [...] My leisurely pace in making sales would prove expensive. [...] You see a cockroach in your kitchen; as the days go by, you meet his relatives." **[L2014-003]**

**2. The durability of the advantage is in doubt.** "So now we sell — really when we think that we’ve — when we’re reevaluating the economic characteristics of the business. [...] But we probably don’t think that their competitive advantage is as strong as we might have thought" **[M2002-004]**. The horizon is the purchase horizon: "we will occasionally reevaluate the economic characteristics that we see 10 years out from the ones that we saw 10 years ago" **[M2000-054]**. The change may be in the industry, not the company: the reinsurers were sold as "a mildly negative judgment on the reinsurance business", "not a negative judgment on their managements" **[M2016-008]**, and the airlines because "I think it changed in a very major way." **[M2020-017]**

**3. The purchase is recognised as a mistake.** "I just decided that I’d made a mistake in evaluating" **[M2020-017]**. The cost of delay is named in the 2008 letter: "sucking my thumb when new facts came in that should have caused me to re-examine my thinking and promptly take action." **[L2008-003]**. Munger quotes Keynes for the licence: "we would reserve the right to change our mind." **[M1994-020]**

**The better use of the money**, where the rows say so. "So you sell if you need money for something else." **[M1998-012]**; "the ideal way is when you found something you like immensely better." **[M1998-013]**; "If we can find a good business to buy, you know, we will sell the least attractive." **[M2007-107]**. Buffett ties this reason to an era of more ideas than money: "Forty years ago my sales were all because I found something that I liked even better. [...] Now I’ve got more money than ideas, and that’s a different equation." **[M2002-004]** (also **[M2000-055]**, **[M2007-104]**). The row's own note records a fourth reason of this kind added to M2009-040 later in the same answer; it is not rowed separately.

**For a controlled business the list is narrower.** "we make only two exceptions: when they promise to start losing money indefinitely or if we have major labor problems." **[M2009-041]** (also **[M2005-093]**, **[M2020-028]**). The forward test is capital: "we will not fund a company that — where we think that it’s going to chew up money in the future." **[M2020-026]**; "you do not want to try and fix something that’s unfixable." **[M2016-031]**. Liking the manager is named as the trap: "if he’d been a jerk, you know, we’d have quit the textile business much faster and (laughs) we’d have been better off." **[M2024-031]**

When the decision is made, it is whole: "when we change our mind, we don’t take half measure or anything of the sort." **[M2020-020]**

**A reading, not a row.** The three reasons are the purchase questions asked again of a holding with the price taken out: the management (Q5), the castle (Q2), and the original analysis (Q1 and Q7). The rows do not say this; it is the drafter's arrangement, and it is offered because it keeps Q11 from becoming a separate checklist. The better use of the money is the one reason that brings price back in, and it does so through the comparison of Q8, not through the quotation of the holding.

### Price alone is not a reason

> "one of the important things in stocks is that **the stock does not know that you own it.** [...] All these feelings, and it has **no impact whatsoever.**" — **[M1995-022]**, 1995 annual meeting, AM

A fall is a reason to buy more if anything: "if it goes down 25 percent in price or 30 percent in price, if you have more money available, buy more. And if you don’t, you know, so what? [...] Just look at the business and judge how it’s doing." **[M1997-144]**. "if the stock goes down 20 or 30 or 40 percent, we don’t feel we’re poor in that situation." **[M2020-018]**. "Selling fine businesses on "scary" news is usually a bad decision." **[L1996-009]**. A gain is no reason either: a large unrealized gain "doesn’t have anything to do with our decision-making." **[M2018-014]**; selling dominant winners is "akin to suggesting that the Bulls trade Michael Jordan because he has become so important to the team." **[L1996-025]**. Selling to rebuy lower is ruled out: "You have to make two decisions right." **[M1999-079]** (also **[M1996-097]**). A full price stops adding, not holding: "We don’t find them uncomfortable, in the sense that we want to sell them." **[M1998-160]**; "If the choice is between a questionable business at a comfortable price or a comfortable business at a questionable price, we much prefer the latter." **[L1999-021]**. Selling for tax is called "a dumb decision." **[M2023-035]**

**The qualifications.** The same speakers say a silly price is a reason: "if they get to a silly price, you probably should sell them." **[M2007-089]**; "if you ever attend a meeting here, and there are 60 or 70 times earnings, keep an eye on me." **[M1996-095]**. They call not selling in the bubble a mistake: "I made a big mistake in not selling several of our larger holdings during The Great Bubble." **[L2003-021]**; "I talked when I should have walked." **[L2004-011]**; Coca-Cola "at 50 times earnings, it was a silly price on the stock." **[M2006-029]**. PetroChina was sold when "we no longer felt it was undervalued compared to other oil companies" **[M2008-114]**. And M2009-040 closes on "no inviolable rule." The rows give no multiple at which a price becomes silly; the "60 or 70 times" and "50 times" are remarks about two cases, not a stated rule, and this draft writes no threshold.

### Grading a threat

> "you have to be thinking all the time and **alert to whether there’s been something that really changes the game** in a big way. And that’s not only true for American Express, that’s true for other things we own, including things we own 100 percent of. And **we’ll be wrong sometimes. We’ll be late sometimes** [...] Assessing the probabilities of those threats being **a minor problem, or a major problem, or a life-threatening problem**, you know, it’s a tough game" — **[M2016-081]**, 2016 annual meeting, PM

The rows show each grade in a case. **Minor**: Walmart's bribery scandal, "I don’t think the earning power of Walmart five years from now will be materially affected" **[M2012-051]**; and Munger's warning against reacting to slow decline, "we can’t change — make a portfolio change — every time something is a little less advantaged than it used to be." **[M2016-080]**. **Major, held through**: the self-driving car "is a real threat to the auto insurance industry. [...] it would not cause us to be thinking for one second about selling GEICO." **[M2014-048]**. **Life-threatening**: the Baltimore department store, "if we’d kept it, we would have gone out of business." **[M2016-031]**; "those businesses will wither and die. And we’d better use the money someplace else." **[M2019-012]**. The rows warn that the slow threat is the hard one to grade: "slow change can be much harder to perceive, and can lull you to sleep easier" **[M2014-038]**. The rows give the three grades but no criteria for sorting a threat into one of them; the grading is the "tough game" itself.

### The holding the speakers would not sell at any price

> "**regardless of price, we have no interest at all in selling any good businesses that Berkshire owns**, and are very reluctant to sell sub-par businesses as long as we expect them to generate at least some cash and as long as we feel good about their managers and labor relations." — **[R1995-011]**, 1995 annual report, owner-related business principles

"we don’t break off the relationships that we develop simply because we get offered a fancy price for something." **[M2000-052]**; "we would never, never sell an operation like that, ever." **[M2022-015]**. The cost is named and accepted: "We don’t see any reason to make an extra half a percent a year or 1 percent a year" **[M1995-044]**; "this mindset that may produce slightly suboptimal returns because of our attitude" **[M2005-094]**.

**The qualification.** The promise covers controlled businesses, not stocks: "we regard any marketable security as available for sale, however unlikely such a sale now seems." **[L2016-010]** (also **[R2016-001]**); "at some price, you have to be willing to sell something that’s a marketable security, forgetting about a controlled business." **[M1996-095]**. Even the controlled business has the two exceptions above **[M2009-041]**, and the proviso that "we’re not going to have to put money in incessantly" **[M2005-094]**.

### Why it is a WEIGHING

No single finding forces a sale. Keeping is "no inviolable rule" **[M2009-040]**; threats are graded on "probabilities" in "a tough game" **[M2016-081]**; the moat test is "more subjective" **[L2007-016]**; giving up a business is among "the hard decisions" **[M2022-064]**. The weighing is not even: "when in doubt, keep holding" **[M2009-040]** puts the burden on the reason to sell, and the narrated mistakes run mostly one way, the wonderful business sold too soon (GEICO **[L1995-012]**, Disney **[M1998-100]**, McDonald's **[L1998-019]**, Cap Cities **[L1995-032]**), with the bubble holdings **[L2003-021]** and the late textile and Tesco exits **[M2024-031]**, **[L2014-003]** on the other side. The one near-STOP is on further money, not on the sale: "we will not knowingly throw good money after bad." **[L2023-010]** (also **[M2020-026]**, **[M2024-010]**).

## Q12 (optional) — WOULD WE BE PROUD OF HOW THE MONEY IS MADE? STOP for named businesses, otherwise WEIGHING.

> "not only do they behave in a way that conforms with the laws, but that they behave in a way where, **if a story were written by an unfriendly but intelligent reporter, the next morning, in their local paper, they would have no problem with their neighbors, their family reading it.**" — **[M2008-011]**, 2008 annual meeting, AM

> "It’s deeply wrong. **We don’t want to make our money selling things that are bad for people.**" — **[M2021-059]**, 2021 annual meeting, PM

> "And we’ve **turned down businesses, including a big tobacco business.** [...] **We want to have people think of us as having won fairly and used wisely. It works.**" — **[M2016-064]**, 2016 annual meeting, PM

The question is optional because its rows are late (2007 to 2025 in the merged count, with seven earlier moral-line rows inside B's owner theme) and because no slice placed it in a purchase ordering. Its weight in the rows is high all the same: "We care more about that than business mistakes, way more." **[M2010-033]**; "we won’t trade reputation for money." **[M2010-032]**

### The newspaper test and the businesses named

The law is the floor, not the test: "every business ought to decline a lot of business that’s perfectly legal and proper to accept." **[M2010-007]**; "we’ve got a huge area of things which is perfectly legal to do, that we think beneath us." **[M2004-076]**. Two further tests sit beside the newspaper: industry custom is no excuse, "the one reason you can’t give at Berkshire [...] is that everybody else is doing it." **[M2009-013]** (also **[M2017-052]**); and nearness to the line is itself the answer, "the very fact that they think it’s that close to the line probably tells them it’s over the line." **[M2010-034]**

The businesses named, each with its row:
- **Casinos.** "the gambling casinos use clever psychological tricks to cause people to hurt themselves. [...] It’s a dirty business, and I don’t think you’ll find a casino soon in Berkshire Hathaway." **[M2007-018]**
- **Tobacco, whole.** The refusal is told twice. "probably one of the best businesses I’ve ever seen, in terms of the economics of it. [...] we sat down in the lobby and just decided that we didn’t want to be in that business." **[M2005-097]**; "“This is probably the best business we’ve ever seen.” [...] And we decided not to do it." **[M2021-013]**; and **[M2016-064]** above.
- **Distributor-loading schemes.** The test is "whether a direct marketing operation is really based on selling product to would-be distributors of one sort, and loading them up, instead of [...] selling it to end users." **[M2013-015]**; with Munger's rider, "there’s likely to be more flimflam in selling magic potions than pots and pans." **[M2013-016]**
- **Gambling dressed as investing.** **[M2021-059]** above, of trading apps; and bitcoin, "in my life I try and avoid things that are stupid and evil and make me look bad in comparison with somebody else. And bitcoin does all three." **[M2022-078]**
- **Promotion on a borrowed reputation.** "we could attract a ton of money, and we could get a big overwrite on it. But it’s not our game." **[M2015-062]**

### Where the line is imperfect

> "there’s something about every business that, if you knew it, you wouldn’t like. [...] if you expect perfection, you know, in your spouse or in your friends or in companies, you’re not going to find it." — **[M2021-012]**, 2021 annual meeting, AM

Against it, in the same answer, the declined tobacco business, "it’s a very tough thing to decide whether you get in or out of a business." **[M2021-013]**. The speakers draw the line at control and doubt it: "we would not have trouble owning stock in a cigarette company. We wouldn’t want to manufacture cigarettes" with "I’m not sure that the logic is perfect on that" **[M2005-096]** (also **[M2004-075]**); "Is that the correct moral line to draw? I don’t know." **[M2007-139]**; "we don’t necessarily draw it perfectly" **[M2018-026]**; "You can draw these lines where you wish." **[M2004-076]**. M2021-059's own concept line extends the rule to investing in such sellers, against the 2005 willingness to own the cigarette stock. Where perfection is not demanded and where a business is refused although it is the best seen is not settled by the rows. [OPEN]

### STOP or WEIGHING

**STOP for the named businesses when the whole is to be bought and run**: casinos **[M2007-018]**, tobacco **[M2005-097]**, **[M2016-064]**, **[M2021-013]**, loading schemes **[M2013-015]**. The refusals are stated as done, not weighed against price; the tobacco business was refused as "one of the best businesses" seen **[M2005-097]**. **WEIGHING otherwise**, and for a marketable stake: the rows own as stock what they would not run **[M2004-075]**, **[M2005-096]**, call the line imperfect **[M2021-012]**, and give the newspaper test as the instrument **[M2008-011]**. The question is a filter on how the money is made, not on the managers' honesty, which Q5 carries.

## A CLOSING NOTE ON THE INVESTOR

This is not a question. No slice treats these rows as a filter on businesses: C's map says of its theme "Not a filter on businesses", D's says "not a business filter", and the merge says of M10 that "it governs the analyst". They are carried here as the speakers' advice to the person doing the work, grouped as the rows group themselves, with the strongest rows of each. Temperament also appears in the foundations, as a condition of the activity; it appears here as conduct, the habits that keep it. The rows join the two: the record is put down not to brilliance but to having "done fewer dumb things than most people" **[M1999-109]**, and "It’s not a business that requires extraordinary intellect. It does require extraordinary discipline." **[M2004-035]**. The M18 rows all fall in 2007 to 2025 (D's map: heaviest in the closing question periods of 2023 to 2025), and many are addressed to students and the young (**[M2008-092]**, **[M2008-093]**, **[M2017-098]**), so they speak to a life, not to a portfolio; Munger says as much when he calls wealth from "passive holding of little bits of paper" alone "a failed life" **[M2001-022]**.

**Invest in yourself.**
> "I think that **the most important investment you can make is in yourself.** I mean, very, very, very few people get anything like their potential horsepower translated into the actual horsepower of their output in life." — **[M2008-092]**, 2008 annual meeting, PM

> "whatever abilities you have **can’t be taken away from you, they can’t actually be inflated away from you.** [...] So, the best investment by far is anything that develops yourself." — **[M2022-033]**, 2022 annual meeting, AM

The test of it is another's purchase: which classmate would you buy "10 percent of for the rest of their life"? Not the highest IQ, but "the person that’s going to be effective." **[M2008-093]**

**Whom to work with.**
> "you are going to **gravitate toward the behavior of those around you.** I tell people to be sure and **associate with people who are better than you are.**" — **[M2007-108]**, 2007 annual meeting, PM

> "Oh yes, **the toxic people** who are trying to fool you or lie to you or aren’t reliable in meeting their commitments. A great lesson of life is **get them the hell out of your life.** [...] And do it fast." — **[M2023-071]**, 2023 annual meeting, PM

And the work itself: "look for the job that you would take if you didn’t need a job." **[M2017-098]** (also **[L2021-010]**).

**Reputation and honesty as a habit.**
> "**the best way to get what you want is to deserve what you want.**" — **[M1998-170]**, 1998 annual meeting, PM

> "it helps if you conducted yourself in life so that other people trust you. [...] And then it helps even more if **other people are right to trust you.**" — **[M2011-031]**, 2011 annual meeting, AM

"if you make yourself a very reliable person and stay reliable all your life [...] it will be very hard for you to fail at anything you want." **[M2007-115]**. The habits are chosen: "very few of those things, you know, are in your DNA. They are choices. And they are also habits." **[M2002-038]**

**The inner scorecard, and the temperament it keeps.**
> "in the end you want to have **an internal score card.** I mean, you are not a better person or a worse person because you live a different kind of life than your neighbor." — **[M2008-059]**, 2008 annual meeting, AM

> "you do have to have an **emotional stability.** You have to have sort of an **inner peace about your decisions.** [...] You have to be able to **think for yourself.**" — **[M2009-020]**, 2009 annual meeting, AM

Envy is ruled out by name ("we — rule out as envy as part of your repertoire." **[M2008-112]**), and so is anger: "you do not want to add one single iota because you’re angry." **[M2011-008]**

**How to learn.**
> "So you just have to get up each morning and try and **go to bed that night a little wiser** than you were when you got up." — **[M2010-086]**, 2010 annual meeting, PM

> "We try and avoid the worst anchoring effect, which is **always your previous conclusion.** We really try and destroy our previous ideas." — **[M2016-054]**, 2016 annual meeting, PM

> "this **constant study of other people’s disasters** and other people’s errors has helped us enormously" — **[M2012-104]**, 2012 annual meeting, PM

Munger makes it a duty: "I think it’s dishonorable to stay stupider than you have to be" **[M2015-093]**. The postmortem is the learning habit applied to one's own selling: "I contrasted all sale decisions versus all purchase decisions." **[M1999-125]**

**What to avoid.**
> "all the trite stuff is what works. I mean, you **avoid doing the really dumb things** [...] And then you want to develop a good character, and good mental habits, and you want to **learn from your mistakes, every single one**" — **[M2004-124]**, 2004 annual meeting, PM

> "You want to go into a game where you’re **very likely to win without having any unusual luck.**" — **[M2023-069]**, 2023 annual meeting, PM

"You don't have to make it back the way that you lost it." **[L1994-036]**; "if I had one piece of advice to give to young people [...] it would be just to don’t get in debt." **[M2004-125]**; "Figure out what you’re bad at and avoid all of it." **[M2022-080]**

### Steering noticed

The auto-loaded CLAUDE.md names a sixth question, "WHAT WOULD PROVE ME WRONG, AND WHEN DO I SELL?", and a holdings framework with named outcomes, and the pull was strongest in Q11. Three places. First, I was tempted to turn M2009-040's three reasons into a list to be written at purchase, which is the six's shape; the rows state them after the fact and close on "no inviolable rule", so they stay as after-purchase reasons, and the ABSENT finding says so. Second, the threat grades of M2016-081 invited a kill-criterion reading, a pre-set line past which one sells; the row gives grades and no criteria, and I wrote none. Third, I reached for outcome words (hold, review, sell) as the verdicts of Q11; the rows give no named outcomes for a holding, so none are written here. The search for a pre-commitment was framed to find one, so that the absence would be tested rather than assumed; what it found was the commitment to keep (**[L2005-006]**, **[M2005-094]**). In Q12 the six's "ARE THEY HONEST" pulled the newspaper test toward a test of management; the rows aim it at how the money is made and at the owner's own conduct, so it is kept there and the managers' integrity is left to Q5. The sub-section order inside Q11 follows the brief; the speakers do not order these tests.
