## Q7 — WHAT IS IT WORTH: HOW MUCH CASH, HOW SURE, HOW SOON, AT THE LONG GOVERNMENT RATE, HELD AS A RANGE; AND IS THE PRICE SO FAR BELOW IT THAT IT NEEDS NO PENCIL? STOP.

The speakers ask it in Aesop's words, and they name the rate in the same breath.

> "To flesh out this principle, you must answer only three questions. **How certain are you that there are indeed birds in the bush? When will they emerge and how many will there be? What is the risk-free interest rate** (which we consider to be the yield on long-term U.S. bonds)?" — **[L2000-021]**, 2000 letter

> "Now, the question is **how much do you get back, how sure are you of getting it, when do you get it?**" — **[M2009-004]**, 2009 meeting

> "You better just stick with businesses that you can understand, **use the government bond rate**. And when you can buy them — something you understand well — at a significant discount, then, you should start getting excited." — **[M1996-025]**, 1996 meeting

> "If you need to use a computer or a calculator to make the calculation, **you shouldn’t buy it**. I mean, it should be so obvious that you don’t have to carry it out to tenths of a percent or hundredths of the percent. **It should scream at you.**" — **[M2009-005]**, 2009 meeting

Q7 comes after the business and the people: price is named last in most of the speakers' purchase orderings (merged map, section 2), because the moat and the management are inputs to the value **[M1999-104]**. A business that has not cleared Q1 and Q2 cannot reach it; its cash cannot be pictured **[M1994-077]**.

### The definition

Value is the cash the owner can take out over the life of the business, discounted back to today, stated in nearly the same words for three decades.

> "It is the **discounted value of the cash that can be taken out of a business during its remaining life**." — **[R1996-018]**, 1996 annual report

> "Despite its fuzziness, however, intrinsic value is **all-important** and is the only logical way to evaluate the relative attractiveness of investments and businesses." — **[L1994-007]**, 1994 letter

> "But the intrinsic value pure definition would be the cash to be generated between now and Judgment Day, discounted at an interest rate that seems appropriate at the time." — **[M2017-027]**, 2017 meeting

The cash counted is the cash that comes out, net of what must go back in. Buffett gives the business credit only "for whatever net cash is left every year" **[M1998-080]**, and he adds a fourth Aesop question in 2014:

> "the real question is, A) **are you going to have to put more cash into after you buy it?**" — **[M2014-068]**, 2014 meeting

> "Because at some day, a financial asset has to give you back cash to justify you laying out cash for it now." — **[M1998-082]**, 1998 meeting

**The same formula for stocks and businesses, and for every asset.** The letter that sets out the three questions opens by saying so, and the meetings repeat it for farms, oil fields and apartment houses **[M2002-079]**, **[M2003-135]**.

> "Leaving aside tax factors, **the formula we use for evaluating stocks and businesses is identical**." — **[L2000-021]**, 2000 letter

> "It applies to outlays for farms, oil royalties, bonds, stocks, lottery tickets, and manufacturing plants." — **[L2000-022]**, 2000 letter

> "Businesses have coupons that are going to develop in the future, too. The only problem is **they aren’t printed on the instrument**." — **[M1997-050]**, 1997 meeting

The industry matters only as evidence about the cash **[M1998-149]**, and growth is inside the equation, never a separate style **[M2000-001]**:

> "Indeed, **growth can destroy value** if it requires cash inputs in the early years of a project or enterprise that exceed the discounted value of the cash that those assets will generate in later years." — **[L2000-023]**, 2000 letter

**The three questions carry the earlier ones inside them.** "How sure" is where the moat and the people enter **[M1999-104]**; what management will do with retained cash is a third element of the calculation that can add to value or require a discount **[L2010-002]**:

> "The moat and the management are part of the valuation process, in that they enter into our thinking as to **the degree of certainty** that we attribute to the stream of income — stream of cash, actually — that we expect in the future and the amount of it." — **[M1999-104]**, 1999 meeting

In 2019 the bush gains a fourth and fifth question, who will try to take it and what the worst case is:

> "how certain he is that he’s going to get to the bush, you know, and then **who else is going to come and try and take the bush away** [...] And your success in investing depends on how well you were able to figure out how certain that bush is, how far away it is, and **what the worst case is**" — **[M2019-023]**, 2019 meeting

Value comes from what the asset produces. Money expected from what someone else will later pay, "irrespective of what the asset produces", is named speculation **[M2002-078]**; the Frozen Corporation, which can never pay its owners, makes the same point **[M1998-084]**.

### The rate

**The long government bond is the rate, used as a common yardstick.** The rows give two reasons: the bond can always be bought, and one rate lets every opportunity be compared with every other.

> "The risk-free rate is used merely to equate one item to another. [...] So that becomes the yardstick rate. **It doesn’t mean we want to buy government bonds.**" — **[M1997-086]**, 1997 meeting

> "We use the Treasury rate just to get **comparability across time and across companies**." — **[M1999-103]**, 1999 meeting

> "I mean, **the yardstick for me is always the U.S. Treasury**." — **[M2020-006]**, 2020 meeting

**No risk premium is stacked into the rate.** The rows state this most firmly in 1996 and 1998, in the words the merged map flags:

> "But **we don’t put the risk factor in, per se**, because essentially, the purity of the idea is that you’re discounting future cash. And it doesn’t make any difference whether cash comes from a risky business or a safe business — so-called safe business." — **[M1996-024]**, 1996 meeting

> "not try to have the whole panoply with all different kinds of risk rates, because, frankly, we think that’d just be playing games with numbers. [...] I mean, it may look mathematical. But **it’s mathematical gibberish** in my view." — **[M1996-025]**, 1996 meeting

> "We feel that once it passes a threshold test of being something about which we feel quite certain, that **the same discount factor tends to apply to everything**." — **[M1998-151]**, 1998 meeting

> "So we think all the capital asset pricing model-type reasoning with different rates of riskadjusted return and all that, we tend to think it is — well, we don’t tend to — **we think it is nonsense**." — **[M1998-152]**, 1998 meeting

**Certainty is handled in three other places, not in the rate.** First, at the understanding gate: risk is "a go/no-go valve" **[M1998-150]**, and a business whose future cannot be pictured is dropped, not discounted harder **[M1998-152]**. Second, in the cash estimate: "We may be more conservative in estimating the returns of cash from some, but the discount rate we would use is a constant" **[M2003-147]**. Third, in the discount from value demanded at purchase:

> "we adjust by simply trying to **buy it at a big discount from that present value** calculated using the risk-free interest rate." — **[M1997-126]**, 1997 meeting

One 1994 answer does put certainty into the rate, and it is carried below as a tension rather than smoothed: "The more certain we feel about a business, the closer we are willing to play it" **[M1994-004]**.

**How the level of rates changes what is paid.** Every asset is "100 percent sensitive to interest rates" **[M1994-047]**; rates are "to the value of assets what gravity is to matter" **[M2021-020]**; and low long rates "push the prices of all productive investments upward" **[L2021-006]**. Two adjustments for unusual rate levels are stated, and they point in opposite directions. In 1996 Buffett says that when rates look very low "we might use a little higher rate" **[M1996-024]**; in 1999 he says "We would probably use a lower discount factor in evaluating any business now, under present-day interest rates" **[M1999-011]**. By 2016 the effect is admitted openly, and inexactly:

> "very cheap money makes me **pay a little more for businesses** than when money was at what we previously thought was normal rates. And very tight money would cause me to pay somewhat less. [...] And if you ask me whether I paid a little more for Precision Castparts because interest rates were around zero, than if they’d been 6 percent, the answer is yes. **I try not to pay too much more, but it has an effect.**" — **[M2016-078]**, 2016 meeting

> "And it does **modestly affect what we pay** for businesses." — **[M2016-079]**, 2016 meeting

The rate that matters is the future rate, not today's quote, and the rows admit nobody can supply it:

> "**The most important thing is future interest rates.** And, you know, and people frequently plug in the current interest rate saying that’s the best they can do. [...] But that doesn’t mean I want use the current figure, either." — **[M2017-084]**, 2017 meeting

### The floor or hurdle

The merge left one point open: does M2016-078 retire the ten percent figure of 2002 and 2003, or only qualify it? Every row found in the v5 ledger that names a floor, a hurdle, a minimum or the figure they quit on is quoted here in date order (search words: hurdle, floor, threshold, minimum, expectancy, "drop out", every "10 percent" and "10%"). Rows using those words for pay plans, acquisition size or position limits are left out.

> "Well, I would say that in a world of 7 percent long-term bond rates that we would certainly want to think we were **discounting future after-tax streams of cash at at least a 10 percent rate**. But that will depend on the certainty we feel about the business." — **[M1994-004]**, 1994 meeting

> "And we are affected in that valuation process to a considerable degree by interest rates, but not by whether they’re 7.3, or 7.0, or 7.5. But I mean, we’ll be thinking much differently if they’re — long-term rates are 11 percent or 5 percent. And — but **we don’t have any magic multiples in mind**." — **[M1995-114]**, 1995 meeting

> "So you can say that isn’t even enough to charge people, but we find that 15 percent gets their attention. And it should get their attention, but **it shouldn’t be such a high-hurdle rate that things that we want to do don’t get done**." — **[M1996-075]**, 1996 meeting (a charge on capital used by the subsidiaries, not a purchase price)

> "Unless, however, we see **a very high probability of at least 10% pre-tax returns** [...] we will sit on the sidelines." — **[L2002-020]**, 2002 letter (the elided after-tax figure carries a damaged character in the row)

> "So we sort of have **a minimum threshold** in our mind about which we’re — below which — we’re unwilling to commit money. [...] rather than bend a little bit and start settling for lower rates for 30 years because rates for 30 days are so low, we would rather just sit it out and wait a while." — **[M2003-148]**, 2003 meeting

> "And we quit on buying — **we don’t want to buy equities where our real expectancy is below 10 percent**. Now, that’s true whether short rates are 6 percent or whether short rates are 1 percent. We just feel that it would get very sloppy to start dipping below that. [...] So, there’s just a point at which we drop out of the game. **And it’s arbitrary.** There’s no — we have no scientific studies or anything." — **[M2003-149]**, 2003 meeting

> "to some considerable extent — **we are guessing at our future opportunity cost**. [...] And if interest rates were to more or less permanently settle at 1 percent or something like that, and Warren were to reappraise his notions of future opportunity cost, **he would change the numbers**." — **[M2003-151]**, 2003 meeting (Munger)

> "on the theory that we’ll find something that gives us 10 percent or better" — **[M2003-152]**, 2003 meeting

> "For instance, **hurdle rates by — based on opportunity costs. Perfectly reasonable concept.**" — **[M2003-130]**, 2003 meeting (Munger)

> "if somebody guaranteed me that the 10-year rate would never go above 4.2 percent for the next 50 years, **we would have to readjust, recalibrate every decision we make** around Berkshire." — **[M2005-104]**, 2005 meeting

> "**We don’t formally have discount rates.** [...] But we are going to want to get a significantly higher return, obviously — in terms of cash produced relative to the amount we’re outlaying now for a business — than we are from a government bond. [...] Well, if government bond rates were 2 percent, we’re not going to buy a business to earn 3 or 3 1/2 percent expectancy over the years." — **[M2007-095]**, 2007 meeting

> "“What’s our hurdle rate today?” I mean, **we’ve never used the term**. [...] we want enough so that we feel very comfortable if they closed down on the stock market for a couple of years, if interest rates go up another hundred basis points or 200 basis points, we’re still happy with what we’ve bought. And above that, I really — I know it sounds kind of fuzzy, **but it is fuzzy**." — **[M2007-096]**, 2007 meeting

> "The concept of a hurdle rate makes nothing but sense, and yet **a lot of terrible errors are made by people who are talking about hurdle rates**." — **[M2007-097]**, 2007 meeting (Munger)

> "when we buy a business, a whole business, we never get a huge bargain and, of course, **we may get down toward 10 percent pretax earnings on what we pay**. That isn’t so awful as you think when you — a lot of the money comes from insurance float that costs you nothing." — **[M2011-062]**, 2011 meeting (Munger)

> "In terms of cost of capital, Charlie and I always figure that **our cost of capital is the — is what could be produced by our second best idea**." — **[M2014-014]**, 2014 meeting

> "very cheap money makes me pay a little more for businesses [...] I try not to pay too much more, but it has an effect." — **[M2016-078]**, 2016 meeting

> "And if the hurdle rate is 15 percent or something, having them all come out at 15.1 or 15.2, because that’s what’s going to happen. I mean, **you’re going to get the numbers you want to hear** and to an extreme degree." — **[M2019-076]**, 2019 meeting (a warning about staff and spreadsheets, not a rate Berkshire uses)

**What the rows support:** a floor that exists, is stated as a number only in 2002 and 2003, is called arbitrary by the man who set it, and moves with lasting changes in the long rate; not a fixed number v5 can apply as a rule.

1. **A floor exists.** Below some expected return they wait: **[M2003-148]**, **[M2007-095]**.
2. **The ten percent figure is stated twice, and qualified both times.** The letter puts it as "a very high probability of at least 10% pre-tax returns" **[L2002-020]**; the meeting calls it "real expectancy" and then "arbitrary", with "no scientific studies" **[M2003-149]**. What it was protecting against was low *short* rates: "whether short rates are 6 percent or whether short rates are 1 percent" **[M2003-149]**, "because rates for 30 days are so low" **[M2003-148]**.
3. **The same meeting says the floor would move with a lasting change in rates.** Munger, in the same answer: if rates settled "more or less permanently" at a low level, Buffett "would change the numbers" **[M2003-151]**. Two years later Buffett says the same of a guaranteed low long rate **[M2005-104]**.
4. **After 2003 no row in the v5 ledger restates ten percent as the floor for buying.** The 2007 rows replace it with the government bond plus a margin that is "significantly higher" and "fuzzy", and disown the term hurdle rate **[M2007-095]**, **[M2007-096]**, **[M2007-097]**. The 2011 row mentions ten percent as roughly where whole-business purchases land, softened by free float **[M2011-062]**, not as a line.
5. **M2016-078 is what M2003-151 predicted: a long period of very cheap money changing the numbers.** It does not withdraw the 2003 floor by name, and no row found says the floor was withdrawn; but it also states no floor, and it says the price paid rose "a little" because rates were near zero.

So M2016-078 **qualifies** the 2003 floor rather than retiring it, confirming the qualification made in 2003: a floor that low *short* rates must not pull down, but that lasting low *long* rates can move, slowly, since "it isn’t that exact a science" **[M2016-078]**. No instance found in the v5 ledger of a floor figure restated after 2011. The draft records the floor as the speakers' minimum expectancy, set against the long government rate and their next-best use of money, with no number of its own; the quoted figures are the speakers' for their own times, and point 5 is the drafter's reading of M2003-151 and M2005-104, not a statement any speaker makes. The tension M2016-078 against M2003-149 stays OPEN below, as the merge marked it.

The second-best-idea rows **[M2014-014]**, **[M2003-117]** and **[M2001-084]** belong to Q8. They show the floor is also an opportunity cost, which is where Munger places it **[M2003-151]**.

### The range, not the point

Value is a range, never a point, and the width of the range is itself a finding.

> "Using precise numbers is, in fact, foolish; **working with a range of possibilities is the better approach**." — **[L2000-024]**, 2000 letter

> "Charlie and I admit that we feel confident in estimating intrinsic value for only a portion of traded equities and then only when we employ **a range of values, rather than some pseudo-precise figure**." — **[L1999-027]**, 1999 letter

> "I say "estimate" because calculations of intrinsic value, though all-important, are **necessarily imprecise and often seriously wrong**. The more uncertain the future of a business, the more possibility there is that the calculation will be wildly off-base." — **[L2005-001]**, 2005 letter

> "And we project out what we think will happen. And **we know whatever our projection is, that it will be wrong**, but we try to be conservative." — **[M2017-013]**, 2017 meeting

> "Now, the intrinsic value is not a specific point, it’s probably a range in my mind that might have a band maybe of 10 percent." — **[M2019-003]**, 2019 meeting

Two people get close but different ranges **[M1996-009]**, **[M2006-082]**, **[M2011-018]**, and fuzziness does not make the method wrong: "the fact that it’s fuzzy to calculate doesn’t mean that it’s not the proper way to think about it" **[M2003-136]**. The rows give an order of work: conservative inputs, without padding each one, and the safety margin applied once at the end **[M2004-055]**. The price is then set against the bottom of the range:

> "If the answer is yes, we will buy the stock (or business) if it sells at a reasonable price in relation to **the bottom boundary of our estimate**." — **[L2013-012]**, 2013 letter

**What a wide range means.** Usually it means no decision:

> "Usually, **the range must be so wide that no useful conclusion can be reached**." — **[L2000-025]**, 2000 letter

> "At the other extreme, there are many times when the most brilliant of investors can't muster a conviction about the birds to emerge, not even when a very broad range of estimates is employed. [...] In cases of this sort, **any capital commitment must be labeled speculative**." — **[L2000-026]**, 2000 letter

A wide range is not offset by a bigger discount. The speakers move on instead:

> "if a business gets to the point where we think the industry in which it operates, the competitive position or anything is so chancy that we can’t really come up with a figure, **we don’t really try to compensate for that sort of thing by having some extra large margin of safety**. We really want to try to go on to something that we understand better." — **[M2007-022]**, 2007 meeting

### Margin of safety and the screamer

The price must be so far below the bottom of the range that the decision is obvious without arithmetic.

> "there ought to be such a margin of safety that it ought to be so attractive that **you don’t have to carry it out to three decimal places**." — **[M1995-115]**, 1995 meeting

> "You don’t — **if you have to actually do it on — with pencil and paper, it’s too close to think about**. I mean, it ought to just kind of scream at you that you’ve got this huge margin of safety." — **[M1996-084]**, 1996 meeting

> "I didn’t need to know whether it was worth 97 billion or 103 billion if I was buying it at 35 billion. [...] **If you have to carry it out to three decimal places, it’s not a good idea.** [...] I might not know how much they weigh, but I would know they were fat." — **[M2008-068]**, 2008 meeting

> "Occasionally, though, even very conservative estimates about the future emergence of birds reveal that the price quoted is **startlingly low in relation to value**." — **[L2000-025]**, 2000 letter

Munger says the calculation is never written out; Buffett agrees, and says why: "If it isn’t pluperfect obvious that it’s going to work out well, if you do the calculation, he tends to go on to the next idea" **[M1996-083]**; "we really like the decision to be obvious enough to us that it doesn’t require making a detailed calculation" **[M1995-032]**.

**The size of the discount follows the certainty of the business.** It is larger when the future is less sure **[M1997-080]**, small for See's or Coca-Cola **[M2007-022]**, and defined by Munger as "getting more value than you’re paying" **[M2007-027]**. One test of whether it is big enough is to imagine the market shut and rates higher: "if they closed down on the stock market for a couple of years, if interest rates go up another hundred basis points or 200 basis points, we’re still happy with what we’ve bought" **[M2007-096]**. The margin is there because things go wrong: "we had a margin of safety when we bought into it, [...] and it turns out we needed it" **[M2002-104]**.

> "Obviously, if you understood a business perfectly — the future of a business — you would need very little in the way of a margin of safety. So the more volatile the business is — or possibility is — but assuming you still want invest in it, **the larger the margin of safety**." — **[M1997-080]**, 1997 meeting

**Where screamers are found.** The stock market gives bargain prices and private sellers do not **[M1994-040]**, **[M2008-041]**. For whole businesses the rows accept a fair price for a wonderful business **[M2003-040]** and, for the rare business that can reinvest at high returns, "you probably should stretch a little" **[M2013-059]**.

### Why it is a STOP

Three ways close the file here.

**Cannot be valued: stop.**

> "And if we don’t have the faintest idea what the future stream is going to look like, **we don’t have the faintest idea what it’s worth, now**." — **[M1994-077]**, 1994 meeting

> "But up to the point where you decide what you’re willing to pay — **you may decide you can’t estimate it at all**. I mean, that’s what happens with us with most companies." — **[M1996-025]**, 1996 meeting

> "If, however, we lack the ability to estimate future earnings � which is usually the case � **we simply move on to other prospects**." — **[L2013-012]**, 2013 letter (the replacement characters are in the row)

**Valued, but the price does not clear the floor: stop.**

> "So, **there’s just a point at which we drop out of the game**." — **[M2003-149]**, 2003 meeting

**Valued, price below value, but the case is close: stop.**

> "So if you really need a calculator to figure out that it’s — the discount rate is 9.6 percent instead of 9.8 percent — **forget about the whole exercise**. Just go onto something that shouts at you." — **[M2009-005]**, 2009 meeting

The 2008 meeting adds that a price is never waived: "we don’t do anything when the phrase “regardless of price” enters into the sentence" **[M2008-041]**. In the rows' three boxes **[M2006-013]**, the first case is "too hard" and the second and third are "out".

### What it rules OUT

- **Book value as value.** "book value is meaningless as an indicator of intrinsic value" **[L1994-008]**; **[M1996-008]**.
- **Ratios, peer multiples and single yardsticks.** Dividend yield, P/E, price to book and growth rates "have nothing to do with valuation except to the extent they provide clues to the amount and timing of cash flows" **[L2000-023]**; no single relative P/E **[M2002-021]**; a GEICO priced against "other insurance stocks" was mispriced **[M1996-016]**.
- **Risk-adjusted discount rates and the capital asset pricing model** **[M1996-025]**, **[M1998-152]**; a "cost of equity capital" is "a perfectly amazing mental malfunction" **[M2003-117]**.
- **Formulas that claim precision.** "there is an ultimate formula, but the trouble is you don’t know what to stick in for the variables" **[M2017-083]**; Munger's "physics envy" **[M2018-082]**; a staff hurdle that every proposal clears by a tenth **[M2019-076]**.
- **Other people's projections.** "we’ve never looked at a projection in connection with either a security we’ve bought or a business we’ve bought" **[M1995-050]**; **[M2003-065]**.
- **Growth carried past the discount rate.** When the compound rate exceeds the discount rate, "you get into infinity" **[M1997-095]**, **[M2004-023]**; Munger: back off **[M2004-024]**.
- **Assets valued on the next buyer.** Speculation **[M2002-078]**.
- **A low quote on short money as a reason to accept a low return** **[M2003-148]**.
- **The close call.** **[M2009-005]**, **[M1996-084]**.

### Mistakes the speakers narrate here

The merged map's section 3 places the price mistakes in group 13 (overpaying on optimistic earning power) and group 4 (cheap poor businesses, and refusing to pay up). Dexter is narrated as a business misjudgment (group 1) and as a price error; only the price half is drawn here.

- **Paying too much for a good business on optimistic earning power.** Precision Castparts: "I was simply too optimistic about PCC's normalized profit potential [...] wrong in my calculation of the proper price to pay for the business" **[L2020-001]**; "I paid too much, in relation to average earnings" **[M2021-039]**. Kraft Heinz: "you can pay too much for a wonderful business" **[M2019-013]**; "you can pay way too much for a growing brand" **[M2019-043]**. The same purchase is the one M2016-078 says cost "a little more" because rates were near zero; no row links the two, and the draft draws no conclusion.
- **Dexter as price.** "I clearly made a mistake in paying what I did for Dexter in 1993" **[L2000-034]**; "I was very wrong in paying what I did, and paying it in the manner I did" **[M2002-116]**; the earnings figure on which the price was set assumed "that the future would be as good as the past" **[M2003-077]**.
- **Overpaying in a hot market for even the best.** **[L1996-032]**, **[M1997-002]**, **[L2014-021]**.
- **Refusing to pay up for the outstanding business, the costliest category by Buffett's own count.** "being reluctant to pay up a little for a business I knew was really outstanding [...] the cost of that has been many, many billions" **[M1997-083]**; See's nearly lost on price **[M2003-146]**, **[M2017-010]**.
- **Buying cheap poor businesses.** Berkshire bought "because it was cheap statistically" **[M1998-007]**; "What you can’t do is turn any investment into a good deal by paying little" **[M2019-015]**.

### Tensions carried

Not resolved. Marks follow the merged map, section 4; the last three are found in this reading and are not in the merge.

1. **[M1996-083] against [M1996-011] and [M1996-024]**: discounted cash is the stated method, yet Munger has never seen it done. SPEAKER, partly: Buffett concedes it **[M1997-126]**.
2. **[M1995-050] against [M1995-032]**: never looked at a projection, yet value is discounted future cash. READER.
3. **[M2003-065] against [M2003-138]**: no interest in others' projections, against feeling "pretty good about our projections". READER: whose projection.
4. **[M1998-151] against [M1997-126]**: no higher rate for uncertainty, yet risk met by a big discount. READER: the rate against the price.
5. **[M2003-149] against [M2003-151]**: a floor whatever short rates do, against a permanent low rate changing the numbers. SPEAKER, partly: "arbitrary".
6. **[M2016-078] against [M2003-149]**: pay a little more when money is very cheap, against no lower expectancy whatever short rates are. **OPEN.** The draft's reading in "The floor or hurdle" is a reading only.
7. **[M1997-003] against [M1997-002]**: certainty about a wonderful business outweighs a few percent of price, yet one can overpay for the best. OPEN.
8. **[M2013-059] against [M2013-045]**: stretch a little for a wonderful business, against a disparity in price and value that is "big". OPEN.
9. **[M2017-024] against [L2000-025]**: the old huge margins of safety are gone ("You’re just going to have to keep reaching for the higher branches"), against acting only on a startlingly low price; also **[M2017-063]**, **[M2023-009]**. OPEN.
10. **[M1994-076] against [M1994-078]**: certainty is prized, yet the easier business is not therefore the better buy. READER: price is the link.
11. **[M1994-004] against [M1996-024] and [M1998-151]**: in 1994 the discount rate rises above the bond and falls with certainty; from 1996 the rate is the bond and certainty stays out of it. OPEN.
12. **[M1996-024] against [M1999-011]**: a little higher rate when rates look very low, against a lower discount factor under present-day rates. OPEN.
13. **[M1994-047] against [M2017-028]**: "I do not have to have a view on interest rates" to decide, against the future long rate as the one statistic he would ask for. OPEN.

### Steering noticed

The auto-loaded map's fifth question, "WHAT IS IT WORTH, AGAINST A GOVERNMENT BOND?", pulled in two places. It made the bond feel like a hurdle to beat; the rows mostly use it as a discount rate and yardstick **[M1997-086]**, **[M1999-103]**, and the 2003 floor is set against short rates and the speakers' own threshold, not the bond (the 2003 PM notes say the same), so the comparison is left to Q8.
