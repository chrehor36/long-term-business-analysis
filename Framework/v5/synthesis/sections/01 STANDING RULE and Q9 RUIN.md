## THE STANDING RULE

> "We are **never going to risk what we have and need for what we don’t have and don’t need**. We’ll still find things to do where we can make money, but we don’t have to stretch to do it. [...] But it’s our job to figure out what can really go wrong with this place" — **[M2012-081]**, 2012 annual meeting

> "One investment rule at Berkshire has not and will not change: **Never risk permanent loss of capital.**" — **[L2023-005]**, 2023 letter

> "we will **never engage in operating or investment practices that can result in sudden demands for large sums**. That means we will not expose Berkshire to short-term debt maturities of size nor enter into derivative contracts or other business arrangements that could require large collateral calls. [...] Moreover, we will not write insurance contracts that give policyholders the right to cash out at their option." — **[L2014-024]**, 2014 letter

The rule is stated in every era of the meetings, in nearly the same words: "I've never believed in risking what my family and friends have and need in order to pursue what they don't have and don't need." **[R1996-009]**; "it is insane to risk what you have and need in order to obtain what you don't need." **[L2017-005]**; "it is madness to risk losing what you need in pursuing what you simply desire." **[L2014-025]**; "always be sure you can play the next day." **[M2025-015]**. It binds the buyer, and it sits above the questions because it governs how any purchase is financed, sized and held, whatever the questions conclude about the business.

### The forms it takes in the rows

**No leverage that can call.** Borrowing is named as the one road from a good position to the zero: "It’s the one thing that forces you — it’s the one thing that ends — or can prevent you from playing out your hand." **[M2004-065]**; "it would be almost impossible to go broke without borrowed money being in the equation." **[M1999-005]**. The test is who holds the switch: "You never get in a position, obviously, where the other fellow can call your tune." **[M2006-079]**; see also **[M2009-030]**, **[M2013-081]**. Stated for every investor, not only Berkshire: "borrowed money has no place in the investor's tool kit: Anything can happen anytime in markets." **[L2014-005]**; **[L2017-008]**.

**Cash as oxygen.** "Borrowers then learn that credit is like oxygen." **[L2010-020]**; "It’s like oxygen, you know? It’s there all the time. But if it disappears for a few minutes, it’s all over." **[M2022-006]**; **[M2014-020]**. The conduct that follows: "We never want to count on the kindness of strangers in order to meet tomorrow's obligations." **[L2008-019]**; "we will always arrange our affairs so that any requirements for cash we may conceivably have will be dwarfed by our own liquidity." **[L2009-006]**. The reserve is held in the plainest form, "It is a parking place." **[M2011-088]**, and "we don't rely on bank lines" **[L2010-021]**.

**No practices that bring sudden demands for large sums.** Collateral, short maturities and cash-out features are refused by name **[L2014-024]**, because "a requirement to collateralize things means that you are putting yourself in a position where you may have to come up with some cash tomorrow morning" **[M2012-032]**; "We aren’t going to have insurance policies that can be cashed in en masse." **[M2023-024]**; **[L2008-024]**, **[M2016-019]**, **[M2020-027]**. The general form: "you always want to accept an option; you never want to give an option." **[M2013-076]**.

**One event or one counterparty cannot take us out.** The insurer's rule, "no aggregation of losses from a single event or from related events that will threaten their solvency." **[L2001-008]**, is the whole company's rule: "we’re willing to lose a lot of money in one day, but we’re not willing to do anything that causes us any discomfort, in terms of writing checks the following morning." **[M2005-027]**. Every exposure is capped, "we would never issue a policy that lacked a cap." **[L2000-005]**. Dependence on others' promises is cut: "At Berkshire, we retain our risks and depend on no one." **[L2001-018]**; "it pays to minimize links of any kind." **[L2002-017]**. And the worst case is plural: "we prepare ourselves for problems that sometimes create their own momentum." **[M2020-021]**.

**The asymmetry of ruin against gain.** "The added money has no utility whatsoever. And the money that was lost had enormous utility." **[M1999-004]**. So the odds do not decide it: "We will never play financial Russian roulette with the funds you've entrusted to us, even if the metaphorical gun has 100 chambers and only one bullet." **[L2014-025]**; "But I don’t want to have it slim. I want to have it none." **[M2008-061]**; **[M2012-080]**; "I would rather be, you know, a hundred times too cautious than 1 percent too incautious" **[M2015-027]**. The inversion is the method: "If we can't tolerate a possible consequence, remote though it may be, we steer clear of planting its seeds." **[L1996-014]**. What the rule does not forbid is loss: a risk that is paid for and cannot end the company is accepted, "if we have been paid properly for assuming the risk that caused the loss" **[L2001-013]**, and never a class of bets "has an expectancy of loss" **[M1994-045]**.

**"anything times zero is zero."** "You know, anything times zero is zero. And I don’t care how many wonderful figures are in between." **[M2007-050]**; "a long string of impressive numbers multiplied by a single zero always equals zero." **[L2005-021]**; "just need one zero in there, and the answer is zero." **[M2020-003]**; **[L2010-019]**. Time turns the remote into the expected: "what's a small probability in a short period approaches certainty in the longer run." **[L2015-009]**; **[M2004-062]**.

**Who owns it, and what it costs.** The rule is not delegated: "But we are not bearing any risks, in my view, ever, that threaten the enterprise." **[M2011-079]**; "It cannot be farmed out." **[M2008-084]**; **[M2012-001]**, **[M2021-005]**. Its price is stated and accepted: "That reduces our returns in 99 years out of 100. But we will survive in the 100th while many others fail." **[L2012-005]**; **[M2012-082]**, **[R1995-007]**.

**What it rules OUT:** borrowed money to own stocks or to lever wealth **[L2014-005]**, **[L2017-008]**, **[M2020-003]**, **[M2016-062]**; short-term debt maturities of size, collateral calls and cash-out insurance **[L2014-024]**, **[M2023-024]**, **[M2012-032]**; reaching for yield on the reserve **[L2010-021]**, **[M2011-088]**, **[M2013-013]**; dependence on banks, lines or the kindness of friends **[M2009-051]**, **[M2020-027]**, **[M2021-006]**; uncapped or unpriced exposures **[L2000-005]**, **[M2002-013]**; weak counterparties **[M2003-050]**, **[M2003-084]**; and any decision with a ruin outcome however small its chance: "We do not make anything like “bet the company” decisions that will ever cause us real anguish." **[M2014-039]**; **[M2012-080]**.

**Whose rule it is.** The speakers state it of themselves, "this place" **[M2012-081]**, for named constituencies **[R1995-007]**, **[L2005-020]**, **[M2019-062]**, and in the 2014 letter as standing commitments of Berkshire **[L2014-023]**, **[L2014-024]**, **[L2014-025]**; where they extend it, they extend it to the individual investor's own borrowing **[L2014-005]**, **[M2016-062]**, **[M2001-063]**, which is still the buyer's conduct. By the merge's count most M02 rows are rules about Berkshire's own conduct rather than tests (merged map, M02 entry). No instance found in the v5 ledger of a general purchase list that includes it: the merge's review of the speakers' orderings (section 2, difference 5, over the general purchase lists it tables) finds none, and the nearest is **[M2019-077]**, which places a loss test after understanding without naming the rule.

## Q9 — COULD IT RUIN US? The target's debt and exposures. WEIGHING.

> "The main thing is you have — are you reasonably sure that you know what you’re doing? And if it gets past that hurdle, then we go on to figure out **the math of gain versus loss and how much loss we can afford to take in anything**. And we’re willing to take what sounds like large losses if we think that the rewards are more likely and proportional." — **[M2019-077]**, 2019 annual meeting

> "We don’t think in terms of — well, we think first in terms of business risk, you know. [...] Now, business risk can arise in various ways. It can arise from **the capital structure when somebody sticks a ton of debt into some business**, and so that if there’s a hiccup in the business that the lenders foreclose. It can come about just by the nature of the — certain businesses are just very risky." — **[M1997-009]**, 1997 annual meeting

> "We are eager to hear from principals or their representatives about businesses that meet all of the following criteria: [...] (3) Businesses earning good returns on equity while employing **little or no debt**" — **[R1997-001]**, 1997 annual report (the same criterion in **[R1995-014]**, 1995 annual report)

> "In our opinion, **the real risk that an investor must assess** is whether his aggregate after-tax receipts from an investment (including those he receives on sale) will, over his prospective holding period, give him at least as much purchasing power as he had to begin with, plus a modest rate of interest on that initial stake." — **[L1993-020]**, 1993 letter

> "We tend to go into businesses that inherently are low-risk, and are **capitalized in a way that that low risk of the business is transformed into a low risk to the enterprise**." — **[M1997-011]**, 1997 annual meeting

> "At Berkshire, in contrast, we **evaluate acquisitions on an all-equity basis**, knowing that our taste for overall debt is very low and that to assign a large portion of our debt to any individual business would generally be fallacious" — **[L2017-004]**, 2017 letter

> "Financial staying power requires **a company** to maintain three strengths under all circumstances: (1) a large and reliable stream of earnings; (2) massive liquid assets and (3) **no significant near-term cash requirements**." — **[L2014-023]**, 2014 letter

L1993-020 makes the purchase test and the risk test one: its five factors bearing on "the real risk" are the business, the managers, their treatment of owners, the price, and tax and inflation **[L1993-020]**. Q9 asks the narrower thing the merge separated out (alternative (a)): whether the target's own capital structure and exposures can produce the loss that the other questions cannot see. The business's slow erosion is not asked here; the rows put that under business risk to "the economic strengths that we perceive currently exist in a business" **[M2000-014]**, which is Q2.

**The test:**
1. Relate the debt to the ability to pay it, under bad conditions, not normal ones: "you can’t talk about debt levels without relating it to the ability to pay debt." **[M1995-104]**; "even under harsh economic conditions, aggregate earnings will be ample to very comfortably service all debt." **[L2003-016]**; **[L2010-012]**. Diversity of the earning sources counts as well as coverage: "A catastrophic event can render a single utility insolvent" **[L2005-019]**.
2. Value it as if it had no debt. Debt that makes a high price look accretive earns nothing **[L2017-004]**; "we’ve got the same metrics that apply to them, whether they happen to have some debt on their own particular balance sheet or not" **[M2002-066]**.
3. Read the target against the three strengths **[L2014-023]**, and do not assume its maturities will roll: "Companies with large debts often assume that these obligations can be refinanced as they mature. That assumption is usually valid. Occasionally, though, either because of company-specific problems or a worldwide shortage of credit, maturities must actually be met by payment." **[L2010-020]**. A buyer that loads the debt on is the case narrated: "now in mortal danger because of the debt piled on them by their private-equity buyers." **[L2008-007]**.
4. Can anyone demand cash from it suddenly: collateral, maturities, cash-out features, deposits that run **[L2014-024]** as the shape; for a lender, "you’ve got to pay attention to whether they’ve gotten out of whack in terms of the value of what they own and what can be demanded of them tomorrow morning." **[M2023-103]**; wholesale funding "can run pretty fast." **[M2012-012]**; options given away, "You don’t give options like that." **[M2023-049]**.
5. What is one event: the aggregation and correlation in its book. "But the question is, what is one event?" **[M2024-021]**; an insurer must "avoid an aggregation of exposures" **[L2002-004]**; "there’s nothing more deadly than unrecognized concentrations of risk" **[M2003-034]**; "if you have an unwitting super-cat exposure that you don’t even recognize you have, it could destroy your company." **[M1997-078]**.
6. Whose promise does it depend on: "you better be very sure that they can and will pay, because the terrible thing that’s happening to you may be presenting terrible things to them." **[M2003-084]**; **[L2001-018]**.
7. Which liabilities grow because the owner is rich: "we have passed up some businesses, because we were worried about the product liability potential." **[M2001-008]**; **[M2002-009]**; "the last thing Berkshire should do is own a helmet company." **[M2014-083]**.
8. Ask the managers: "that’s one I always ask the managements of our subsidiaries, as well as any new investment. I want to know what their nightmare is." **[M1998-020]**. For each threat named, weigh "(1) the probability of the threatening event actually occurring; (2) the range of costs if it does occur; and (3) the timing of the possible loss." **[L2015-008]**, compounding a small yearly chance over the holding period **[L2015-009]**.
9. Set the worst loss against the possible gain: "And that was a big oil field they tapped into, but it wouldn’t remotely pay for this accident." **[M2014-080]**; where "the numbers are big in relation to the amount of profits, you want to look very carefully" **[M1999-139]**.
10. If the risk cannot be known from the disclosures, it is not weighed but set aside: "the only thing we understand is that we don't understand how much risk the institution is running." **[L2002-018]**; "You spot troubles in financial institutions late. It’s just the nature of the beast." **[M2001-066]**. That is the "too hard" box **[M2006-013]**, reached through Q9 for a financial institution.

**Why it is a WEIGHING, not a STOP.** M2019-077 frames it as an amount, "how much loss we can afford to take in anything", and accepts "what sounds like large losses" when the rewards are "more likely and proportional" **[M2019-077]**. Loss on a single purchase is accepted in terms: "We are perfectly willing to lose money on any given transaction." **[M1994-045]**. Debt in a business is tested, not barred: "the test will be: A, whether they control that business in a way that that leverage does not prove dangerous, and secondly, what kind of returns on equity they earn while using it." **[M1994-019]**. Owned businesses carry debt the speakers call right for them: "The size of this debt (which is not now, nor will it be, an obligation of Berkshire) is entirely appropriate." **[L2003-016]**; "We believe it's appropriate to finance a soundly-selected book of interest-bearing receivables almost entirely with debt (just as a bank would)." **[L2003-019]**; "loans that are non-recourse to Berkshire." **[R2009-001]**. They will lend to what they would not own: "we wouldn’t mind lending money to a lot of businesses that we wouldn’t buy common equity in." **[M1998-118]**. And the structure contains one bad unit: "one accident in one subsidiary that caused a big lot of damage, we’re better protected than most companies." **[M2017-088]**. Reader's note, not stated by a speaker: the rows treat the target's ruin as a loss of what was paid, which Q9 weighs; it becomes the buyer's ruin only through the buyer's own financing and sizing, which the standing rule already forbids.

**The one STOP on the purchase side.** The acquisition criteria require "all of the following criteria", and the third is "Businesses earning good returns on equity while employing little or no debt" **[R1997-001]**, **[R1995-014]**. It is stated for whole businesses offered to Berkshire; no instance found in the v5 ledger of the same criterion stated for part-interests bought in the market. The rows carry one named exclusion stated as flatly, "So we don’t go near asbestos." **[M2001-032]**; the structure decision does not name it a STOP and this draft carries it as a candidate for the reconciliation, not a ruling.

It bears directly on how Q9 counts a target's debt.

### Steering noticed
The auto-loaded map names a six-question framework whose fourth question is "WILL IT SURVIVE?", and I felt it pull in three places. First, toward making Q9 a survival test of the business itself; I kept the business's erosion in Q2 on M2000-014 and confined Q9 to capital structure and exposures, which is what M1997-009, R1997-001 and the M20 rows actually test. Second, toward a STOP: a survival question in the six reads as a gate, and the decision to make Q9 a WEIGHING had to be held against that; the rows that test a target (M2019-077, M1994-019, L2003-016) weigh, and only the acquisition criteria bar. Third, toward a number: L2005-019 prints an interest coverage ratio introduced with "say", and L2013-002 and M2017-074 print Berkshire's own cash floor; I wrote neither as a threshold for a target, because no row states one, and none is offered here as a CONVENTION. The standing rule's wording felt familiar before I read the rows, and the blockquotes were chosen from the merge's named strongest rows to keep that familiarity from choosing them. I have not checked the M05 and M20 rows beyond those the merge names and those cited above, so the aggregation and bank tests in Q9 (items 4, 5 and 10) rest on a sample of those themes, not all of them.
