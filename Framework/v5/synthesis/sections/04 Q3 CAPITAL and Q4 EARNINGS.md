## Q3 — HOW MUCH CAPITAL MUST GO IN to get the earnings out, and what does the added capital earn? WEIGHING.

In the speakers' words: "does it look like it has good economics? Has it earned high returns on capital?" **[M1995-051]**, and, of any growth, "whether that’s good or bad depends on what we earn on that incremental $130 million over time" **[M2001-019]**.

> "No, the first question is, can I understand it? [...] And then the second question is, you know, does it look like it has **good economics**? **Has it earned high returns on capital?**" — **[M1995-051]**, 1995 annual meeting

> "**The best business is one that gives you more and more money every year without putting up anything to get it, or very little.** [...] The second-best business is a business that also gives you more and more money. It takes more money, but the rate at which you invest — reinvest — the money to get that growth is a **very satisfactory rate**. **The worst business of all** is the one that grows a lot, and where you’re forced [...] forced to grow to stay in the game at all, and where you’re reinvesting the capital at a **very low rate of return**." — **[M1998-081]**, 1998 annual meeting

> "In earlier days, Charlie and I shunned capital-intensive businesses such as public utilities. Indeed, the best businesses by far for owners continue to be those that have **high returns on capital** and that **require little incremental investment to grow**. [...] we are today quite willing to enter businesses that regularly require large capital expenditures. We expect only that these businesses have reasonable expectations of earning **decent returns on the incremental sums they invest**." — **[L2009-012]**, 2009 letter

The question stands second in the 1995 ordering, after understanding **[M1995-051]**, and third in the 2017 list of "key qualities", as "good returns on the net tangible assets required to operate the business; opportunities for internal growth at attractive returns" **[L2017-002]**. Munger reports it as Buffett's lifelong test of good against bad: "the good business just throws up one easy decision after another" **[M1998-131]**.

### The tests

**1. What has it earned on the capital it needs, over time?** "we always look at what a business does in terms of what it earns on capital" **[M1998-005]**, and "the best businesses, by definition, are going to be businesses that earn very high returns on capital employed over time" **[M1995-047]**. The capital is the capital the business needs, not what a buyer paid: "we’re defining it in terms of the capital actually needed in the business. Whether it’s a good investment for us depends on how much we pay for that in the end" **[M2010-090]**. The measure is "return on tangible assets" **[M2011-060]**, the way "to measure profitability overall" **[M2014-007]**; book value is ignored, while "We do look at what a company is able to earn on invested assets and what it can earn on incremental invested assets" **[M2000-065]**. Return on sales is not the measure: a thin margin "only works in terms of return on capital if you turn your equity extraordinarily fast" **[M2017-096]**.

A high reported return is read with care before it is credited. Rule out "a cyclical peak in earnings, a monopolistic position, or leverage" **[L1994-009]**. Return on equity can be manufactured: "if you keep the equity low enough by buying shares back, why, you could make return on equity whatever you want" **[M1998-017]**; "we could make it almost any number you want if we just used enough leverage" **[M2001-054]**; repeated special charges flatter it, and AT&T's return computed with them in: "it’s not been good at all" **[M1999-033]**. Published averages "overstate what’s earned anyway" because the low-return companies drop out **[M1995-087]**. And unprecedented returns are not assumed to last: "we would not want to buy things on the basis that these returns would be sustained" **[M1998-016]**. The reason the test governs is time: "if you have a business that’s earning 5 or 6 percent on equity and you hold it for a long time, you are not going to do well in investing. Even if you buy it cheap to start with. Time is the enemy of the poor business, and it’s the friend of the great business" **[M1998-006]**; also **[M1998-120]**.

**2. How much must be reinvested to stand still, and how much to grow?** "Every year we spend amounts equal to our depreciation charge simply to stay in the same economic place" **[L2000-035]**. The speakers name the two kinds of need: "expenditures that a company must make to maintain its competitive position" and "optional outlays, aimed at business growth, that management expects will produce more than a dollar of value for each dollar spent" **[L1999-024]**; the owner is entitled to the manager's best guess of the first and to "know how much we are spending, beyond that maintenance cost, to build the business for the future" **[M2000-144]**. The tests inside it: look at "the amount of capital required to produce incremental revenues" **[M2000-088]**; does the profit end the year as cash, or "there’s your profit sitting in the yard" **[M2008-036]**; does the business report "the 12 percent on capital but there’s never any cash" **[M2003-122]**. Where the spending is compulsory, the business is a trap: "you have to spend money like crazy if it’s attractive to spend money, and you have to spend it the same way if it’s unattractive" **[M1998-128]**, and Munger: "we have tried to avoid places where there was a lot of compulsory reinvestment just in order to stand still" **[M1997-016]**. Inflation is the same test sharpened: the best business "retains its earning power in real dollars without commensurate investment" **[M2005-049]**; of the utilities, "To earn more, their owners must invest more" **[L2011-019]**; also **[M2004-008]**, **[M2015-049]**, **[M2022-050]**.

**3. The growth arithmetic: where growth adds and where it destroys.** "There’s a huge difference in the business that grows and requires a lot of capital to do so, and the business that grows, and doesn’t require capital" **[M1994-081]**; a business that does not grow at all can be the better investment **[M1994-080]**, **[M1994-081]**. "growth has been a curse in the airline business because more and more capital has been put into the business at inadequate returns. Now, growth is wonderful at See’s Candy, because it requires relatively little incremental investment to sell more pounds of candy" **[M2001-019]**. The 2007 letter gives the scale: "nothing shabby about earning $82 million pre-tax on $400 million of net tangible assets. But that equation for the owner is vastly different from the See's situation" **[L2007-008]**, and the gruesome case, where investors were "attracted by growth when they should have been repelled by it" **[L2007-009]**. The three are summed as savings accounts: the great one's rate rises, the good one pays "an attractive rate of interest that will be earned also on deposits that are added", and "the gruesome account both pays an inadequate interest rate and requires you to keep adding money at those disappointing returns" **[L2007-010]**. Price and reinvestment are read together: "the current multiple interacts with the reinvestment of capital and the rate at which that capital’s invested" **[M1995-113]**; also **[M1999-039]**.

For retained earnings the same test applies per share: "we do expect that normalized earning power to increase over time. And if it doesn’t, you know, one way or another, we’re failing you because we’re retaining those earnings" **[M2018-045]**; also **[M2016-059]**, **[M2018-046]**, **[M2023-003]**. Rising earnings alone prove nothing: "we’re not earning a higher rate of return on capital than we were when we started. We just put way more capital into the business" **[M2023-081]**.

The rate cannot run past its base. "an abundance of funds tends to dampen returns" **[L1996-002]**; GEICO is "not a business where, if you double the capital, you can double the earnings easily" **[M1995-069]**; "Most of the great businesses generate lots of money. They do not generate lots of opportunities to earn high returns on incremental capital" **[M2003-120]**. Munger: "what 15 percent per annum compounded will do is grow way faster than the economy can grow" **[M1997-096]**; Buffett: "when the compound rate becomes higher than the discount rate, you get into infinite numbers" **[M1997-095]**. The test: "if you trace out the mathematics of it, you bump into absurdities, then you better change expectations somewhat" **[M1999-067]**; compare the earnings a price implies with how many companies ever reach them **[M1999-076]**, **[L2000-038]**; doubt "companies out there that are implicitly promising you [...] that they’re going to earn at these rates of 20 percent-plus" **[M1999-024]**.

**4. The capital-heavy business as a second best.** The rows accept it, named as such: "as the second-best choice, still a good choice, the answer is yes. It’s not as good as the best choice" **[M2018-055]**; Munger: "they’re good enough" **[M2018-056]**. The condition is a decent return on the added sums **[L2009-012]**: "cash-consuming businesses, by their nature, are unattractive unless the cash they consume gets to earn a reasonable return" **[M2012-057]**. Regulated returns are fair, not rich: "deploy large sums at fair returns" **[L2005-005]**; "there’s no way we get rich on our utility investments. But there’s no way we get poor, either" **[M2009-104]**; "if they earn a decent return on capital, it can be a good business over time" **[M2007-112]**. The capital-heavy businesses that frighten are those "where you just pump in more money without knowing" the return **[M2009-104]**; the same figures that please from a growing utility would be hated from "a declining department store" **[M2014-088]**. The speakers state its costs plainly: BNSF must spend "more than its depreciation charge to simply maintain its present level of business", and earns "a pittance on the replacement value of the property" though "an acceptable return on its purchase price" **[L2023-009]**; also **[M2024-058]**, **[M2024-011]**, **[M2023-101]**.

### Why it is a WEIGHING, and the one place it is a STOP

It is a WEIGHING because the rows grade businesses on it rather than exclude them. The 1998 passage ranks three grades and admits the second **[M1998-081]**; the 2009 letter enters capital-heavy businesses on decent returns **[L2009-012]**; the 2018 answer names them second best **[M2018-055]**; and a non-growing business can be the better buy **[M1994-080]**. Return on capital also describes the business, not the purchase, so price can change the verdict on the investment while leaving the verdict on the business **[M2010-090]**, **[M2018-070]**. The low-return business held long comes nearest to a stop **[M1998-006]**, **[M1998-120]**, but the rows say it will not do well, not that it is barred. A required numeric return on capital: no instance found in the v5 ledger for this section's rows; the nearest are a 15 percent charge for capital to Berkshire's own managers, "it shouldn’t be such a high-hurdle rate that things that we want to do don’t get done" **[M1996-075]**, and "we’ve never used the term" for a hurdle rate **[M2007-096]**.

The one STOP is the acquisition criteria, which ask for businesses "that meet all of the following criteria", among them "(3) Businesses earning good returns on equity while employing little or no debt" **[R1997-001]**. For a whole business offered to the buyer, a business that fails (3) is not a candidate. The criteria name no figure for "good"; the judgment of the return remains a weighing even inside the stop.

### Tensions carried

- **The no-capital ideal against the high-return reinvestor.** "if you double the size of the business, you don’t need any more capital. And those are really wonderful businesses" **[M1996-074]**; "the ideal business" takes no capital and grows **[M2017-038]**, **[M2016-003]**, **[M2025-048]**. Against: "if you can find a business with a high rate of return on capital that can keep using more capital on that — that that’s the best business in the world" **[M1995-059]**; "The ideal business is one that earns very high returns on capital and could keep using lots of capital at those high returns" **[M2003-119]**; "we particularly favor the rare enterprise that can deploy additional capital at high returns in the future" **[L2023-002]**; and of the utilities, "we actually prefer the ones where net [...] investment is required" **[M2014-086]**. The speakers come near a bridge, that such reinvestors are "very, very, very few" **[M2003-119]** and that the cash of a no-capital business can be invested elsewhere **[L2007-007]**, **[M2016-003]**, but they never rank the two ideals against each other. OPEN, as the merge finds it in every slice.
- **Capital-heavy as good enough, against the ideal** **[M2018-055]**, **[M2018-056]** against **[M2017-038]**. SPEAKER: a second best forced by size, "we can’t get more money deployed in capital-light businesses at prices that make sense to us" **[M2018-055]**; "increasing capital — acts as an anchor on returns" **[M2016-003]**.
- **Capital-light wins in inflation, against capital-heavy at decent returns** **[M2011-054]** against **[L2009-012]**. SPEAKER: bridged in part by the replacement value of a railroad in inflation **[M2011-055]** and by naming the utilities as failing the inflation test **[L2011-019]**.
- **Natural limits.** Munger: some strong businesses "can’t rationally be expanded very much" **[M1997-070]**; Buffett: "you want to be careful that you don’t talk yourself into thinking a business that has limits" when it "really has way more potential" **[M1997-072]**. OPEN.

## Q4 — DO THE NUMBERS SHOW WHAT IT EARNS after every real cost, and can its condition be known from them? STOP on confusion, otherwise WEIGHING.

In the speakers' words: "you should understand what the figures are saying and what they don’t say and what they can’t say and what the management would like them to say that the auditors wouldn’t like them to say" **[M2025-032]**.

> "The numbers in any accounting report **mean nothing, per se, as to economic value**. They are guidelines to tell you something about how to get at economic value. [...] And of course, if we find numbers where it looks like people are taking the **most optimistic interpretation** of things that they can under GAAP and all of that, we get **very worried** about people who look like they massage the numbers in any way." — **[M1994-079]**, 1994 annual meeting

> "if I don’t understand it, I figure it’s probably because **the management doesn’t want me to understand it**. And if the management doesn’t want me to understand it, there’s probably something wrong going on. I mean, people don’t obfuscate with numbers, usually, without a purpose. And when you run into that **the best thing to do is you stay away**." — **[M2003-029]**, 2003 annual meeting

> "Depreciation charges are a more complicated subject but are **almost always true costs**. Certainly they are at Berkshire. I wish we could keep our businesses competitive while spending less than our depreciation charge, but in 51 years I've yet to figure out how to do so." — **[L2015-004]**, 2015 letter

### What the figures do and do not tell

The accounts are where the reading starts and never where it ends. Munger: "they’re a place to start economic analysis. The figures are frequently quite silly on a functional basis" **[M1994-017]**; Buffett: "you start with the accounting figures as the raw material of understanding a business, but you have to bring something additional to that" **[M2003-028]**, and "we sort of rewrite the accounting for any business that we’re looking at" **[M1994-016]**; also **[M2002-066]**. Accounting is "the language of business", but not "a perfect language" **[M1998-093]**; it "doesn’t reflect value" **[M2022-070]**, yet is "a terribly useful tool, if understood, in order to estimate value" **[M2017-064]**. The economics, not the treatment, decide: the speakers would "much prefer to purchase $2 of earnings that is not reportable by us under standard accounting principles than to purchase $1 of earnings that is reportable" **[R1995-006]**; and a cost left uncounted is still a cost, as GM and Ford learned of pensions: "believe me, it’s real" **[M2005-057]**.

The rows name what to read and how. Read "balance sheets over an 8 or 10 year period before I even look at the income account because there are certain things it’s harder to hide or play games with on the balance sheet" **[M2025-032]**. Read operating earnings, not a net income swung by securities gains: "Ignore our net income figure, however" **[L2010-017]**; net income is "sometimes [...] the all-deceptive figure" **[M2011-003]**; also **[M2019-001]**, **[M2023-001]**. Check the beginning and terminal years of any growth rate, since "a base year in which earnings were poor can produce a breathtaking, but meaningless, growth rate" **[L2005-003]**; also **[L2003-004]**. Separate businesses with different balance sheets, since "lumping them together [...] impedes analysis" **[L2008-005]**. Ask whether a subsidiary's results would stand without a parent's credit **[L2010-010]**, what a seller kept after tax ("What they got was about $2.5 billion") **[M2010-066]**, and whether the cash is free to use: "off-shore cash is simply not worth as much as cash held at home" **[L2016-009]**. Book value tells little of a good business: "a low price-book ratio means nothing to us" **[M1998-116]**; also **[L2018-001]**. Purchased goodwill is read separately: forget it when judging the business, include it when judging the capital allocation, "because we paid for it" **[M2011-060]**.

### The costs that are real

- **Depreciation.** "With rare exceptions, depreciation is an economic cost every bit as real as wages, materials, or taxes" **[R1996-023]**; "the cash outlay it represents is paid up front, before the asset acquired has delivered any benefits to the business" **[L2002-040]**; "It’s reverse float" **[M2003-106]**, "the worst kind of an expense" **[M2017-099]**. It is "not inappropriate in most companies to use as a proxy for required capital expenditures" **[M1998-127]**, but where it falls short the shortfall is subtracted: at railroads, "merely spending their depreciation expense will not keep them in the same place" **[M2016-092]**, and the reader "should also subtract something to reflect BNSF's inadequate depreciation charge" **[L2015-004]**; in heavy-capital businesses "your depreciation charges are inadequate and you’re kidding yourself as to your real economic profits" **[M2015-049]**.
- **EBITDA, therefore, is not earnings.** "The one figure we regard as utter nonsense is the so-called EBITDA" **[M1998-086]**; "does management think the tooth fairy pays for capital expenditures?" **[L2000-036]**; Munger: substitute "the phrase, “bullshit earnings.”" **[M2003-109]**, and "Earnings before what really counts in costs" **[M2012-035]**. The test: "I’ll look at that figure when you tell me you’ll make all the capital expenditures" **[M2003-108]**. Coverage is measured as "pre-tax earnings/interest, not EBITDA/interest" **[L2012-002]**.
- **Stock pay.** "Stock-based compensation" is "the most egregious example" of real expenses owners are told to ignore **[L2015-003]**; people found prices easier to inflate "if they didn’t count compensation expenses" **[M2003-112]**; a seller once proposed adding earnings "by sort of lying about our accounting" through options **[M2019-078]**. Dilution from options is computed, not waved off **[M2014-004]**. The speakers' own definition of earnings: "a figure calculated after interest, taxes, depreciation, amortization and all forms of compensation" **[L2021-003]**.
- **Restructuring and special charges.** "a large chunk of costs that should properly be attributed to a number of years is dumped into a single quarter" **[L1998-031]**; telling owners year after year to ignore necessary business adjustments "is misleading" **[L2016-007]**; "we do not ask you to forget about those costs" **[M1999-023]**.
- **Catastrophe losses.** Excluding them from "true" earnings "is deceptive nonsense" **[L2002-002]**; "this “except for” stuff doesn’t mean much in insurance" **[M2018-024]**.
- **Pensions and retiree promises.** "The real lollapalooza is pension fund accounting" **[M2003-110]**; assumed returns are chosen for the earnings they produce **[M2001-026]**, **[L2007-017]**, **[L2007-020]**; a pension book is "a big annuity company on the side", whose "liabilities are a lot more certain than the assets over time" **[M2013-075]**.
- **Taxes.** The government holds "what I call the Class AA stock" **[M2021-035]**; of deferred taxes in a utility, "I don’t think I would look at that as a hidden form of equity" **[M2015-030]**; also **[M2016-058]**.
- **Not every charge is real.** Amortization of purchased intangibles is often added back **[M1997-015]**, **[M2017-061]**, **[M2018-006]**, but "Some truly deplete over time while others never lose value. With software, for example, amortization charges are very real expenses" **[L2012-003]**.

### The tells

"accounting can offer you a lot of insight into the character of management. [...] Obviously, if some prepaid expense, deferred asset accounts start building up suspiciously high, and inventories look out of line, you know, with sales [...] you want to look twice at companies like that" **[M1995-064]**. The rows add their kin:

- **Weak accounting where it is visible.** "If a company still does not expense options, or if its pension assumptions are fanciful, watch out. [...] There is seldom just one cockroach in the kitchen" **[L2002-039]**.
- **Predictions and the habit of beating them.** Predicting growth rates is "both deceptive and dangerous" **[L2000-037]**; predictions "corrode CEO behavior" and "can turn fudging into fraud" **[L2000-039]**; "we become downright incredulous if they consistently reach their declared targets" **[L2002-041]**; beating expectations: "It requires no talent to manipulate numbers: Only a deep desire to deceive is required" **[L2022-006]**; also **[M2005-038]**, **[M2010-001]**.
- **Adjusted earnings featured.** "a management that regularly attempts to wave away very real costs by highlighting "adjusted per-share earnings" makes us nervous. That's because bad behavior is contagious" **[L2016-006]**; also **[M2016-095]**.
- **Big baths, merger reserves, reserves that move with a stock sale.** "When deals occur in which liabilities are increased immediately and substantially, simple logic says that at least one of those virtues must have been lacking" **[L1998-034]**; the abuse is in "the subsequent release back into earnings" **[M1999-048]**; reserves "down very suspiciously" before an offering; "They give themselves away. But I can’t give you a checklist of 40 items" **[M2013-085]**; also **[L1998-033]**, **[M2001-027]**.
- **Profits that may not exist.** "when you see a company with lots of gains on securitization, you ought to get a little suspicious" **[M2003-101]**; both sides of one contract booking a profit **[M2013-091]**, marks on both sides that "don’t equate out to zero" **[M2007-037]**, **[M2022-072]**; assets "good until reached for" **[M2008-064]**; earnings that rise "as your credit is destroyed" **[M2009-003]**.
- **Clear speech.** A plus: a management "that doesn’t care about the accounting but does explain to you in clear terms what’s going on" **[M1994-018]**; "we expect a company's CEO to explain in his or her own words what's happening" **[L2000-036]**.

The tells do not always read as fraud. Munger: "they’re not being deliberately fraudulent, because they’re deluded" **[M2013-087]**; Buffett on decent neighbours who "play games with any number that came to them" **[M2022-070]**; and the practice spreads by imitation: "Bad accounting drives out good" **[L1998-030]**; also **[L1998-029]**, **[M1999-049]**. The verdict is on the accounts either way.

### Why confusion is a STOP

"when the accounting confuses you, I would just tend to forget about it as a company. I mean, it’s probably — it may well be intentional, and in any event, you don’t want to go near it. I — we have never had any great investment results from companies whose accounting we regarded as suspect" **[M1995-063]**. Confusion is treated as a signal of purpose, "people don’t obfuscate with numbers, usually, without a purpose" **[M2003-029]**, and suspicion is enough: "If you ever get suspicious about accounting, just go onto the next company" **[M1995-065]**. On EBITDA talk the stop is stated as a count: "the number of times we’re going to buy into a company [...] where people are talking about EBITDA, is going to be about zero" **[M2002-026]**. The cost is named and accepted: "that may mean we pass up a whole lot of other opportunities, too" **[M2002-026]**. Where revenues and expenses are not matched "on something close to cash in the short-term", the opportunity for games is larger **[M1995-065]**; the speakers name construction in progress and financial institutions as "particularly, probably, prone to it" **[M2013-086]**. Whether a financial institution's condition can be known at all is the merge's separate theme (M20) and is not drafted here.

The STOP has an edge the rows draw: a management merely "preoccupied with accounting considerations" is "a negative", but "we can’t afford to use it as a total exclusionary factor" **[M1994-018]**. Short of confusion or suspicion, Q4 is a WEIGHING: the earnings are recast and carried forward.

### What it rules OUT

Companies whose accounting confuses or is suspect **[M1995-063]**, **[M2003-029]**; EBITDA as earnings and managements that talk it **[M2002-026]**, **[R1996-023]**; earnings that leave out stock pay, recurring restructuring or catastrophe losses **[L2015-003]**, **[L2016-007]**, **[L2002-002]**; managements that habitually make or beat their numbers **[L2002-041]**, **[L2022-006]**; returns on equity made by shrinking equity or by leverage **[M1998-017]**, **[M2001-054]**; growth rates whose base year was picked **[L2005-003]**.

### Tensions carried

- **Earnings plus amortization, against depreciation as a real cost** **[M1997-015]** against **[R1996-023]**. READER: compatible, since the row adding back amortization is the same speaker who refuses to equate depreciation with amortization **[R1996-023]**.
- **Depreciation as a proxy, against depreciation inadequate.** "not inappropriate in most companies" **[M1998-127]** against the railroads **[L2015-004]**, **[M2016-092]**. SPEAKER: bridged by "most".
- **Goodwill.** Not amortized "in the ordinary case", yet Munger says "there would be plenty of cases" where amortization "would be rational and, in fact, should be required" **[M1999-037]** against **[M1999-035]**; Buffett adds back amortization "with some trepidation" **[L2015-003]**. OPEN.
- **Positive equity.** "nothing magic about a company having a positive shareholders’ equity" **[M1995-098]** against Munger, "I think there is something to be said for a positive shareholders’ equity" **[M1995-100]**. OPEN.
- **Preoccupation not exclusionary, confusion fatal** **[M1994-018]** against **[M1995-063]**. READER: two different objects, a management's interest in its accounts and the analyst's inability to read them.

### How Q3 and Q4 feed Q7

Q7 asks how much cash, how sure and how soon. Q4 supplies the cash figure the speakers would accept: "regular pretax earnings", not EBITDA **[M2012-034]**, after "interest, taxes, depreciation, amortization and all forms of compensation" **[L2021-003]**, less any shortfall of depreciation against what keeps the business in place **[L2015-004]**, plus amortization that does not deplete **[L2012-003]**. Q3 supplies what must be put back to get it: the maintenance need as a stated guess **[M2000-144]**, the growth outlays and what each dollar of them earns **[L1999-024]**, **[M2001-019]**, and the rate at which retained capital is redeployed, which "interacts" with the price paid **[M1995-113]**. Where reinvestment eats the reported figure, the cash the owner receives "will regularly fall considerably short" of reported earnings **[L2023-009]**. Q3 also caps the arithmetic Q7 may use: no growth rate above the discount rate carried forever **[M1997-095]**, and no result that traces to an absurdity **[M1999-067]**. And Q4's figures are only the beginning, since "to figure out that answer, you have to understand something about business" **[M1994-079]**.

### Steering noticed

The auto-loaded six put return on capital and accounting nowhere as questions of their own and fold owner earnings into valuation; I felt that pull when deciding how much of Q4 to send forward to Q7, and kept the recast earnings here because the rows treat the accounts as a test of the business and of management's character **[M1995-064]**, not only as a valuation input. The six also carry a phrase, *owner earnings*, that I nearly used as my own term; no instance of that phrase was found in the v5 ledger, so the section uses the rows' own words: "operating earnings" **[M2022-003]**, "normalized earning power" **[M2018-045]**, "true economic earnings" **[L2015-004]**. The six's habit of a sector method for insurers pulled me toward a separate float paragraph; I left float to the merge's M05. Within the rows, the order of the four tests in Q3 and the decision to call the low-return business the nearest thing to a stop rather than a stop are mine. Placing the Coca-Cola, See's and BNSF illustrations was easy because the rows repeat them; a reader should know that the capital-heavy rows (2007 onward) are nearly all about Berkshire's own utilities and railroad, and become tests of another company only by inversion. The blockquote attribution format, with its dash, was set by the drafting instruction; my own prose carries none.
