## Q6 — WHAT WILL THEY DO WITH THE MONEY AND WITH THE OWNERS? WEIGHING.

**The question in the speakers' words.** The 1993 letter names it as a factor of its own, split off from the manager's
ability to run the business:

> "2) The certainty with which management can be evaluated, both as to its ability to realize the full potential of the business and to wisely employ its cash flows; 3) The certainty with which management can be counted on to channel the rewards from the business to the shareholders rather than to itself" **[L1993-020]** (1993)

And the meetings return to it as the central job and the central judgment:

> "it is, if not the key function of the CEO, it’s one of two or three key functions at say 80 or 90 percent of all companies. And if you can’t do it yourself, you’re going to make a lot of mistakes." **[M1994-082]** (1994)

> "And then the biggest judgment you have to make is how well capital will be deployed in the future. [...] the question becomes, “What do we do with the money, as it comes in?” And that will have a huge impact on the value 10 years from now." **[M2001-111]** (2001)

**Why it has its own place.** Three reasons, each from the rows. First, L1993-020 lists the employment of cash flows and
the channelling of rewards as items separate from the evaluation of the business, and puts them before price **[L1993-020]**.
Second, the speakers rank it: "test number one" **[M1998-033]**, "the biggest judgment" **[M2001-111]**, "The first law of
capital allocation" **[L2011-003]**. Third, the speakers say it can be judged apart from the present value of the
businesses: "it is not only a question what the present businesses are worth. It’s a judgment on the efficiency or the
effectiveness with which retained earnings will be used." **[M2007-031]** (2007). A business can pass Q2 and Q3 and still
have its surplus spent badly; L1994-016 says the really good business is precisely the one that throws off more than it
can use **[L1994-016]**, so the better the business, the larger the question.

### Part A: the money

#### The retention test

The rule, stated in every era:

> "And any time we thought that we weren’t going to get more than a dollar of value per dollar retained, that, obviously, would be the course to follow." **[M1994-061]** (1994)

> "We test the wisdom of retaining earnings by assessing whether retention, over time, delivers shareholders at least $1 of market value for each $1 retained." **[R1995-009]** (1995)

> "Obviously, if we can keep a dollar and it becomes, on a present-value basis, worth more than a dollar, it’s foolish to pay it out." **[M2000-049]** (2000)

> "The test about whether to pay dividends is whether you can continue to create more than one dollar of value for every dollar you retain." **[M2008-104]** (2008)

> "over time, if we’re going to keep your money, we have to earn a better-than-average return on that money we keep and that has to translate into the stock selling at a premium over the money we retain from you." **[M2011-072]** (2011)

The test has two halves, keep or return, and then the best use:

> "The first question is, is when you have capital, is it better to keep it or return it to shareholders? It’s better to return it to shareholders when you cannot create more than a dollar of value with that capital. That’s test number one. And if you pass that threshold, [...] then you simply look around for the thing that you feel the surest about, and that promises the greatest return weighted for that certainty." **[M1998-033]** (1998)

The yardstick is opportunity cost, not a formula. "The cost of every deal we do is measured by the second best deal
that’s around at a given time, including more — doing more of some of the things we’re already in." **[M2001-084]** (2001).
Munger: "it’s your alternatives that are competing for the use of your time or money" **[M2003-117]** (2003); Buffett:
"we measure alternatives against each other, and we measure alternatives against dividends, and we measure alternatives
against repurchase of shares." **[M2003-116]** (2003).

The check is made after the fact and over a span: "if after three or four years, you’ve found that the dollars we’ve
retained hasn’t created more of that in value, then the presumption becomes very strong at that point that we should
start paying out money." **[M1998-110]** (1998). The 1995 report put the span at "a five-year rolling basis" **[R1995-009]**,
and the 2009 report rewrote the test because a falling market could fail a good allocator: "(1) during the period did our
book-value gain exceed the performance of the S&P; and (2) did our stock consistently sell at a premium to book" **[R2009-002]**
(2009). The speakers also name the retainer's bias: "almost any management that wants to retain money is going to
rationalize it" **[M1998-110]**, and of themselves, "there’ll be a certain — denial — we’ll go through" **[M1997-094]** (1997).

**The test as the analyst runs it.** Take the earnings the company has kept over a span of years (three or four in
**[M1998-110]**, five in **[R1995-009]** and **[R2009-002]**). Ask whether value per share rose by at least that much,
read in market value (**[R1995-009]**, **[M2011-072]**) and against intrinsic value, since the market leg alone misleads
when prices fall **[R2009-002]**. Then ask the forward question: "Can you keep using all of the capital you generate,
effectively, for a very long time?" **[M2010-097]** (2010). Where the business cannot use its earnings, as with See's,
"it would be an enormous mistake for See’s Candy to retain money" **[M2008-104]**. Where a manager keeps capital in a
declining operation, the 2014 letter expects him to stay: "A CEO with capital employed in a declining operation seldom
elects to massively redeploy that capital into unrelated activities." **[L2014-016]** (2014).

#### Buybacks

**Only below value, and only with money the business does not need.**

> "There is only one combination of facts that makes it advisable for a company to repurchase its shares: First, the company has available funds -- cash plus sensible borrowing capacity -- beyond the near-term needs of the business and, second, finds its stock selling in the market below its intrinsic value, conservatively-calculated. To this we add a caveat: Shareholders should have been supplied all the information they need for estimating that value." **[L1999-023]** (1999)

> "Continuing shareholders are hurt unless shares are purchased below intrinsic value. The first law of capital allocation [...] is that what is smart at one price is dumb at another." **[L2011-003]** (2011)

The needs of the business come first even when the stock is cheap: "One is when a business both needs all its available
money to protect or expand its own operations and is also uncomfortable adding further debt. Here, the internal need for
funds should take priority." **[L2016-003]** (2016). The second exception is a better use elsewhere **[L2016-003]**; the
same two tests appear at the meeting: "if you’ve got other things that are more intelligent, you don’t do it. And if it
isn’t intelligent on an absolute basis, you also don’t do it." **[M2022-083]** (2022).

**The owner's view against the manager's.** The speakers frame the buyback as one partner buying out another:

> "If you had a partnership and the partner wanted to sell out to you at 120 percent of what the business is worth, you’d say forget it. And if he’d want to sell out to you for 80 percent of what it’s worth, you’d take it. It’s not complicated." **[M2015-074]** (2015)

> "The real thing is to buy stock — repurchase shares — only when you think you’re doing it at a price where the remaining shareholders have had — are worth more the moment after you repurchased it than they were the moment before." **[M2019-002]** (2019)

The manager's view, as they report it, is a fixed sum, a show of confidence or a prop: "repurchase announcements almost
never refer to a price above which repurchases will be eschewed" **[L2016-002]** (2016); "dictated by management's desire to
"show confidence" or be in fashion rather than by a desire to enhance per-share value" **[L1999-028]** (1999); "a lot of
share buying is designed to, sort of, prop the stock price. In other words, it’s not bargain-seeking." **[M2006-041]** (2006).
Munger on the motive: "if you’re repurchasing stock, just a bull it higher, it’s deeply immoral. But if you’re repurchasing
stock because it’s a fair thing to do in the interest of your existing shareholders, it’s a highly moral act." **[M2021-031]**
(2021). Buying to cover options is ruled out by name **[M2014-008]**, and so is buying to prevent dilution: "dilution by
itself is a negative and buying back your stock at too high a price is another negative. So it has to be related to
valuation." **[M2016-049]** (2016).

**What a buyback at a high price does.** "If you’re repurchasing shares above a rationally calculated intrinsic value, you
are harming your shareholders, just as if you issue shares beneath that figure" **[M1996-013]** (1996). "if you buy a dollar
bill for 90 cents, you’re doing your shareholders a favor. And if you buy it for $1.10, you are doing them no favor at all."
**[M2014-008]** (2014). Companies that issue options and then repurchase "sell low and then they buy high" **[M1998-027]** (1998).

**The speakers' own practice.** A demonstrable margin, not a fine one: "it should be by a demonstrable margin." **[M2016-048]**
(2016); "we would not want to buy a dollar bill for 95 cents, or 94 cents, or 93 cents." **[M1999-101]** (1999). Sellers told:
"we want to be very sure that every shareholder that sells to us knows that we think that it’s dramatically — or
significantly — undervalued" **[M2012-010]** (2012). A floor of cash kept first: "as long as we don’t take our cash position
below 20 billion" **[M2012-010]**. Never a prop: "We will not, however, prop the stock at any level." **[L2019-010]** (2019).
And the error they will accept: "We will never buy in our stock at a silly price. We may make a mistake by not buying it at
a cheap price." **[M2009-049]** (2009). For the analyst, a target's programme is read against these: does it name a price
**[L2016-002]**, does it serve the business's needs first **[L2016-003]**, and are the sellers told **[L1999-023]**.

#### Issuance and deals

**Value given against value got.** The rule is the same whatever the currency:

> "Only if we receive less in value than we give. That’s the key to it. [...] If we give 200 million of cash for a business that’s worth a 150 million, we are worse off. We may not have issued a share of stock. But we have diluted the value of your stock if we do that." **[M1995-001]** (1995)

> "Too often CEOs seem blind to an elementary reality: The intrinsic value of the shares you give in an acquisition must not be greater than the intrinsic value of the business you receive. [...] You can't get rich trading a hundred-dollar bill for eight tens (even if your advisor has handed you an expensive "fairness" opinion endorsing that swap)." **[L2014-012]** (2014)

> "Most stock deals, they think about what they’re getting and they don’t think about what they’re giving. [...] I’ve heard a lot of discussion about dilution and when dilution will be overcome and all that sort of thing, but that is not the question." **[M2007-123]** (2007)

Berkshire's own rule of issue: "We will issue common stock only when we receive as much in business value as we give. This
rule applies to all forms of issuance" **[R1995-010]** (1995). Why acquirers lose: "the acquirer typically gives up more
intrinsic value than it receives." **[L1994-015]** (1994). A premium is defensible only if "a) its stock is overvalued
relative to the acquiree's or b) the two enterprises will earn more combined than they would separately" **[L1997-031]**
(1997), and the speakers "never count on synergies" **[L2016-008]** (2016).

**Stock deals.** "When a management proudly acquires another company for stock, the shareholders of the acquirer are
concurrently selling part of their interest in everything they own." **[L2005-004]** (2005). The base rate is set by the
forces around the chief executive: "So there are all these forces that push toward deals, and if you try to push toward
deals, you’re going to get a lot of dumb deals." **[M2014-076]** (2014). The serial issuer is a warning sign of character:
"At both BPL and Berkshire, we have never invested in companies that are hell-bent on issuing shares. That behavior is one
of the surest indicators of a promotion-minded management, weak accounting, a stock that is overpriced and [...] outright
dishonesty." **[L2014-015]** (2014).

**The right tests of a deal.** Per-share value years out, not the first year: "it is our judgment on a 10-year basis."
**[M1999-061]** (1999); "we have rejected many merger and purchase opportunities that would have boosted current and
near-term earnings but that would have reduced per-share intrinsic value." **[L1994-014]** (1994). Debt does not rescue a bad
price: "even a high-priced deal will usually boost per-share earnings if it is debt-financed. At Berkshire, in contrast, we
evaluate acquisitions on an all-equity basis" **[L2017-004]** (2017). And afterwards: "Post mortems of acquisitions, in which
reality is honestly compared to the original projections, are rare in American boardrooms. They should instead be standard
practice." **[L2014-013]** (2014).

**The one STOP the rows carry in Part A.**

> "If we wouldn't dream of selling Berkshire in its entirety at the current market price, why in the world should we "sell" a significant part of the company at that same inadequate price by issuing our stock in a merger? [...] If shares of a prospective acquirer are selling below their intrinsic value, it's impossible for that buyer to make a sensible deal in an all-stock deal. You simply can't exchange an undervalued stock for a fully-valued one without hurting your shareholders." **[L2009-019]** (2009)

Stated as a stop for the analyst: a company whose stock sits below its intrinsic value and pays for an acquisition wholly
in that stock has made a deal the rows call "impossible" to make sensibly **[L2009-019]**. No further weighing of that deal
is done. Everything else in Part A is graded (see below).

#### Dividends and the use of cash

**When a dividend is right.** When the dollar cannot be used at more than a dollar: "a company that expects to regularly earn
more than it can profitably employ in its business, should be paying out dividends." **[M2004-089]** (2004); "there’s no
reason to keep a dollar in the business that’s worth 90 cents if you keep it in the business." **[M2000-050]** (2000). Not by
ratio: "the idea of paying out 20 percent of your earnings, or 10 percent, or 30 percent of earnings in dividends strikes us
as nuts." **[M2000-051]** (2000). The speakers prefer, where retention passes the test, that owners sell a little stock
rather than receive a dividend, because "dividends impose a specific cash-out policy upon all shareholders" **[L2012-014]**
(2012). Whatever the policy, "dividend policy should always be clear, consistent and rational." **[L2012-015]** (2012).

**Cash as the default, not the goal.** "Cash at Berkshire is a residual. [...] if we have cash around, it’s simply because we
haven’t found anything we like to do" **[M1995-057]** (1995); "if there’s nothing smart to do, cash is the default option."
**[M2003-072]** (2003); "the option is cash and not doing something that we really are excited about." **[M2007-105]** (2007).
Idle cash is not to be cured by a marginal deal: "we'll try to resist the temptation to do something marginal simply because
we are long on cash." **[L1993-007]** (1993); "We like even less doing dumb deals because we do them forever." **[M2006-028]**
(2006). A description of cash as a call option: no instance found in the v5 ledger by a search for that phrase. Cash also
has a floor that is a matter of survival, not of use: "available cash or credit — is a lot like oxygen" **[M2014-020]** (2014);
that row belongs to the standing rule and Q9, and is named here only so the floor is not read as idle money.

**The order of uses the rows give.** First the business itself: "A company's management should first examine reinvestment
possibilities offered by its current business [...] to otherwise widen the economic moat separating the company from its
competitors." **[L2012-011]** (2012). The 2021 letter gives Berkshire's full order: "The first is always front and center in
our minds: Increase the long-term earning power of Berkshire's controlled businesses through internal growth or by making
acquisitions. [...] Our second choice is to buy non-controlling part-interests [...] Our final path to value creation is to
repurchase Berkshire shares." **[L2021-005]** (2021). The 2001 meeting gives the gate before the order: keep or pay out
first, then dividend or repurchase **[M2001-082]**. The order is subordinate to price: the 2016 letter lets a cheap stock
yield to "far greater value" elsewhere **[L2016-003]**, and M2003-116 measures every use against every other.

#### Mistakes the speakers narrate here

From group 9 (stock-paid deals, value given over value got) and group 12 (retention and buybacks) of section 3 of the merged map.

- **Dexter.** "I clearly made a mistake in paying what I did for Dexter in 1993. Furthermore, I compounded that mistake in a
  huge way by using Berkshire shares in payment." **[L2000-034]** (2000); "one of the dumbest deals in the history of the
  world. And I did it all by myself." **[M2007-124]** (2007); "Trading shares of a wonderful business [...] for ownership of
  a so-so business irreparably destroys value." **[L2014-011]** (2014); "almost any time we’ve issued shares, it’s been a
  mistake." **[M2015-026]** (2015). The general lesson came first: "when I've issued stock, I've cost you money." **[L1997-029]** (1997).
- **BNSF's stock leg.** Accepted in 2009 as "a close one" **[L2009-021]**, called a mistake in 2012: "we would not use our stock
  now, and we wouldn’t even use it for 30 or 40 percent of some deal." **[M2012-021]** (2012); "I could’ve always gone to the
  market and repurchased a bunch of stocks subsequently" **[M2014-030]** (2014).
- **The small value-destroying deal.** "We need to show that we are in the hunt. Besides, it's only a small deal," and
  Munger's Chihuahua **[L2009-023]** (2009).
- **Deals bought on projections and myths.** "In aggregate, the 10 earned one-quarter of what they were projected to earn"
  **[M2002-034]** (2002); Xerox and Crum & Forster **[M2010-067]** (2010); GEICO's acquisitions took "their eyes off the ball"
  **[M2014-075]** (2014); Gillette and Duracell, "what Gillette received in business value in this acquisition was not

- **Retention in a dying business.** Textiles: "The dumbest thing I could have done was to pursue "opportunities" to improve

- **Buybacks not made.** "I have erred in not making repurchases. My appraisal of Berkshire's value was then too conservative

- **The test badly written.** "I should have written the "five-year rolling basis" sentence differently" **[R2009-002]** (2009);

- **A dividend paid.** "I paid out 10 cents a share back in the 1960s which was a big mistake" **[M2012-100]** (2012).

#### Why Part A is a WEIGHING

The tests are stated as judgments, not thresholds. "you don’t need a mathematical answer" **[M1998-033]**; "That’s
subjective, but any given decision like that is subjective. Over time, you get an objective test" **[M2000-050]**; "And you
never know the answer to that." **[M2001-082]**. The retention test runs "over time" **[R1995-009]**, and its failure raises
a presumption **[M1998-110]** or shifts a burden of proof **[M2005-021]**, words of weight, not of exclusion. The buyback
rules carry their own allowance for error: "they’ll make mistakes in that, but you make mistakes in a lot of businesses.
But over — that should be the guiding principle." **[M2020-044]** (2020). The one place the rows close the door is the
all-stock deal by an undervalued acquirer **[L2009-019]**, stated above as a STOP. A company that has made such a deal is
not cleared by the rest of Part A.

#### Tensions carried

From section 4 of the merged map, "Retention, buybacks and issuance". Not resolved here.

- **[OPEN]** Buybacks in a super business "almost an appeal regardless of price" **[M1997-131]**, and "even at prices that may
  give you nose bleeds" **[M1998-027]**, against "above a rationally calculated intrinsic value, you are harming your
  shareholders" **[M1996-013]** and Munger's "a price so high it would be foolish" **[M1998-028]**.
- **[OPEN]** A dollar kept with no present use, "the answer is no", held on the odds of a use "in the next few years"
  **[M1998-111]**, against the three-to-four-year check **[M1998-110]**.
- **[SPEAKER]** Pay out what cannot be employed **[M2004-089]** against Berkshire's retained cash; reconciled by the expected
  deployment and the burden of proof **[M2005-021]**.
- **[SPEAKER]** BNSF's stock leg accepted **[L2009-021]** and later called a mistake **[M2012-021]**; "the mix was OK".
- **[OPEN]** Teledyne's issue-high, buy-back-low cycle told as "a sensational record" **[M2011-034]** and as treating the
  shareholder group "as somebody to be taken advantage of" **[M2013-027]**.
- **[SPEAKER]** Large buybacks need sellers who misprice the stock **[M2022-082]**, against wanting to keep the present
  owners **[M2022-053]**.
- **[OPEN]** Berkshire can move cash "anyplace that it makes sense" **[M2003-121]**, while "the batting average of American
  industry in redeploying capital has been" poor **[M2003-123]**: the same act praised in the speakers and doubted in others.
- **[OPEN]** Volatility the buyer's friend **[L1993-018]**, yet "we see no way that volatility helps our shareholders as a
  group" **[M1994-013]**, which bears on whether a manager should welcome a mispriced stock to buy in.
- **[DRAFTER, not in the merged map]** L2009-019 is named as the one STOP, but L2014-015 states an exclusion in the past
  tense, "we have never invested in companies that are hell-bent on issuing shares" **[L2014-015]**. Whether serial issuance
  is a second STOP, or a sign to be weighed under Q5's integrity test, is left to the reconciliation.

#### Steering noticed

The auto-loaded six put capital allocation inside a manager question ("ARE THEY HONEST, AND ARE THEY RATIONAL?"), and I
felt it pull in two places: toward reading the buyback rows as evidence of rationality rather than as a test of the money,
and toward letting the retention check stand in for the six's "what would prove me wrong, and when do I sell". I kept the
check where the rows put it, a periodic test of a retaining management **[M1998-110]**, **[R2009-002]**, and did not write it
as a sell trigger. The brief also steered: it named one STOP, and the rows offer a second candidate **[L2014-015]**, which I
carried as a tension instead of either adding or suppressing it. The brief's phrase "cash as an option" is not the rows'
phrase; I used their words, "the default option" **[M2003-072]**. Most buyback and issuance rows are Berkshire speaking of
itself (M2009-049, M2012-010, R1995-010); turning them into tests of another company is an inversion, as the merged map
warns under its Q6 entry.
