## Q5 — WHO RUNS IT? Able, honest, in love with the business rather than the money, and the same once paid. STOP on integrity, WEIGHING on ability.

The question, in the words of the row that asks it whole: "do we really want to be in partnership with this person and count on them to behave in the future when they don’t own the business, as they behaved in the past when they do own the business. And that stops a fair number of deals." **[M2015-005]**

> "Well, I think you judge management by **two yardsticks**. One is **how well they run the business** [...] You have to have some understanding of **the hand they were dealt** when they themselves got a chance to play the hand. [...] And then the second thing you want to figure out is **how well that they treat their owners**." — **[M1994-008]**, 1994 annual meeting, AM

> "I don’t know how to tell someone else how to select managers using those criteria: **do they love the business or do they love the money?** It’s very, very important. I mean, **it’s crucial.**" — **[M2000-098]**, 2000 annual meeting, PM

> "And the decision we have to make is, **are they going to have the same passion for the business after they hand us the stock certificate and we hand them the money?** [...] And if we’re wrong on that, **no contract is going to save us.**" — **[M2009-072]**, 2009 annual meeting, PM

> "And that **trustworthiness is more important than the brains.** It’s not that they don’t have the brains, but **we wouldn’t hire anybody, no matter how able, if we didn’t trust them.**" — **[M2015-047]**, 2015 annual meeting, PM

The rows ask four things of the people: can they run it, will they treat the owners right, do they love the work more than the cheque, and will that survive the cheque. Integrity is the one the rows make absolute. Ability they weigh, and they weigh it against the business, which they put first: "we still look to the underlying business, though." **[M1996-037]**

---

### The two yardsticks

The first yardstick is the record read against the circumstances. Buffett reads "what they’ve accomplished and what their competitors have accomplished, and seeing how they have allocated capital over time" **[M1994-008]**; the capital half is Q6's subject (see Q6). The second is the treatment of owners, and the two travel together: "how often the ones that, in my view, are the poor managers also turn out to be the ones that really don’t think that much about the shareholders, too. The two often go hand in hand." **[M1994-009]**

The 1993 letter states the same pair as two of the five factors of real risk: "The certainty with which management can be evaluated, both as to its ability to realize the full potential of the business and to wisely employ its cash flows" and "The certainty with which management can be counted on to channel the rewards from the business to the shareholders rather than to itself" **[L1993-020]**. Munger names the cost of failing either: "One, the guy favors himself at the expense of the shareholders, and the other is he’s — he does foolish things. [...] Either way, it’s very costly to you, as the shareholder." **[M1995-109]**

The formula that carries both is the one-line purchase rule: "businesses that have good underlying economics and are run by **honest and able people**" **[L1994-001]**. The traits under the two words recur in every era: "brains and energy and integrity" **[M1997-115]**; "integrity, intelligence, and experience, and dedication" **[M1998-062]**; "brains, passion and integrity" **[L2007-014]**.

### Integrity, the STOP

Integrity is not one trait among the others; without it the others turn against the owner.

> "We’re looking for energy. And we’re looking for integrity. And we tell them, **if they don’t have the last, the first two will kill you.** Because if you hire somebody without integrity, you really want them to be dumb and lazy, don’t you?" — **[M2005-003]**, 2005 annual meeting, AM

> "His instructions included this warning: "**Never deal with a rascal under the expectation that you can prevent him from cheating you.**" [...] People are not that easy to read. **Sincerity and empathy can easily be faked.**" — **[L2023-003]**, 2023 letter

Kiewit's version is repeated in 2016, with the front-page test added: someone "pursuing a course of action which, if put on the front page, you know, would make you very unhappy, you can get in a lot of trouble. It may take a while." **[M2016-033]** The rule is older than the phrase: "you can’t make a good deal with a bad person. And you can — that means we just forget about it. I mean, we don’t try and protect ourselves by contracts" **[M1995-084]**. And it is applied on doubt, not on proof: "If you’ve got doubts, forget it." **[M2013-088]**; "we rule out 90 percent of the times. And we may be wrong about a fair number that we’re ruling out. The important thing is whether the ones we’re ruling in we’re right about." **[M2007-092]**

**What "no contract is going to save us" means** **[M2009-072]**. The rows offer no legal or structural safeguard for character. After the sale "they have no financial need to work. They have to want to work. And we can’t stand there with a whip. We don’t have any contracts at Berkshire. They don’t work" **[M2005-002]**; passion "Can’t be done by contract." **[M2008-005]**; "Our model is a seamless web of trust that’s deserved on both sides." **[M2009-073]** Due diligence cannot reach it either: "how are you going to check that as — by due diligence, you know?" **[M2016-067]**. Nor can ownership change it afterwards: "We think it’s like marrying somebody to change them. It just doesn’t work very well." **[M2012-050]**; "you better not count on the fact that you’re going to change their course of action." **[M2009-009]** So the only protection is the judgment made before buying, and a doubt there is a no.

**The tells of dishonesty the rows name.**
- Too good to be true: "they usually are people that tell you things that are too good to be true, for one thing." **[M2002-028]**; "we’re deeply suspicious when the proposition is too good to be true." **[M2007-091]**
- Reports that dance: "We can usually tell when somebody’s dancing around something, or where their — when the reports are essentially a little dishonest, or biased" **[M1995-111]**; the industry's key figure left out, or "touched on, it was done in a dishonest manner" **[M2007-082]**; numbers obscured on purpose, "people don’t obfuscate with numbers, usually, without a purpose." **[M2003-029]**
- A management fixed on its stock price: "when we see the price of a stock posted in the lobby of the headquarters or something, you know, things like that make us nervous." **[M2004-067]**; "if you’re trying to get your stock to be overpriced, you’re very likely to cheat on your earnings and cheat on projections. Cheat on everything." **[M2014-010]**
- Taking credit: the people wanted "don’t take credit for things that they didn’t do" **[M1998-167]**, repeated as "they don’t claim credit for things they don’t do" **[M2015-045]**.
- Owners as marks: leaders who "view shareholders as patsies, not partners" **[L2001-002]**; the contrast is those who "have gotten rich with their shareholders and not off their shareholders" **[M2003-055]**.
- What they talk and laugh about: "people give themselves away fairly often. [...] what they regard as important and not important, there are a lot of clues" **[M2007-090]**; "It’s like they got a sign on their chest that just says, “Jerk. Jerk. Jerk.”" **[M1997-152]**
- Professed, not held: "everybody mouths the integrity, even when it’s lacking." **[M2010-069]**

Reputation is not a tell of honesty. Lloyd's "had this terrific reputation — behavioral reputation — over centuries. And I think that they coasted for a while on that." **[M1995-066]**; Royal Dutch "would have been near the top of my list" before "the oil reserves phonied for years" **[M2004-068]**. Some fields fail the test as a whole: "there are whole fields that you can just quit playing because it looks like there’s too much fraud in it." **[M2002-025]**

### Love of the business, and after the sale

The test is the primacy of the love, not the absence of interest in money: "Nothing wrong with liking the money. In fact, we’d be a little disappointed if most of them didn’t like the money. But in terms of whether the primacy is loving the money or loving the business, that’s very important to us." **[M2000-053]** The reason is practical: "If they love the money, there’s nothing wrong with that, but they probably wouldn’t be running the business for us a year or two down the road." **[M2003-060]** Munger ranks it above talent: "I would argue that probably the passion is more important than the brainpower." **[M2003-063]**

The cheque is the hazard. "three times out of four, thereabouts, the manager is the owner and is receiving tens of millions, maybe hundreds of millions of dollars. So they don’t have to work. And we have to decide in that time when we meet them whether they love the business or love money." **[M1998-061]** The question becomes whether the behaviour survives the payment: "will they be feeling the same way after the deal as they did the day before the deal?" **[M2009-096]**; "we count on them to run the business with as much enthusiasm after they have the money as they did before." **[M2013-084]** The speakers concede there is no instrument for it: "assessing whether he’s going to behave differently in the future in running that business than he has in the past when he owned it, that’s incredibly important, but there’s no checklist in the world that’s going to answer that." **[M2016-066]** Love cannot be supplied after the fact: "you can’t put that into somebody. But we do recognize it when it’s there." **[M2009-098]**

The seller's conduct at the sale is read as evidence:

> "We find it meaningful when an owner **cares about whom he sells to**. [...] When this emotional attachment exists, it signals that important qualities will likely be found within the business: **honest accounting, pride of product, respect for customers, and a loyal group of associates** [...] When an owner auctions off his business, exhibiting a total lack of interest in what follows, you will frequently find that it has been **dressed up for sale**, particularly when the seller is a "financial owner."" — **[L2000-008]**, 2000 letter

The reverse case is put bluntly: a business offered for resale "it’s a piece of meat to them. And if it’s a piece of meat to them, you know, what am I going to do with it?" **[M2003-061]** The positive signs are small acts: a seller who "shared a meaningful part of his proceeds from the sale with a large number of his associates" **[L1995-007]**; one who "cared about who bought it, and was realistic as to price" **[L2001-004]**; one who did not want to "leave the employees behind" **[M2002-030]**. For a hired executive the same love reads as "What can I do for Berkshire," not, "What can Berkshire do for me?" **[M2017-070]**, as being "all in" for the company, not for himself **[L2014-028]**, and in what he wants for himself: the banker who lost interest when the new building had to be shorter than a competitor's "taught me a lot about the guy’s motivations in life." **[M2023-056]**

### Ability, the WEIGHING

The rows rank ability below the business, in plain words:

> "**The really great business is one that doesn’t require good management.** I mean, that is a terrific business. And the poor business is one that can only succeed, or even survive, with great management." — **[M1996-037]**, 1996 annual meeting, PM

> "And Tom Murphy, you know, he said **the real key was buying the right business.** [...] we could have brought in Tom Murphy and told him his job was to run the textile business. And it would have done a little bit better. **But it still would have failed.**" — **[M2024-030]**, 2024 annual meeting, AM

"If you have a big enough moat, you don’t need as much management." **[M1999-106]** "there are some businesses so good that they’ll easily stand a lot of folly in the managerial suite." **[M1995-110]** Ability therefore enters as a weight on value, not as a gate: "The moat and the management are part of the valuation process, in that they enter into our thinking as to the degree of certainty that we attribute to the stream of income" **[M1999-104]**; "if the CEO's talents or motives are suspect, today's value must be discounted." **[L2010-002]**

The weight is heavy where the business is weak or commodity-like. "Buying a retailer without good management is like buying the Eiffel Tower without an elevator." **[L1995-006]** For an insurer, "No installed base, key patents, critical real estate or natural resource position protects an insurer's competitive position. [...] The critical variables, therefore, are managerial brains, discipline and integrity." **[L2003-014]** For a whole-business purchase, management must already be there: "Management in place (we can't supply it)" **[R1995-014]**. And the wrong person can sink a good one: "the number one risk factor is that this business gets the wrong management." **[M2021-042]**

**What ability shows in, as the rows read it.**
- A long record, not a promise: "we don’t like banjo hitters who suddenly proclaim that they can become power hitters." **[M1996-038]**; "we’ve actually seen they batted .350, or whatever it may be, in the major leagues." **[M2005-039]**
- How the record was made: "we’ve always been more concerned about how our record is achieved than the precise record itself." **[M2012-089]**; "a product of sound thinking rather than simply being in tune with a trend or simply just being lucky." **[M2013-068]**
- Facing reality: "tell us the truth but tell themselves the truth, which is even more important." **[M2000-153]**; "He never engages in wishful thinking or otherwise distorts reality" **[L2000-018]**.
- Focus: "Loss of focus is what most worries Charlie and me when we contemplate investing in businesses that in general look outstanding." **[L1996-033]**; "He has no ego compulsions forcing him into some sort of action." **[M1996-092]**
- Sense in everything, not brilliance sometimes: "everything Tony says and does makes sense." **[M2000-130]**; "IQ does not always translate into rationality and behavioral success or wisdom" **[M2020-049]**.
- Knowing the limits: "It's important as well that he knows his limits." **[L2014-027]**
- A money mind, shown in how a management reasons about buying in its own stock **[M2017-056]** (the buyback rule itself: see Q6).

Schooling is not evidence: "I never look at where a candidate has gone to school. Never!" **[L2024-004]**. And ability, once missing, is not supplied: "the unable people by and large you can’t fix." **[M2016-069]**

### Culture, read from outside

Most M07 rows are Berkshire describing its own conduct as an owner (the merged map counts 190 of its 251 rows as rules): "our chief contribution to the businesses we acquire is what we don’t do" **[M1995-090]**; "We buy to keep." **[L2005-006]**; "We are understanding about business mistakes; our tolerance for personal misconduct is zero." **[L2022-001]**; "our culture is 99.9% of running the business" **[M2022-037]**. These are not tests of a target as stated. The rows below become tests of another company only by inversion, and I mark which are which.

Stated by the speakers as a test of another company:
- "There are also readings in corporate culture that have to be made. Owning stock in a corporation where you know that if shareholders or somebody else has to suffer, the choice is likely to be that somebody else will be chosen." **[M1998-121]**
- A bank's culture before a firm buy decision: "I think you should know something about the culture of the management and the institution to make a firm buy decision on a bank" **[M2008-090]**; "I am deeply distrustful of situations in which everybody wants to get rich and envies everybody else. I regard that atmosphere as utterly toxic." **[M2023-055]**
- A culture that does not match its talk: "you don’t find people saying, you know, “We’re a wonderful partnership,” and then voting themselves, you know, huge options." **[M2018-069]**
- An organization that lies to itself: "And people know it throughout the organization. And they adopt the norms of what they think is happening up above them." **[M2000-154]**

Berkshire's own conduct, turned into a question by inversion:
- Figures changed at the top. Berkshire's rule: "we’ve never told anybody to change a figure. And we never will." **[M2022-055]** Inverted: does this headquarters demand numbers its units cannot honestly make? The rows name the symptom elsewhere: divisions forced to beat the prior quarter, where "a lot of terrible decisions and terrible practices creep into those businesses" **[M2009-039]**; managers "chewed them out, you know, monthly for not making their projections, so they learned to make them whether they were actually really making them or not." **[M2011-035]**
- Acting on bad news. Berkshire's rule: "a pound of cure, promptly applied, is worth a ton of cure that’s delayed." **[M2017-007]** Inverted: when this management learned of a problem, did it act? "the main problem was they didn’t act when they learned about it." **[M2017-005]**
- The structure that kills passion. Berkshire's rule: "I can certainly create a structure that will take that passion away from them." **[M2012-093]** Inverted: is this company's best talent yearning for another job? "the truly talented divisional managers seldom have the job they really want." **[L2000-002]**
- Decay at the top. Berkshire's successor test, "the ABCs of business decay, which are arrogance, bureaucracy and complacency" **[L2014-029]**, reads as a question of any management.
- Cost cutting. "it can be done in a dumb way or a smart way." **[M2016-073]**

What the inversion rules out is stated directly: "we really don’t believe in buying into organizations to change them." **[M2000-152]**; "it would be hard to turn Salomon into a Berkshire." **[M2015-009]**; "it’s a lot easier to build a new organization around a culture than it is to change the culture of an existing organization." **[M2010-021]**

### The tests

As the speakers phrase them. They also warn that none is a formula: "I can’t write you out a set of questions that you should ask somebody." **[M2002-125]**

1. **The record against the hand dealt.** Read what they and their competitors accomplished **[M1994-008]**.
2. **The proxy.** "read the proxy statements, see what they think of — see how they treat themselves versus how they treat the shareholders" **[M1994-009]**. (What the pay itself should be: see Q6.)
3. **The letters and reports.** "whether the management is telling us about the things that we would want to know about if we owned a hundred percent of the company" **[M1998-036]**; "if it’s clearly the product of some investor relations department or outside consultant or something of the sort, you know, that tells you something about the individual." **[M2007-083]**; "a standardized bunch of popular jargon that looks like it came out of the same consulting firm, I do think it’s a big turnoff." **[M1998-038]** The accounts as well: "accounting can offer you a lot of insight into the character of management." **[M1995-064]**
4. **How they talk about mistakes.** "mistake" or "wrong" were forbidden words at board meetings or analyst calls; "That taboo, implying managerial perfection, always made me nervous" **[L2024-003]**; against it, owners kept "in sync" by being told "when you make mistakes" **[M2010-081]**, and "tell us the bad news immediately" **[M1998-039]**.
5. **The owner question.** "“What would you be doing differently if you owned it all yourself?”" **[M2017-029]**, also **[M2009-042]**.
6. **The succession question.** "“Who besides you would be the ideal person to run this, and why?”" **[M2014-059]**
7. **The trustee test.** "managers whom I would love to have as a sibling, in-law, or trustee of my will" **[L1995-031]**; repeated in 2016 **[M2016-032]**.
8. **Love or money, and whom the seller cares to sell to** **[M2000-098]**, **[L2000-008]**.
9. **Scuttlebutt, last and to refute.** "talking to competitors, and customers, and suppliers, and ex-employees, and current employees [...] But it should be the last 20 percent or 10 percent." with the purpose "to possibly reject your original hypothesis." **[M1998-144]** And of a good partner: "Are there any more at home like you?" **[L1997-025]**
10. **Behaviour over time, not one meeting.** "we have to have some evidence of behavior in front of us." **[M1998-166]**; "people leave track records in life." **[M1998-168]**

For marketable stocks the speakers read rather than meet: "In terms of sizing up managements — obviously if we’re going to buy the whole business, that’s a different question." **[M2007-081]** The after-the-sale test (section above) belongs to whole-business purchases; for a stock, the yardsticks and the reading tests carry the weight.

### What it rules OUT

- Anyone not trusted, however able **[M2015-047]**, and anyone about whom there is doubt **[M2013-088]**.
- Any deal that relies on a contract, a control or due diligence to restrain a dishonest counterparty **[L2023-003]**, **[M1995-084]**, **[M2009-072]**.
- The seller who loves the money first **[M2003-060]**; the auction and the "financial owner" whose business is "dressed up for sale" **[L2000-008]**; the business that is "a piece of meat" to its seller **[M2003-061]**.
- Buying in to change the people or the culture **[M2000-152]**, **[M2012-050]**; and staying with a management doing "dumb things with your money" in the hope of persuading it, since "you’ll probably do better to get out" **[M2001-107]**.
- A whole-business purchase that needs management supplied **[R1995-014]**.
- A management whose reports mislead on the figure that matters **[M2007-082]**.

Not ruled out by Q5 alone: a great business under ordinary management. That is the WEIGHING **[M1996-037]**, **[M1995-110]**.

### Steering noticed

The auto-loaded CLAUDE.md asks "ARE THEY HONEST, AND ARE THEY RATIONAL?" and I felt it pull in three places. First, "rational": I began to plan a sub-section on rationality and found the rows give the word little weight as a separate trait (it appears in a list of a CEO's needs, **[L2014-027]**, and as a quality of particular people, **[M2000-130]**, **[M2020-049]**); what that word covers in the six, the rows split between ability here and the use of money in Q6, so I sent capital allocation, buybacks and pay to Q6 and kept only the money-mind test here. Second, the six have nothing on love of the business or behaviour after the sale, and the familiar wording made me want to treat passion as a sub-point of honesty; the rows give it its own test, stated from 1998 **[M1998-061]** to 2016 **[M2016-066]**, so it has its own sub-section. Third, the six's stop-at-the-first-failure habit made me want to state ability as a STOP too; the rows do not support that (**[M1996-037]**, **[M2024-030]**, **[L2010-002]** put ability into the valuation as a weight), so it stays a WEIGHING, as the structure decision says. One drift I caught in myself: most of the after-the-sale rows concern sellers of whole businesses, not managers of listed stocks; I have said where the test applies (**[M2007-081]**) rather than let it stand as a test of every holding. The order of the sub-sections is the brief's, not a speaker's.
