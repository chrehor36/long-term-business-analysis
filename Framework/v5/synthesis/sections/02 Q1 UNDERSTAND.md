## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND IT? Where its economics will be in five, ten or twenty years, inside the perimeter of what I know. STOP.

**The question, in the speakers' words:** "can I understand it?" **[M1995-051]**, where understanding means "a reasonable fix on about what the earning power and competitive position will look like in five or 10 years" **[M2012-065]**, asked by someone who knows "where the perimeter is" **[M2010-083]**.

> "No, the **first question is, can I understand it?** And unless it’s going to be in a business that I think I can understand, **there’s no sense looking at it.**" — **[M1995-051]**, 1995 annual meeting (AM)

> "The answer is we will never buy anything we don’t think we understand. And our **definition of understanding** is thinking that we have a reasonable probability of being able to asses **where the business will be in 10 years**." — **[M2000-037]**, 2000 annual meeting (AM); "asses" is the transcript's spelling

> "**The main thing** is you have — are you **reasonably sure that you know what you’re doing?** And if it gets past that hurdle, then we go on to figure out the math of gain versus loss" — **[M2019-077]**, 2019 annual meeting (PM)

### What understanding means

**It is foreseeing the economics, not knowing the product.** The rows define the word three times in the same way across fifteen years.

> "my idea of understanding a business is that you’ve got a **pretty good idea where it’s going to be in ten years**." — **[M1997-021]**, 1997 annual meeting (AM)

> "Oh, **we understand the product.** We understand what it does for people. **We just don’t know the economics of it 10 years from now.** [...] It’s the predictability of the economics of the situation 10 years out." — **[M2000-104]**, 2000 annual meeting (PM)

> "it isn’t that I don’t understand, you know, what a certain business does. But when I say understand, it means that I think I have a reasonably — a reasonable fix on about **what the earning power and competitive position will look like in five or 10 years.** So I’ve got some notion of **how the industry will develop and where the company will stand within the industry.**" — **[M2012-065]**, 2012 annual meeting (PM)

The converse holds: the product can stay opaque if the economics are clear. Of a petroleum-additives business, "I never would understand the chemistry of it, but I — but that’s not necessarily vital. What is important is that **I understand the economic dynamics of the industry.**" **[M2011-014]**. The reason the horizon matters is stated once: "that’s what investing is, is seeing out. You don’t get paid for what’s already happened." **[M2007-025]**. The horizon runs "five or 10 or 20 years" **[M2005-089]**, and the precision asked is low: "that didn’t mean we had to do it to four decimal places or anything of the sort, but we had to have a feel for it, and we had to know our limitations." **[M2015-086]**.

**Seeing the industry is not seeing the company.** "there’s industries we know that may have a wonderful future, but we don’t have the faintest idea who the winners will be, so we don’t think about those, either." **[M2012-067]**. The 2009 letter says why growth does not settle it: "Just because Charlie and I can clearly see dramatic growth ahead for an industry does not mean we can judge what its profit margins and returns on capital will be as a host of competitors battle for supremacy." **[L2009-005]**.

**Change is the enemy of the forecast.** "We view change as more of a threat into the investment process than an opportunity." **[M1999-063]**; "a business that must deal with fast-moving technology is not going to lend itself to reliable evaluations of its long-term economics." **[L1993-023]**. The later rows move the object from the technology to the customer: Apple is "much more of a consumer products business" and "in terms of laying out what their prospective customers will do in the future, as opposed to, say, IBM’s customers, it’s a different sort of analysis." **[M2017-019]**; "we know what we think we can project out in terms of consumer behavior and threats to a business." **[M2023-030]**.

**The "too hard" box is a verdict, not a judgment of quality.** "Charlie says, you know, “We’ve got three boxes at the company: in, out, and too hard.” (Laughter) And a lot of things end up in the “too hard” pile, and it doesn’t bother us." **[M2006-013]**. Munger: "We throw almost all decisions into the too hard pile, and we just sift for a few decisions that we can make that are easy." **[M2007-046]**. A business in the box may be cheap and good: "It doesn’t mean it isn’t a good buy. It doesn’t mean it isn’t selling for a fraction of its worth. It just means that we don’t know how to evaluate it." **[M2000-038]**.

**The perimeter.** "The biggest thing is not how big your circle of competence is, but knowing where the perimeter is." **[M2010-083]**. Its edge is drawn conservatively: "if you have doubts about something being into your circle of competence, it isn’t." **[M2002-092]**; "it’s better to be well within the circle than to be trying to tiptoe along the line." **[M2002-093]**; "I’d rather have the narrow — the universe be a little too — interpret it as being a little too — a little smaller than it really is, than being interpreted as larger than it is." **[M2005-101]**. A dry spell does not widen it: "if we have trouble finding things within our circle, we will not enlarge the circle. You know, we’ll wait." **[M1995-018]**. Knowing the edge is self-realism: "that ability to know when you’re playing the game in which you’re going to win, and playing outside of that game, is a huge asset." **[M2014-051]**.

**The five-minute rule is in the rows.** "if we can’t make a decision in five minutes, we can’t make it in five months." **[M2008-086]**; "We probably know in the first five minutes or less whether something is likely to — or has a reasonable chance of happening." **[M2017-046]**; "we know instantly" **[M1997-148]**; "we can also tell in ten seconds whether it’s something of interest." **[M2023-030]**. The quick decision is made from the documents, not instead of them: "So if you can’t make a decision on something like PetroChina off the figures, forget about going further" **[M2008-069]**, and the PetroChina report "it’s right there in black and white, in a report that anybody can get. And we just sit in the office and read those things" **[M2005-015]**.

**What cannot be judged from the outside (M20).** Financial institutions are the named case. "You spot troubles in financial institutions late. It’s just the nature of the beast." **[M2001-066]**; "with financial institutions, it’s much tougher. Then you add — throw in derivatives on top of it, and, you know, it’s — no one probably knows, you know, perfectly, what some of the — or even within a reasonable range — the exact condition of some of the biggest, you know, banks in the world." **[M2005-068]**; "the only thing we understand is that we don't understand how much risk the institution is running." **[L2002-018]**. Understanding each transaction is not understanding the whole: of a finance company, "even though I could understand every individual transaction they did, I don’t regard the whole enterprise, or the operation of it, necessarily as being within my circle of competence." **[M2002-094]**. Of Chinese banks: "It’s no judgment that there’s anything bad." **[M2007-052]**. Of a bank's culture: "that’s hard to do for 99 percent of the banks." **[M2008-090]**. Of Gen Re's book: "Charlie and I could’ve spent 24 hours a day, and had the help of 10 or 20 math Ph.Ds. and we still wouldn’t have known what was going on." **[M2013-089]**.

### The tests

Each is a question the speakers put, with the row that puts it.

1. **Where will it be in ten years?** "What’s that chart going to look the next 10 years? [...] You’re trying to print the next 10 years of Value Line in your head." **[M1999-132]**; with **[M1997-021]**, **[M2000-037]**.
2. **What are the key variables, and how predictable are they?** "trying to identify the key variables in that particular business, and evaluating how predictable they were first, because that is the first step." **[M1998-044]**. For Coca-Cola the two are "unit case sales and shares outstanding" **[M1998-040]**.
3. **Do the past statements tell me the future ones?** "do I understand enough about this business so that the financial statements will tell me the information that’s useful to me in making a judgment about what the future financial statements are going to look like." **[M2008-033]**.
4. **Is it important and knowable?** "If something’s important but unknowable, forget it." **[M2006-076]**.
5. **Would the insiders write it down?** "they would not want to put down on paper their predictions about where 10 companies you would choose in the tech field would be in 10 years, in terms of their economics. They would say, “That’s too hard.”" **[M2000-105]**.
6. **Can I name the winner, not just the industry?** **[M2012-067]**; "seeing which ones, ten years from now, might be the best ones to be in" **[M1997-119]**.
7. **Is the forecast about customers or about technology?** **[M2017-019]**, **[M2023-030]**.
8. **How far off could I be?** "we’ve got a model in our mind, of what that place is likely to look like over some period of years. And then we also have some model in our mind of how far off we can be." **[M2011-084]**; "The chances of being way wrong in IBM are probably less, at least for us, than being way wrong with Google or Apple." **[M2012-073]**.
9. **Do I doubt it is inside?** Then it is not. **[M2002-092]**.
10. **Do I only think I understand it?** "what would bother me is if I think I understand a business and I don’t." **[M1997-024]**; ask friends: "Charlie’s given me a few contributions occasionally, saying, “What the hell do you know about that?”" **[M2014-053]**.
11. **Abroad: do I understand the governance and the tax?** "where we think we understand the nuances of the corporate governance system and taxation and that sort of thing." **[M2000-141]**.
12. **For a lender or a derivatives book (M20):** when both sides of a zero-sum trade "put on the books a profit that day" **[M2004-026]**; interest "accrued but was not paid" taken "as if it were paid" **[M2006-025]**; whether the payment fits "their actual � not hoped-for � income" (the replacement characters are the transcription's) **[L2008-011]**; whether funds come "from a natural customer base" or "on a wholesale basis, and that money can run pretty fast" **[M2012-012]**; and "what it achieves after operating for an extended period in a no-growth mode" **[L2004-009]**.

### Why it is a STOP

The rows say no further work is done, and say why more work would not help.

> "unless it’s going to be in a business that I think I can understand, **there’s no sense looking at it.**" — **[M1995-051]**, 1995 annual meeting (AM)

> "**If something is not very predictable, forget it.**" — **[M1998-044]**, 1998 annual meeting (AM)

> "our immediate decision is whether we can figure the — what’s being offered out to us or not. I mean, **there’s a go/no-go signal.** [...] We want to make sure that we don’t waste any time thinking about things that, when we get all through thinking about them, we’re not going to know enough to make the decision on. **So we just rule those out.**" — **[M2008-085]**, 2008 annual meeting (PM)

> "And **if we can’t understand it**, obviously, it’s not going to have — **we can’t make a decision as to whether it has a sustainable edge.**" — **[M1997-148]**, 1997 annual meeting (PM)

Study does not cure it. "we’re not going to learn enough in the followings five months to make up for the fact that we went in deficient in the first place." **[M2008-086]**; "We couldn't solve this problem, moreover, even if we were to spend years intensely studying those industries." **[L1993-023]**; "Our problem -- which we can't solve by studying up -- is that we have no insights into which participants in the tech field possess a truly durable competitive advantage." **[L1999-018]**. Nor does a low price: **[M2000-038]** above. The stop costs nothing the speakers count: "there is no penalty in investing if you don’t swing at a ball that’s in the strike zone" **[M2018-087]**.

**The verdicts.** The rows' three boxes **[M2006-013]**: IN passes to Q2; OUT and "too hard" close the file. Whether a fourth box is needed for a document not yet read is left to the reconciliation, as the structure decision says; the rows above put the reading inside the five minutes (**[M2008-069]**, **[M2005-015]**), and no instance of a separate not-yet-read verdict was found in the v5 ledger rows read for this section.

### What it rules OUT

- Fast-moving technology and businesses that change: **[L1993-023]**, **[M1999-063]**; "if something comes in where there’s a technological component that’s of significance [...] it won’t make it through the filter." **[M1998-008]**.
- Businesses that live on continued invention: "if they had never invented any more pharmaceuticals, it would be a terrible business." **[M1999-075]**.
- Start-ups: "Start-ups are not our game." **[L2007-015]**; "We don’t do startups." **[M2008-088]**.
- Industries whose winners cannot be named: **[M2012-067]**, **[M2014-097]**, **[L2009-005]**.
- Black boxes and instruments that cannot be read: "it’s not a bad area to just say, “This one’s too tough,” and go onto something else that’s a lot easier." **[M2009-062]**; the CDO-squared that took "750,000 pages, presumably, to evaluate one security" **[M2008-099]**.
- Banks, lenders and derivatives books whose condition cannot be seen: **[M2001-066]**, **[L2002-018]**, **[M2007-052]**, **[M2008-090]**.
- Whole areas quitclaimed: "it is entirely fair, as an investor, to just quitclaim certain areas of business as having too many problems." **[M2002-102]**.
- Fields where others are better: "a good idea not to play where the other people are better." **[M2017-020]**.
- Anything only believed to be understood: **[M1997-024]**; the narrated example is retail, "it’s easy to sort of think you understand retail, and then subsequently find out you don’t" **[M2014-052]**.

What it does not rule out: a technology label on a consumer business (**[M2017-019]**; "We are not an insurance company, we’re a tech company" is called a fundraising story in **[M2022-077]**), and an understood business in a less familiar country, where the rows ask a lower price rather than a pass: "We wanted to get paid more for going into China, and we did, because we don’t know the game as well there." **[M2006-021]**.

### The order: understanding before the castle

The merged map's section 2 tables the speakers' orderings and counts eleven general lists from 1994 to 2019 that name understanding first, the only element introduced with "first" or "The main thing" as a gate. Five lists put the advantage first or omit understanding. Read in full:

- **[M2008-039]** names the advantage, then understanding: "we want a business with a durable competitive advantage, which both of the companies you named do. And we want something we understand." It answers a question about two named companies, and the advantage is asserted of them before the general criteria are listed; the sequence is "And ... And", with no word of order.
- **[M2005-043]**, about U.K. businesses, gives "the same criteria we applied over here: a durable competitive advantage, and a management that we like and trust, and a reasonable price", with no understanding at all.
- **[M2010-061]** and **[L2017-002]** likewise omit it; the second is the letter's description of what is sought in "new stand-alone businesses".
- **[M2017-008]** joins the two: "we can look out five, or 10, or 20 years and decide that the competitive advantage that it had at the present would last over that period." The looking-out is the understanding of **[M2012-065]**.

**How the order is settled.** Q1 stays before Q2. No row in the five says the advantage comes first or can be judged without understanding. One row says it cannot: "if we can’t understand it, obviously, [...] we can’t make a decision as to whether it has a sustainable edge." **[M1997-148]**, which places "a sustainable edge" after the first filter in the same answer. **[M1995-051]** and **[M1998-088]** give the same sequence with "first" and "then". The definition rows explain why the five lists can skip it: understanding already contains the competitive position (**[M2012-065]**: "earning power and competitive position"; **[M2011-014]**: "are there competitive moats? Is there ease of entry?"), so a speaker naming the moat for a business he already understands is taking Q1 as passed, not reordering. The drafter's reading, from those rows: Q1 asks whether the economics and position can be foreseen at all; Q2 asks what the foresight shows about the castle.

**Where the rows disagree, plainly.** The spoken sequence in **[M2008-039]** is advantage then understanding, and it is not marked as an aside. A deliberate written text, **[L2017-002]**, leaves understanding out of the "key qualities" entirely. The acquisition criteria number "Simple businesses (if there's lots of technology, we won't understand it)" fifth of six, after earning power, returns and management, while asking for businesses that "meet all of the following criteria" **[R1997-001]**, which requires all and ranks none. These are carried, not explained away.
