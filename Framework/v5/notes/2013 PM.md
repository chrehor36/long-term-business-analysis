## What this session taught
The afternoon is mostly about how a purchase is judged, and the answer is never a ratio: a stock is a business, the figures that matter differ by kind of business, and the decision rests on a five-to-ten-year view held with confidence and a big gap between price and value (M2013-044 to M2013-046). The same view decides where Berkshire will invest: anywhere that view can be formed, and nowhere chosen by country or concept (M2013-066, M2013-078).
On competitive position, two checks recur: whether customers can drop or squeeze the business (American Express, M2013-043), and whether a competitor can simply be created (railroad against airline, M2013-054). Consolidation into a few players does not cure commodity economics (M2013-052), and Munger concedes the railroad miss while keeping airlines in the too-hard pile (M2013-053). Moat conviction is held in degrees, measured by how many things could go wrong, and a pension plan is an annuity company on the side whose liabilities are surer than its assets (M2013-074, M2013-075).
On price, the wonderful business is defined (high, durable returns on capital, better still with room to reinvest), and for it one stretches a little; bargains come in auction markets, not negotiated sales; buybacks are priced off intrinsic value, never book, with unease about profiting from partners (M2013-055, M2013-056, M2013-059, M2013-060).
On behaviour: people turn fearful and greedy en masse, the edge is not to be caught up, and no margin or rug-pull is allowed, so falling prices can be bought (M2013-079 to M2013-081). Accept options, never grant them (M2013-076).
On people and accounts: assess sellers for their enthusiasm after payment, watch reserves that move with the owners' interest, distrust accounts where the write-off pace or progress billing is a judgment, and walk away on doubt; Gen Re's derivatives showed that some books cannot be understood by anyone (M2013-084 to M2013-091).
