## What this session taught
The FY2010 letter adds a third element to valuing Berkshire, and any company: what management will do with retained earnings, a judgment that discounts today's value when the CEO's talent or motives are suspect (L2010-002). The meeting repeats that intrinsic value is a range partly for this reason (M2011-018).
Capital allocation is set against the whole world: do not confine reinvestment to your own industry, compare a business's price with what stocks and bonds can earn; the textile years are the confession of doing the opposite (L2010-003 to L2010-005, M2011-033).
The letter's last section turns survival into a rule and a test: to finish first, you must first finish; leverage is addictive and a single zero erases any record; refinancing is usually available until it is not, and only cash will do; keep cash in Treasury bills rather than reach for a few basis points (L2010-019 to L2010-021).
On judging people the year is shaped by Sokol: wealth is no bar to misconduct, ask a recommender when he bought, do not assume able people are rational, never decide in anger, choose a successor on character as much as skill, and separate the chair (M2011-004 to M2011-011). On compliance, a culture of trust and acts that show the rules mean business, not a bigger compliance department (M2011-041 to M2011-043).
On moats, Lubrizol gives the method: understand the industry's economics, not its technology; ask whether a large sum could take the business on; small markets with fanatical service can be protected (M2011-014 to M2011-017).
On asset classes, the meeting sorts investments into currency claims, unproductive assets and productive assets, and the choice of category comes first (M2011-024 to M2011-029).
On paying managers of money: how the record was achieved, sensitivity to risk never measured by beta, deferrals and carryforwards so bad years claw against good, and a fee structure that does not reward gathering money (L2010-014, L2010-015, M2011-032).
