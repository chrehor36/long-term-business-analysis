## What this session taught
- Value is restated in its plainest form: the discounted cash in and out of a business, and where that stream cannot be foreseen the value is simply unknown. The preference is for estimates held with high probability within a range over a lower-looking price resting on weak numbers.
- Growth is treated as neutral until one asks what it costs in capital; a business that does not grow but throws off cash can beat a fast grower that consumes its earnings.
- Capital allocation is the chief executive's own job. Operating capital stays with operating managers without review; the surplus goes to the top. Retained earnings over a ten-year tenure make this an enormous factor, and few CEOs are picked for it.
- Concentration is endorsed at up to 40 percent of a portfolio, but only when the investor knows enough to buy the whole business and business, price and people leave virtually no risk; those who cannot judge businesses should diversify.
- An understandable business is described operationally: brief reading of its public reports leaves an outsider about as well informed as a twenty-year insider.
- Several plain conduct rules: no comment on holdings, no buying on press reports, no hoarding cash for a fall, no currency hedging, no insurance on immaterial risks, puts are inferior to buying outright.
- Pay in a high-pay industry is acceptable only when tied to very large results for the owners.
