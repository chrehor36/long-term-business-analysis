## What this session taught
The FY2002 letter is about promises that are paid later and booked now. In reinsurance and derivatives alike, earnings rest on estimates that cannot be checked for years, errors lean toward whoever is paid on them, exits are nearly impossible, and collateral triggers and linked counterparties turn one failure into many (L2002-011 to 017). The response is a fortress: few links, only the strongest counterparties, alertness to low-probability catastrophe (L2002-007, L2002-017, L2002-019; M2003-050, M2003-056).
The meeting adds the insurer's temptation: cash comes first and losses later, so a writer who sees no losses expands until a few correlated errors overcome a lifetime; the brokers find whoever will write at a dumb price (M2003-031 to 034, with Mutual of Omaha and GEICO as cases).
Correlation is the recurring word in both documents: diversified-looking credits correlate in a crisis, and unrecognized concentration is called the deadliest risk (L2002-016, M2003-034).
Governance is read through incentives: whoever has the stake wins a negotiation, top pay has no parity of concern, consultants never recommend a cut, and directors who need their fees are not independent; the remedy offered is directors with bought stakes and large owners acting together (L2002-024 to 035; M2003-021 to 023). Buffett names his own silence on boards as a mistake (L2002-025).
For the reader of a company: weak visible accounting predicts hidden conduct, targets met every time invite incredulity, figures that cannot be understood are meant not to be, and depreciation is cash paid in advance (L2002-039 to 041, M2003-029).
On the price of a stock, the market serves and does not instruct; Buffett's narrated mistake is stopping a purchase because the price rose (M2003-046, M2003-047). Munger's See's story is the companion: they nearly refused to pay up for quality and learned from a critic (M2003-039), and the meeting gives the wonderful-business-at-a-fair-price line for the first time in this read (M2003-040).
On managers, the love-of-the-business test recurs again, now with Munger ranking passion above brainpower and Buffett's contrast with a business bought to resell (M2003-060, M2003-061, M2003-063).
A stated hurdle appears in the letter for the first time in this read: a very high probability of at least 10% pre-tax, or sit out (L2002-020).
