## What this session taught
- The 1997 letter is a letter about a full market: the fat pitch is waited for (L1997-006), high prices mean low prospective earnings rather than a forecast (L1997-005), the margin of safety is eroded by a cheery consensus (L1997-035), and buyers should want lower prices (L1997-009). The morning repeats it in speech: do not buy on the premise that record returns on equity last (M1998-016), and returns come from price paid and what the business does afterwards (M1998-047).
- Rare-event insurance is taught as a case of deceptive evidence: years of profit prove nothing (L1997-015), the paid expert with nothing at risk is more dangerous for being sincere (L1997-016), and the earthquake does not know the premium (M1998-035). Price is held and volume allowed to fall (L1997-017).
- A recurring method: find the two or so variables that decide a business, judge whether they are predictable, and ignore the rest (GEICO's policies and seasoned profit, Coke's unit cases and share count: L1997-019, M1998-040, M1998-042, M1998-044). If the variables are not predictable, "forget it".
- The cigar-butt approach is given up as a narrated mistake, with Berkshire itself named as one (M1998-006, M1998-007): time is the friend of the high-return business and the enemy of the low one.
- Issuing stock is confessed as having cost owners money (L1997-029), and not buying back Berkshire is confessed as a missed boat (M1998-051); a takeover premium needs either an overvalued currency or real combined earnings (L1997-031).
- Management is read through candour: the annual report should tell a 100 percent owner what he would want (M1998-036), bad news immediately (M1998-039), and the selling owner must love the business more than the money (M1998-061). Integrity counted more than lease and contract checks in the Star deal (L1997-026).
- Thinking habits are stated outright: what is important and knowable (M1998-023), "and then what?" (M1998-032), examine contradicting evidence at once (M1998-004), multiple models (M1998-059), and no bonus for difficulty (M1998-003).

## Tensions seen
- Buybacks: Buffett likes repurchases by a wonderful business "even at prices that may give you nose bleeds" (M1998-027), while Munger says every stock has a price at which repurchase is foolish (M1998-028, rowed as tension) and the letter says buybacks are worth more the lower the price (L1997-010). Buffett concedes the last point in the same answer.
- Selling: "the real thing to do with a great business is just hang on for dear life", yet he trimmed in 1997 to move toward bonds and calls it possibly a mistake (M1998-012), against L1996-025 (do not trim dominant winners). Noted in M1998-012's notes.
- The fat pitch (L1997-006) against the unconventional commitments of the same letter (L1997-007, L1997-008, M1998-052): with no fat pitch in sight, he bought zeros, oil and silver, which he says carry only a belief, not a knowledge, of profit. Not rowed as tension; both sides are his own and he draws the line himself.
- The cost-of-float definitions: L1997-013 says float has been worth more than equal net worth, and M1998-029 limits that to float that costs less than nothing. A refinement, not a contradiction.
