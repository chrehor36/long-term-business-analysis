## What this session taught
Risk is defined as permanent capital loss or an inadequate return on the capital put in, judged by looking at the business and its competitors, never by volatility (M2001-080, M2001-083); See's loses money in two quarters a year and is "one of the least risky businesses I know."
Capital has three questions in order: retain only if a dollar kept makes more than a dollar, repurchase only at a significant discount to conservatively calculated value, then the most with the least risk; the cost of any deal is the second-best deal, and the theorists' cost of equity is rejected (M2001-081, M2001-082, M2001-084, M2001-086).
Investing is valuing the business and comparing with price (M2001-060); the investor worth backing sees stocks as pieces of businesses (M2001-108).
The moat is the one instruction to managers (M2001-071), but whether moats are getting rarer is left open between the two speakers (M2001-069, M2001-070), and the brand moat is shown narrowing under retailer power and overreaching price (M2001-087 to M2001-090).
Financial institutions are read with special suspicion: figures hide much, trouble shows late, insolvency can come with cash on hand, and pay that front-ends uncertain profits is "dynamite" (M2001-066 to M2001-068, M2001-072 to M2001-075, M2001-077).
Shorting is rejected despite overvaluation being commoner than undervaluation (M2001-091 to M2001-095).
Graham's net-working-capital method is defined and set aside for "a cheap, wonderful business", with Munger's reason that working capital no longer reliably comes back to owners (M2001-113 to M2001-115).
Management that does dumb things with a good business is left, not lobbied (M2001-107).
