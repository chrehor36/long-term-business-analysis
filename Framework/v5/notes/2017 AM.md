## What this session taught
The FY2016 letter's new lessons are on buybacks, accounting candour and the investment business. A buyback is judged as buying out a partner in a private company, should carry a price ceiling, and should be skipped even when cheap if the business needs the money or a better use exists (L2016-002, L2016-003). A CEO who features adjusted earnings infects the people below him, and restructuring that recurs every year is a cost (L2016-006, L2016-007). Success in money management breeds failure through inflows, size and fee incentives, and the consultant and the wealthy client together keep money away from the index (L2016-012, L2016-013).
The morning's centre is people and governance: culture over rulebooks, incentives designed with care and watched, a CEO who acts at once when bad news reaches him (the Salomon delay and the fine-sized seriousness), directors who bought their own shares, pay consultants who cannot recommend less (M2017-003 to M2017-007, M2017-051, M2017-053).
On the successor: capital allocation must be close to the chief's main talent, a "money mind" is distinct from IQ, and how a management reasons about buying in its own stock is a quick test of it; the ideal successor is already rich and paid by an option whose strike rises with retained earnings (M2017-049, M2017-050, M2017-054 to M2017-056).
On businesses: the sweet spot is a durable advantage, a trusted manager and then price; See's question was whether people would keep choosing it over the low bid; every castle draws marauders; the capital-light grower is ideal; industries move both ways, chemicals to commodity and railroads to good (M2017-008, M2017-009, M2017-012, M2017-026, M2017-038, M2017-039).
On the times: Munger twice says the easy bargains are gone, quality now costs more and small statistical advantages must do (M2017-024, M2017-063), while Buffett says consumer behavior is harder to foresee than it was (M2017-047).
