## What this session taught
The FY2004 letter's insurance section reads as an account of how a commodity business can still earn: the product is a commodity when nobody asks for it by name (L2004-003), and then there are two routes, price discipline that lets volume fall without layoffs or the low-cost position pressed without let-up (L2004-004, L2004-005, L2004-007), with GEICO's under-reserving as the case of the first failing (L2004-008).
The meeting adds a test of a business's strength in its own pricing: whether a modest rise would make sales "fall off a cliff", and how much agony managers feel before raising prices, with newspapers as the business whose agony has grown (M2005-019, M2005-020); competitive strength shows over time in passing through input costs (M2005-017).
Incentives run through both documents: a manager paid to deploy money buys what he does not understand (M2005-007, M2005-008), announced numbers bend loss reserves even without pay attached (M2005-036), and headquarters' wish for smooth rising earnings is Munger's "blood brother of evil" (M2005-037); the GSEs are the narrated case of promised growth ending in accounting shenanigans (M2005-042).
On risk, the morning restates worst-case planning and adds two lines: a large loss in one day is acceptable, discomfort in paying the next morning is not, and physically possible events absent from history are still to be thought about (M2005-027, M2005-028); the letter adds that rapid growth hides problems, so earning power is tested in a no-growth stretch (L2004-009), and the zinc write-off gives the arithmetic of simple propositions (L2004-002).
On mania, Buffett treats farmland and housing alike: plain fixed output bid far above its cost, lending that loosens as prices rise, guarantees that stop the lender looking, and appreciation substituted for income, which he calls the bigger-fool game (M2005-024, M2005-030 to M2005-032).
On his own mistakes, the letter confesses not acting on the Bubble ("I talked when I should have walked", L2004-011) and adopting directors' sessions without the CEO and a whistleblower line decades late (L2004-013).
For owners, the letter gives three questions large holders should press (L2004-014) and a structure for acceptable options (L2004-016); the meeting tells individuals that their best investment is their own abilities and that they tend to arrive at the wrong time (M2005-045, M2005-046).

## What in the brief was wrong or unclear
The restatement rules are split by document but say nothing about a passage that crosses documents: section 6 says a letter passage that restates "an earlier year's letter" is rowed only if it adds a new test or rule, and that a meeting passage restating "an earlier meeting row, or the same year's letter" is rowed, but neither covers a letter passage that restates only an earlier meeting row or an earlier report row. This unit had two: NICO's no-layoff promise (L2004-005) restates M1996-046 and M2004-077, and the fractional-ownership view (L2004-010) restates R1996-001; by contrast "Run your business as if it were the only asset your family will own" (line 145) restates the letter row L1998-010, so the letter rule applied and it was not rowed. I treated the letter rule as reaching earlier letters only, rowed the two letter passages that had appeared only in meetings or the report, and named the earlier ids in evolution_notes; a stricter reading would have dropped L2004-005 and L2004-010. Second, section 6 allows an elision to cross "another speaker's interjection" when the row's speaker resumes the thought, but M2005-047 also crosses a one-line question by its own speaker (Munger, line 521) between his two statements; I treated it as part of the same exchange and said so in the row.
