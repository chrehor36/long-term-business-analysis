## What this session taught
The FY2006 letter turns to the people who will run money after Buffett: the test for a manager is not brains or a recent record but an inborn sense for risks never seen before, with temperament (independent thinking, emotional stability, an understanding of human and institutional behavior) as the second test (L2006-012, L2006-013). The meeting repeats it three times (M2007-015, M2007-049, M2007-050), adding scale, "anything times zero is zero", and that the hire must already know how, not be taught (M2007-048).
On businesses, the letter gives the clearest account in this read of how a franchise forms and decays: the newspaper's circularity of readers and advertisers, eroding fundamentals that beat good management, and insiders blind to it (L2006-006 to L2006-009), with ISCAR as the opposite case, a business that earns by making its customers more money (L2006-003).
On valuation, the meeting gives a worked method: value a business as a farm on conservative assumptions, Aesop's birds, the McDonald's stand, and a margin of safety that is not used to cover a business one cannot predict (M2007-022, M2007-043 to M2007-045); Munger adds that no mechanical method exists and that margin of safety is getting more value than one pays, in any form (M2007-027, M2007-042).
On markets and risk, Buffett names the unrecognized crowded trade and the invisible leverage of derivatives (M2007-034, M2007-035), Munger rejects Gaussian disaster models (M2007-040), and both tie bad accounting to bad behavior: bonuses on phony profits, marks that do not net to zero, profits booked on loans before they prove good (M2007-036, M2007-037, M2007-061).
New checks in this read: whether a record could be chance, by the hat test on Walter Schloss (L2006-018); what an executive does when the wallet is his own (L2006-015); whether two derivative marks net to zero (M2007-037); whether a price is set by supply and demand rather than a story (M2007-056).
