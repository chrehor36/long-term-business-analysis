## What this session taught
The afternoon spends two long answers on how a franchise erodes: more competitors and more routes to the same audience lower every route's value (M2006-051, M2006-052), a product can stay worth its price and still lose to cheap alternatives (M2006-053), a self-reinforcing circle runs in reverse (M2006-060), and the newspaper is seen as a wrapper for preprints that other carriers can carry (M2006-063). Both speakers confess the bulletproof-franchise error, Buffett on newspapers and Munger on General Motors (M2006-061, M2006-062), and Buffett says why owners resisted seeing it: nobody had made a mistake buying a monopoly paper for decades (M2006-064).
On price, a declining stream deserves a much lower multiple than a growing one of the same size, and insiders who see "the first robin" reinvest in the decline (M2006-058, M2006-059).
On sellers, the "strategic buyer" is defined as the one who pays too much (M2006-092), and a business bought to be resold carries figures that cannot be trusted (M2006-093, M2006-094); Munger sets "welcome partners" against "doing deals" (M2006-057).
On decision-making, heading 15 gathers in one answer what earlier rows held apart: right by facts and reasoning, not agreement (M2006-075), the important and knowable against price (M2006-076), the market serving rather than instructing (M2006-077), and never letting another call your tune (M2006-079).
On small sums, one idea worth a ton (M2006-071), smaller stocks (M2006-072), concentration in the understood mispricing (M2006-073), and Munger's opportunity cost as a default option raised by expertise (M2006-074).
New in this read: a large short interest as a frequent mark of fraud (M2006-098), gambling as created risk against insurance as transferred risk (M2006-095), the portfolio-insurance "doomsday machine" of individually sensible procedures (M2006-055), and court discretion overriding the book's priorities in bankruptcy (M2006-090).
