## What this session taught
- The business ranks above the manager: the great business needs no great management, the poor one cannot survive without it, and an able manager in a terrible business should leave (M1996-037). Yet the manager is judged hard too: a long record, no banjo hitters, growth only when logical, no ego compulsion, focus (M1996-038, M1996-092, M1996-048).
- Concentration is argued from knowledge: diversification protects against ignorance, and three businesses resistant to effective competition carry less risk than fifty big ones (M1996-040 to 042).
- Durability is asked as a question of change. Change is assumed to work against the owner; the test is whether one can see the industry and the competitive pecking order 10 or 20 years out, and whether an asset (Gillette's credibility, Disney's share of mind) can be built by a rival at all (M1996-058, M1996-059, M1996-062, M1996-063).
- Where the value sits is a test: in the institution (Mayo Clinic, the mouse with no agent) or in individuals who can claim it (the brain surgeon, the movie business) (M1996-065, M1996-079, M1996-081).
- Insurance discipline is built into incentives: no layoffs for lower volume, no premium budget, share only in profitable markets, and capital flowing into an industry is a warning (M1996-045 to 047, M1996-086).
- Valuation is held lightly: DCF is the stated frame but is never written out; if pencil is needed the case is too close (M1996-083, M1996-084, M1996-085).
- Pay: charge managers for capital at a fair rate, a fixed-price option is an interest-free loan, and the plainest alignment is buying the stock for cash (M1996-075 to 078).
