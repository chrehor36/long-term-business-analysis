## What this session taught
The afternoon's longest thread is pay and boards. Pay in a capital-heavy business must charge for capital; the CEO usually sets his own pay through a committee he picks; peer comparisons ratchet it up; a hundred-page plan is itself a warning; a director whose fees matter to him is not independent; and the board is judged on three jobs, not on checklists (M2009-081 to M2009-087, M2009-094).
On the remedy the two partners part: Buffett wants the largest institutions to embarrass the egregious cases, Munger doubts those institutions, whose own managers are overpaid (M2009-093, M2009-095).
On managers: the question at a purchase is whether the seller's passion survives the cheque, which no contract can secure; trust deserved on both sides replaces contracts; love of the business can be spotted but not instilled; and both admit they are slow, slowest with people they love, when a manager declines (M2009-072, M2009-073, M2009-096 to M2009-098).
On buying: the cheaper the better for a lifelong buyer who knows values; pricing, not bottom-picking; do not wait for 1973-74, since such times come when nobody has money; buy fast when the price is right, and look under every shell (M2009-099 to M2009-103).
On business quality: the best business needs little capital and has a moat other than the cost of entry, yet moats fill with sand; a capital-heavy business without an assured return is the one to fear (M2009-104 to M2009-106).
A survival test new to these units: a business whose bill is frequent, visible and impossible to give up may, in a bad inflation, be taken over by the state (M2009-088).
Smaller lessons: value is the only kind of investing (M2009-065); a security's seniority is measured by the equity beneath it, and an issuer's call is exercised against the holder (M2009-079, M2009-080); post-mortems are done by those who made the decision and kept private (M2009-069, M2009-070).
