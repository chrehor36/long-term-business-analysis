## What this session taught
The afternoon is half Abel. His capital allocation is the inherited one set out in order: reputation first, a balance sheet that never depends on a bank, the owned businesses' needs, then whole businesses only when value against risk is right, then stocks as pieces of cash flow, and nothing without a view of the economics twenty years out. The utility questions carry the session's sharpest lesson from both men: a fixed return is priced for a defined risk, and when courts and states change the risk, no return is enough; Buffett adds that a game you will lose where others hold the pen should be quit, that one ruling is read as a societal trend that lowers value, and that it is easier to do stupid things with other people's money. On markets: the best deals come amid pessimism, and capital-light businesses beat capital-heavy ones, with insurance capital doubling as a guarantee fund. On people: read balance sheets before income statements, ask managers which competitor they would own and short, argue the other side better than its advocates, and know that bad behaviour at the top spreads downward faster than good. The failed health-care venture is told as a lesson about an entrenched industry's political power. Buffett closes by keeping every share as he steps down, which he ties to backing his successor.
