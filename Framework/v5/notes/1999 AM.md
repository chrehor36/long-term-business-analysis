## What this session taught
The FY1998 letter is mostly about honest numbers: options are a cost to be charged when valuing a company (L1998-025), restructuring and merger reserves are where earnings get manufactured (L1998-031, L1998-034), and the managers who do it are often otherwise decent people acting on the premise that their job is the stock price (L1998-029).
The meeting carries the same thread into practice: option cost as public-warrant value averaged over years (M1999-025), pay tied to what a manager controls (M1999-029), and a proper option needing a cost of capital (M1999-030).
On valuation, Buffett endorses discounting owner cash flows at the long-term government rate for comparability and then demanding a discount from that figure (M1999-011), and says the same model applies abroad with accounting adjusted in the head (M1999-052).
On risk, the LTCM answers put leverage at the centre of ruin for able people and refuse any price for a small chance of ruin (M1999-004 to M1999-007).
On picking, the session separates a great industry from a good investment twice: autos and airlines (M1999-034) and the internet's effect on retail (M1999-013, M1999-016), with Munger noting that losing advantages does not always kill a business (M1999-015).
Price discipline on owned wonders: hold them at full P/Es, but enthusiasm for adding falls with the multiple (M1999-055), while the 1993 drug-stock miss is narrated as a mistake of omission (M1999-043).
Bargains, Buffett says, come from marginal sellers in weak markets, never from negotiated sales where "the person on the other side cares too much" (M1999-041).

## Tensions seen
- M1999-037 (Munger) against M1999-035 (Buffett): goodwill should not be amortized in the ordinary case, but Munger says there are plenty of cases where amortization is rational and should be required.
- Not rowed as a tension, noted here: R1998-002 calls the eggs-in-one-basket position comfortable because Berkshire is diversified within, while M1999-044 says that when a winner cannot be picked one should buy a group; both are about where diversification belongs, and they do not clearly conflict.
