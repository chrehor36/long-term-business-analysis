## What this session taught
The FY2014 letter proper adds little that earlier letters lack; its new lessons are about the investor's own conduct: risk depends on whether one may be forced to sell, near-term needs belong in Treasuries, borrowed money has no place in an investor's tool kit, and Tesco shows the cost of selling slowly after souring on management (L2014-003 to L2014-005).
The morning session returns again and again to people and incentives: the lender who keeps the loan, Singleton's incentives grown so strong that honest people went too far, a CEO's forecast or teasing that leads subordinates to hide or fudge, character and trust ranked above brains and record, and very bright people self-destructing for money they did not need (M2015-001, M2015-032 to M2015-034, M2015-045 to M2015-047).
On culture: it comes from the top, must be consistent, rewarded and punished, selects its own members, and a terrible one cannot be turned (M2015-008, M2015-009, M2015-013).
On staffing the speakers push harder than in 2014: only the people needed, efficiency demanded by capitalism, with Buffett tolerating excess only under a manager he values (M2015-003, M2015-037, M2015-039).
On safety and survival: capital far beyond any conceivable need, others' problems never able to hurt Berkshire, small probabilities treated as certainties over many exposures, and a hundred times too cautious rather than 1 percent incautious, with the missed opportunities accepted (M2015-020, M2015-021, M2015-027, M2015-035).
On markets: no macro forecasts, profits valued against the bond yield, the same principles in every country, and an easy game for anyone who controls his emotions (M2015-015, M2015-016, M2015-029, M2015-040 to M2015-043).
On businesses: brands as promises that must be protected, low cost as the defence against disruption, and asking where an advantage really lies before adding units or entering finance (M2015-010, M2015-011, M2015-025, M2015-038).

## What in the brief was wrong or unclear
The 2010 PM to 2011 PM convention says text in a letter file after the letter's signature is "signed text outside the letter, read and not rowed", and its only example is the biennial memo to the managers. The FY2014 letter file carries, after the signature at lines 1177-1180, Buffett's "Berkshire � Past, Present and Future" (lines 1207-1915) and Munger's "Vice Chairman's Thoughts � Past and Future" (lines 1919-2178): about a third of the file, addressed to shareholders, announced by the letter's own note to readers (lines 80-84) and footnote (line 169), and the text this morning's questioners cite most. The brief does not say whether a signed text to shareholders that the letter itself points to counts as part of the letter, nor whether Munger's signed text in a letter file can yield L-rows at all. I applied the convention literally: both were read whole, not rowed, and listed under Rows with line spans of every passage that would have been a candidate, so the operator can decide; I chose that side because rows cannot be withdrawn while a re-queue can add them, and because the pair is a separate shelf class (a fact I have only from the auto-loaded protocol, recorded above). If the operator rules them in, they are perhaps thirty or more rows, many of them new (the cigar butt and Munger's blueprint, issuing shares worth more than what is received, the three strengths of financial staying power, the ABCs of business decay, Munger's fifteen elements of the Berkshire system and his errors of omission).

### What this taught
Buffett's anniversary text is the most mistake-dense document read so far in this read: the Stanton tender lost over an eighth of a point, NICO put into the wrong vehicle, See's nearly lost to caution over three times tangible assets, and Dexter generalised into a rule that trading a wonderful business's shares for a so-so business destroys value for good (L2014-007, L2014-008, L2014-010, L2014-011).
On acquisitions it gives the clearest single test in the letters so far: the intrinsic value of the shares given against that of the business received, with premiums-to-market and per-share accretion named as the wrong measures (L2014-012, L2014-013); share issuers and promoters are screened out (L2014-014, L2014-015, L2014-019, L2014-020).
On survival it names three strengths, large reliable earnings, massive liquid assets, no near-term cash needs, and bars anything that lets others demand large sums suddenly, closing with "it is madness to risk losing what you need in pursuing what you simply desire" (L2014-023 to L2014-025).
On succession it is a test of the person: rational, calm, decisive, knowing his limits, "all in" for the company, able to fight arrogance, bureaucracy and complacency, with a non-executive chairman to remove him if needed (L2014-026 to L2014-030).
Munger's text is a written constitution: fifteen elements, the aims behind them, and the claim that concentrated attention and long tenure (Wooden's method) did the heavy work; his largest stated errors are omissions, not commissions (L2014-031 to L2014-046).

### What in the brief was wrong or unclear (supplement)
The supplement's instruction says to row the pair "exactly as for a letter", but the letter conventions in section 6 do not fit it cleanly in two places. First, the restatement rule ("a letter passage that restates an earlier year's letter in substance ... is rowed only if it adds a new test or rule") was written for Buffett re-saying his own letters; it does not say whether it binds Munger, who has no earlier letter, when his signed text restates Buffett's letters or the reports (cash not stock, little debt, confidentiality to sellers). I held Buffett's text to the rule and rowed Munger's restatements with the ids named, on the reading that the rule is about a speaker's own repetition; the operator may prefer the stricter reading, which would drop L2014-031, L2014-035, L2014-038 and L2014-039 to listed repeats.
