## What this session taught
The FY1999 letter's longest passage is about buybacks: one combination of facts (surplus funds, price well below conservatively calculated value, owners informed) and the continuing owner's interest in a low price (L1999-023, L1999-026, L1999-031). Its definition of a business's two "needs", maintenance of competitive position and growth spending expected to return more than a dollar per dollar, is the closest the letter comes to defining what must be spent before cash is free (L1999-024).
The meeting gives the clearest statement so far of how value is computed: birds in the bush, when they come out, and interest rates (M2000-002), in practice a 20-year cash stream discounted at a proper rate against the price (M2000-035), with growth only an input (M2000-001) and no P/E or loss cutoff (M2000-033).
Risk is defined as what could reduce the business's economic strengths over five to fifteen years (M2000-014), and the moat is called "the primary criterion of a great business" (M2000-018), to be judged each year ahead of the P&L (M2000-075).
Three tests of a moat recur: charging more while holding share (M2000-031), growing through adversity (M2000-032), and no entrants despite certain demand (M2000-077).
Understanding is defined as a reasonable probability of assessing the business ten years out (M2000-037); where that fails, as in tech, nothing is bought (M2000-009, L1999-018).
Structure can outweigh management in both directions: the idiot nephew in a monopoly paper, the genius in a newly contested one (M2000-071), and Dexter's able managers against foreign costs (L1999-016).
Pay is how the owner speaks to every employee (M2000-058), and stock-price pay is a lottery ticket (M2000-062).
