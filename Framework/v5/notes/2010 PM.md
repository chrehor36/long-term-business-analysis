## What this session taught
The afternoon returns again and again to incentives and the structure that tames them. "Everyone else is doing it" is the hardest pressure on integrity, shown by 498 of 500 companies choosing not to expense options; professing integrity proves nothing; the cure is a system in which those who decide bear the consequences, and an organisation built to tempt as little as possible, which is why no budgets go to the top (M2010-069 to M2010-072). Advisers paid per deal, and managers bored with hard competition at home, push companies into acquisitions (M2010-067, M2010-068).
On business quality: a good business is defined by the capital it actually needs, a good investment by the price paid; the very best grow large with no capital; incredible pricing power means a near monopoly; pricing power lasts only while the customer needs you, as the newspapers show (M2010-090 to M2010-092, M2010-052, M2010-053, M2010-062).
On the investor: no forecasts; three checks (lasting advantage, management, price) then a comparison against cash and bonds; judge a stock like a farm; the investor who is scared when others are, or needs someone to restore his courage, will not make money; know the perimeter of competence, keep asking simple questions of local businesses, and when many things are cheap but none is known well, buy a package (M2010-060, M2010-061, M2010-073, M2010-074, M2010-083 to M2010-085).
On owners: tell them what kind of place you run and deliver it; catering to expectations that operations cannot meet leads to accounting games (M2010-080, M2010-081).
On capital: the retained-dollar test misled when written as a five-year market test; the question for the future is whether all the capital generated can be used well (M2010-045, M2010-046, M2010-097).
Munger's BYD answer is the one place the session pulls against the earlier rows: the boast of avoiding new technology, reconciled by a test of people who have already won their spurs (M2010-095).
