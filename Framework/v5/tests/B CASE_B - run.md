## 3. The questions, in order

### Q1. CAN I UNDERSTAND IT? (STOP)

**The test as the draft states it.** Understanding is "a reasonable fix on about what the earning power and competitive position will look like in five or 10 years" (**[M2012-065]**), "where the business will be in 10 years" (**[M2000-037]**); it is the economics, not the product: "We understand the product. [...] We just don't know the economics of it 10 years from now." (**[M2000-104]**). Where fast-moving technology puts the forecast out of reach, the box is TOO HARD, not OUT (draft, Q1, "Sent to TOO HARD, not OUT"; **[L1993-023]**, **[M2000-038]**).

**The tests, applied to the case.**

1. *Where will it be in ten years?* (**[M1999-132]**, **[M1997-021]**). The record in the case is a business transformed inside eight years: net sales 24,006 (FY2007) to 233,715 (FY2015), year-on-year growth of 56%, 14%, 52%, 66%, 45%, 9%, 7% and 28% (CASE_B.json, `revenue`, filed 2009-10-27 to 2015-10-28). A business whose size moved tenfold in eight years and whose growth swings between 7% and 66% gives no visible ten-year shape from the figures alone.
2. *What are the key variables, and how predictable are they?* (**[M1998-044]**). From the description, the variables are the replacement and upgrade behaviour of buyers of the hardware lines, the gross margin held against "aggressive price cutting by competitors" and "industry-wide downward pressure on gross margins", and the continued success of new products. The company's own Item 1 says it "relies primarily on the innovative skills of its personnel" (DESC_B.md). That is the business "quite dependent on the technology continuing to gallop" (**[M1999-075]**), and a key variable that rests on continued invention is not a predictable one.
3. *Do the past statements tell me the future ones?* (**[M2008-033]**). Not from this case. Net margin moved 26.7% (FY2012) to 21.7% (FY2013) to 22.8% (FY2015), and net income fell 11% in FY2013 on higher sales (CASE_B.json, `net_income`, `revenue`). The case carries no sales by product line, no unit volumes and no gross margin, so I cannot tell what drove the 28% jump in FY2015 or whether it repeats. "In many cases, the answer would be no" (**[M2008-033]**); here it is no on the evidence carried.
4. *Is it important and knowable?* (**[M2006-076]**). The durability of the product cycle is the important thing, and the case does not make it knowable.
5. *Would the insiders write it down?* (**[M2000-105]**). The filing's own risk language (frequent product introductions, rapid technological advance, competitors who "may sell at little or no profit", sole-sourced custom parts, assembly concentrated in one overseas region) is the opposite of a written ten-year forecast.
6. *Can I name the winner, not just the industry?* (**[M2012-067]**, **[L2009-005]**). The figures show this company won the last eight years. Whether it is the winner of the next ten in an industry its filing describes as a price-cutting technology race is the question the rows say cannot be answered by seeing growth: seeing "dramatic growth ahead for an industry does not mean we can judge what its profit margins and returns on capital will be as a host of competitors battle for supremacy" (**[L2009-005]**).
7. *Is the forecast about customers or about technology?* (**[M2017-019]**, **[M2023-030]**). This is the one test that could carry the name IN. The description has a customer side: proprietary operating systems on every line, a cloud service that ties a user's devices together, marketplaces for content and applications, and a large developer community. If buyers stay inside that system and repurchase, the forecast is a forecast of consumer behaviour, which the draft does not rule out (draft, Q1, "What it does not rule out: a technology label on a consumer business"). But the case carries no evidence of that behaviour: no retention, no repeat-purchase rate, no installed base, no unit figures. And both rows the draft gives for this door are dated after the anchor (2017 and 2023); in a point-in-time case I do not treat them as available evidence (see section 8). The company's own description frames its competition as technological. On the evidence in hand the forecast is about technology.
8. *How far off could I be?* (**[M2011-084]**). Far. The speakers' own comparison of a slow-changing technology customer base with consumer technology platforms is that "the chances of being way wrong" are larger with the latter, because "I just don't know how to evaluate the people that are out there working, either in big companies or in garages, that are trying to think of something that will change the world" (**[M2012-073]**, dated before the anchor).
9. *Do I doubt it is inside?* Yes. "if you have doubts about something being into your circle of competence, it isn't." (**[M2002-092]**); the perimeter is drawn "a little smaller than it really is" (**[M2005-101]**).
10. *Do I only think I understand it?* (**[M1997-024]**). The risk is real: the products are familiar to everyone, and familiarity with the product is exactly what the rows separate from understanding the economics (**[M2000-104]**).

Tests 11 (governance and tax abroad) and 12 (lenders and derivatives books) do not apply as stated; the business is not a lender.

**Verdict: TOO HARD.** The draft's rows send a business "that must deal with fast-moving technology" to the box where "we don't know how to evaluate it" (**[L1993-023]**, **[M1998-008]**, **[M2000-038]**, **[M2006-013]**); this filing describes its own markets that way and says its edge rests on continued invention (**[M1999-075]**); the one door the draft leaves open, the consumer-behaviour reading (test 7), needs evidence the case does not carry; and doubt about the perimeter is itself the answer (**[M2002-092]**). Study would not cure it from this seat: "we're not going to learn enough in the followings five months to make up for the fact that we went in deficient in the first place." (**[M2008-086]**). This is not a judgment of the business's quality; it "doesn't mean it isn't selling for a fraction of its worth" (**[M2000-038]**).

**What would resolve it**, named and not invented: the full Form 10-K of record for FY2015 (Item 7, net sales and unit sales by product line, gross margin by year, and the segment note), the FY2016 interim 10-Qs to the anchor (to see whether the FY2015 surge in the leading product line held), and any filed or independent evidence of customer retention and repurchase across the product lines. The first two would answer tests 2 and 3; only the third could answer test 7. None is in the case.

## 8. What in the draft was wrong or unclear

The draft has no rule on **the date of the rows** in a point-in-time run, and in this case that decides Q1. The only door the draft opens for a technology-heavy consumer business ("What it does not rule out: a technology label on a consumer business", test 7, "Is the forecast about customers or about technology?") rests on **[M2017-019]** and **[M2023-030]**, both said after this anchor. And several rows the draft quotes as examples in Q1 (test 8), Q2 (test 8) and elsewhere name specific technology and consumer-hardware companies, so a blind case in this sector can be unblinded by the draft itself, and its hindsight can leak into the verdict. I treated post-anchor rows as statements of the draft's text, not as evidence about the case. I decided Q1 on the case and on rows dated before the anchor. The draft should say whether rows dated after the anchor may be used, and should keep named-company examples out of its tests or mark them. The second gap is that test 7 asks the right question but gives no criterion for telling a "consumer products" business from one with "a technological component that's of significance" (**[M1998-008]**). A filing that describes both, as this one does, gets no help from the draft, and the verdict becomes the analyst's preference. I resolved it by the evidence the case actually holds, which speaks to technology and not to customer behaviour. Two smaller defects: the protocol requires accession numbers that the Test B' case files do not carry, and Q7's unresolved pull between the bond rate (**[M1996-024]**) and the stated floor (**[M2003-149]**) leaves the value of any long-lived, low-capital business spanning a factor of about four at a 2.59% bond. Even a name that passed Q1 to Q6 could not get a usable range from Q7 until that pull is settled.
