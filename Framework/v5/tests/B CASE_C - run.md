## 3. Q1 to Q8

### Q1. CAN I UNDERSTAND IT? STOP. **Verdict: TOO HARD.**

**The test as the draft states it.** Understanding is "a reasonable fix on about what the earning power and competitive position will look like in five or 10 years" **[M2012-065]**, **[M2000-037]**; it is "the predictability of the economics of the situation 10 years out", not knowledge of the product **[M2000-104]**. The draft's tests 1 to 12 applied:

1. **Where will it be in ten years** **[M1997-021]**, **[M1999-132]**. Not answerable from the case. The description names four growth initiatives, each a technology bet (emerging markets aside): "business analytics, cloud computing, and instrumented 'smart' infrastructure". Cloud computing, which the company names as an initiative, is also a change in how its own customers buy servers, storage, data-centre outsourcing and software; which way that cuts on the two services segments and the systems segment over ten years is not visible from Item 1 and four years of tagged figures.
2. **Key variables and how predictable** **[M1998-044]**. Partly identifiable: the annuity base (about 60% of external services revenue, about two-thirds of software revenue), the backlog, the share count (1,456.9 to 1,287.4 diluted, 2007 to 2010, down 11.6%), and margin (net income over revenue 10.5% to 14.9%, 2007 to 2010, on revenue flat between 95,758 and 103,630). What decides whether the annuity holds for ten years (renewal and pricing on outsourcing and maintenance as the delivery model changes) is not predictable from the case. "If something is not very predictable, forget it." **[M1998-044]**
3. **Do the past statements tell me the future ones** **[M2008-033]**. The past four years show flat revenue and rising earnings per share. They do not tell the future ones, because the earnings rise came from margin and a shrinking count, not from volume, and the segments whose future is in question (systems including semiconductors; outsourcing) are exactly the ones a change in technology would reprice. Answer: no, with the case in hand.
4. **Important and knowable** **[M2006-076]**. The direction of the enterprise computing market over ten years is important to every segment and, from here, not knowable.
5. **Would insiders write it down** **[M2000-105]**: of ten-year forecasts "in the tech field", the row's answer is "That's too hard"; nothing in the case puts this business outside the field the row names. Its own systems segment sells semiconductors and servers.
6. **Can I name the winner, not just the industry** **[M2012-067]**, **[L2009-005]**. The description counts "hundreds of competitors worldwide", across global IT and consulting firms, offshore providers, accounting-firm consultancies, platform and database vendors and server makers. The company says it leads; the case gives no figure (share, relative cost, retention) by which the analyst can see that it will still lead in ten years.
7. **Customers or technology** **[M2023-030]**. Both. The customer half (large enterprises and governments with mission-critical systems and annuity contracts) is the readable part; the technology half (semiconductors, servers, storage, middleware, cloud) is the part that decides the systems segment and bears on the rest.
8. **How far off could I be** **[M2011-084]**. Wide: the model has five segments, a captive lender and 170 countries, and the variable that matters most (the effect of a shift in delivery technology on the annuity) has no range the case lets me draw.
9. **Do I doubt it is inside? Then it is not** **[M2002-092]**. I doubt it.
10. **Do I only think I understand it** **[M1997-024]**. The familiar shape of an enterprise-computing company is the risk the row names.
11. Abroad: not applicable as a separate test (home-country company), though 170 countries widen the model.
12. **The lender inside it** **[M2002-094]**, **[M2012-012]**, **[L2002-018]**. The financing segment holds two- to seven-year leases and loans and carries residual-value risk on returned equipment. The case carries no segment balance sheet, no receivable quality, no residual assumptions and no funding structure; the only evidence of the asset side is management's sentence that its knowledge "mitigates" the risk. Understanding each lease is not understanding the book **[M2002-094]**.

**The rows that decide it.** "a business that must deal with fast-moving technology is not going to lend itself to reliable evaluations of its long-term economics" **[L1993-023]**; "if something comes in where there's a technological component that's of significance, or where we think the future technology could hurt the business as it presently exists [...] it won't make it through the filter" **[M1998-008]**; "we have no insights into which participants in the tech field possess a truly durable competitive advantage" **[L1999-018]**, a problem "we can't solve by studying up" **[L1999-018]**, **[M2008-086]**. The description itself supplies the evidence the rows ask about: a decade spent "exiting commoditized segments while acquiring over 100 companies" shows a position held by continual remaking, and the draft carries that pattern under "A moat that must be continuously rebuilt will eventually be no moat at all" **[L2007-005]** and "industries where it's very hard to evaluate moats [...] the businesses of rapid change" **[M2001-069]**.

**The box.** The draft sends fast-moving technology and change to TOO HARD, not OUT (Q1, "Sent to TOO HARD, not OUT"): "It doesn't mean it isn't a good buy [...] It just means that we don't know how to evaluate it." **[M2000-038]**; the three boxes **[M2006-013]**. This is a statement about the analyst's reach from this case, not about the business: the annuity base and the flat-revenue, rising-margin record are real and are recorded below for the parent's scoring. Per **[M2001-006]**, a pass on something outside the circle is not counted an error.

**Filing facts it rests on.** Revenue, net income and diluted shares for 2007 to 2010 (CASE_C.json, filed 2010-02-23 and 2011-02-22, accession not carried); DESC_C.md paragraphs 1 to 4 (from the filing of record's Item 1, accession not carried).

## 8. What in the draft was wrong or unclear

Three things. First, the draft's own Q1 text quotes two rows that name a specific large enterprise-computing company, one in the change paragraph and test 7 (**[M2017-019]**, 2017, which ends "I was wrong on the first one") and one in test 8 (**[M2012-073]**, 2012, on the chance of "being way wrong" in that company); both post-date this anchor, and a blind run of any enterprise-computing case is handed a speaker's later verdict by the very text it must apply. I did not test whether that company is this case, and I did not rely on either row. The draft needs a rule for rows dated after a run's anchor (barred in point-in-time tests) and should not carry company-specific verdict rows inside its general tests. Second, Q1 has no instruction for a conglomerate of segments with different readability: here an annuity services and software base that could perhaps be foreseen sits beside a semiconductor and server segment and a captive lender that cannot. The draft does not say whether Q1 is answered for the whole, for the dominant segment, or segment by segment with the worst governing; I answered for the whole, since the segments share the customer and the technology risk, and said so. Third, the draft's TOO HARD for technology rests on rows saying study does not cure it, yet the protocol asks a TOO HARD to name "the document that would resolve it"; for a technology-change TOO HARD no document does, and the draft should say that such a box is terminal rather than awaiting evidence, distinct from a TOO HARD that a missing filing (here, the financing-segment note) could lift.
