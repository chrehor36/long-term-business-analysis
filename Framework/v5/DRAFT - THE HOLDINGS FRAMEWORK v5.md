# THE HOLDINGS FRAMEWORK — v5 (DRAFT, not in force)

**2026-10-04. Drafted from `principle_ledger_v5.csv` (the blind read of the Berkshire annual-meeting transcripts, 1994 to 2025, and the letters and signed annual-report sections they discuss), under the structure the operator approved on 2026-10-04 (`Framework/v5/synthesis/STRUCTURE DECISION 2026-10-04.md`, item 3: selling is asked after purchase, as the rows state it, and is the spine of this document).**

This is a candidate. It governs nothing. The current holdings framework governs every review of a position until a ruling case, written with quotes and put to the operator, says otherwise; until then this document is read, tested and argued with, and never applied to a holding.

**The standard.** Every rule is a verbatim quote from `principle_ledger_v5.csv`, with its id in bold and its year. Every number either carries the row it comes from or is labelled CONVENTION with a one-line rationale; otherwise it is not written. An absence is worded "no instance found in the v5 ledger" with the search that looked for it. Where the speakers pull against each other, the pull is carried as an open question in section V, not settled by preference.

**The reading rule.** STOP and WEIGHING carry the meanings of the purchase draft. A STOP is a question whose answer settles the matter on its ground: here, either that no action follows from it, or that action follows. A WEIGHING is a question whose answer is recorded and weighed with the others and does not settle the matter by itself. Where a question is a STOP on one ground and a WEIGHING on another, its heading says which.

**Where it sits.** The purchase draft asks this document's spine as its Q11, "having bought: has the business changed, or only its price?", and calls it a WEIGHING. This document unfolds that question into the questions an owner asks of a position, in the order the rows give them. Inside quotations `[...]` marks an elision in document order; the transcripts' own dashes, spellings and quotation marks are kept as printed.

# I. THE GOVERNING RULE

## The default is to keep

The rows state the owner's default before they state any test. It is to keep, and it is told to everyone in advance.

> "Unlike many business buyers, Berkshire has no "exit strategy." We buy to keep." — **[L2005-006]**, 2005

> "And so we will not — if a business has been disappointing to us, but we like the people there and we’re not having — not because of labor problems — and we’re not going to have to put money in incessantly, we will stay with it when business school theory and management theory would say get rid of it and do something else. [...] we want the shareholders to know that we have this mindset that may produce slightly suboptimal returns because of our attitude. But that’s the way we’re going to play it, and we tell people ahead of time that that’s the way we’re going to play it." — **[M2005-094]**, 2005

> "But we continue to think that it is usually foolish to part with an interest in a business that is both understandable and durably wonderful. Business interests of that kind are simply too hard to replace. [...] An investor should ordinarily hold a small piece of an outstanding business with the same tenacity that an owner would exhibit if he owned all of that business." — **[L1993-014]**, 1993

> "I mean the real thing to do with a great business is just hang on for dear life." — **[M1998-012]**, 1998

The same default is put as a way of buying: "the best thing to do is buy a stock that you don’t ever want to sell." **[M1998-011]**, and as the test of any investment, "The best way to look at any investment is, how will I feel if I own it forever, you know, and put all my family’s net worth in it?" **[M1998-158]**. Munger: "Generally speaking, trying to dance in and out of the companies you really love, on a long-term basis, has not been a good idea for most investors." **[M1999-078]**. Buffett, in 2019: "over the years, we want to keep the wonderful businesses." **[M2019-051]**. The reason given is arithmetic as well as temperament: "tax-paying investors will realize a far, far greater sum from a single investment that compounds internally at a given rate than from a succession of investments compounding at the same rate." **[L1993-008]**; and "Time is the friend of the wonderful business." **[L2010-013]**.

The default belongs to a kind of business, not to every position. "if you own a lousy business, you have to sell it at some point. I mean, if you own a group of lousy businesses, you better hope some of them get taken over or something happens. You need turnover. If you own a wonderful business, you know, you don’t want turnover, basically." **[M1995-060]**. The discipline that makes keeping safe sits at the entrance: L2005-006 goes on, "We do, though, have an entrance strategy, looking for businesses in this country or abroad that meet our six criteria and are available at a price that will produce a reasonable return." **[L2005-006]**; and because what is bought is kept, the buying must be right: "We like even less doing dumb deals because we do them forever." **[M2006-028]**; "we’re going to own these businesses forever. So, we want a business that we think is going to have, if run well, some kind of competitive advantage — over many decades." **[M2003-037]**.

## The quote serves the holder

> "the market is there to serve you and not to instruct you. That’s of enormous importance. When people talk about momentum in stocks or charting or any kind of things like that, they’re saying that the market is instructing you. The market doesn’t instruct us; the market is there to serve us. If it does something silly, we get a chance to do something because it’s doing something silly. We do it. But it doesn’t tell us anything. It just tells us prices. And if the price is out of line where the facts and reasoning lead you, then you — then action is called for. And if it doesn’t, you forget it and go play bridge that day and the next day, see whether there’s something new." — **[M2006-077]**, 2006

> "the only thing you have to do is to remember that this guy next door is there to serve you and not to instruct you. You bought the farm because you thought the farm was — had the potential. You don’t really need a quote on it. [...] And maybe he’ll name a silly price that he’ll buy at, in which case you sell, if you want to. Or maybe he’ll name a very low price and you’ll buy his farm from him. But you don’t have to. And you don’t want to put yourself in a position where you have to sell." — **[M2020-007]**, 2020

> "one of the important things in stocks is that the stock does not know that you own it. [...] All these feelings, and it has no impact whatsoever." — **[M1995-022]**, 1995

For the holder, the servant does three things and only three. It names a price at which he may sell if he wishes **[M2020-007]**; it names a price at which he may buy more, "if it goes down 25 percent in price or 30 percent in price, if you have more money available, buy more. And if you don’t, you know, so what?" **[M1997-144]**; and through the company's own buybacks it can raise his share without his paying anything, which is why a holder of a wonderful business that buys in its stock should want the price low: "we’d much rather have the stock at a lower price so we could buy more stock." **[M2019-044]**; "we hoped even after we were done buying and ran out of money that if it was cheap the company would keep buying, in effect, taking our interest up." **[M2022-027]**. What the quote does not do is measure the holding. "we do not measure the progress of our investments by what their market prices do during any given year." **[L2007-016]**; "we don’t mark up, and down, our wholly-owned businesses, based on stock market valuations." **[M2016-021]**; "I wouldn’t worry too much about what happens over this six months or this 12 months." **[M2012-097]**.

The servant serves only an owner who can never be forced to sell to it. "You’ve got to be prepared, when you buy a stock, to have it go down 50 percent — or more — and be comfortable with it, as long as you’re comfortable with the holding." and "if you hold it on borrowed money, you know, you could have been cleaned out. There wasn’t anything wrong with Berkshire when those three times occurred." **[M2020-022]**. The same reservation runs through the 1994 meeting: owners are better off when stocks get cheaper "unless you own your shares on borrowed money or are going to sell them in a very short period of time" **[M1994-036]**. This is the purchase draft's standing rule (never risk what you have and need) applied to the holder: a position held on terms that can force a sale has no servant, only a master.

## How the holding is measured

The rows measure a holding the way they measure a business owned outright.

> "Rather, we evaluate their performance by the two methods we apply to the businesses we own. The first test is improvement in earnings, with our making due allowance for industry conditions. The second test, more subjective, is whether their "moats" [...] have widened during the year." — **[L2007-016]**, 2007

For a part-owner the earnings measured are his share of what the business earns, whether paid out or not: "Our look-through earnings have grown at a good clip over the years, and our stock price has risen correspondingly.  Had those gains in earnings not materialized, there would have been little increase in Berkshire's value." **[L1996-038]** (the definition is **[L1994-024]**). The yearly figure is neither ignored nor obeyed: "Yearly figures, it should be noted, are neither to be ignored nor viewed as all-important." **[L2010-001]**; "We don't care about the bumps; what matters are the overall results." **[L2000-006]**. Munger in the 2009 panic: "And I wouldn’t get too excited about the fact that the stock goes down. [...] What matters are things like this" **[M2009-026]**. The difference between the two measures is one of visibility, not of reality: "When we’re right about businesses, it shows up in future earning power, which you can see, but it doesn’t jump out at you the same way changes in stock values do." **[M2014-044]**.

## The owner's conduct toward what he owns

The rows that describe Berkshire as an owner are rules about its own conduct; inverted, they are the private holder's conduct toward what he holds. Four carry over.

**He does not demand what a good owner refuses to demand.** "I think our chief contribution to the businesses we acquire is what we don’t do." **[M1995-090]**; "I can certainly create a structure that will take that passion away from them. And Berkshire is a negative art in that way." **[M2012-093]**. The structure named is the quarterly demand: "they try and force the divisions to come up with profits for every quarter that are better than the profits from the same quarter in the previous year. And a lot of terrible decisions and terrible practices creep into those businesses." **[M2009-039]**; and "our managerial decisions at Berkshire have been made from the viewpoint of the shareholders who are staying, not those who are leaving." **[L2018-003]**. A holder who judges a company by its quarter asks of it what the rows say breeds bad practice. Munger allows a first year or two of noise: "We don’t care what we report in the first year or two of — after buying anything." **[M1994-003]**.

**He measures, and against the best.** Leaving alone is not looking away. "we are measuring ourselves very carefully against what they do. And if changes are needed, we’ll do that." **[M2019-045]**; "I look at those figures every month, and I look at everybody else’s figures every month, and I try to — I’m always looking for any signs of underperformance" **[M2016-074]**. The review is continuous in thought and rare in action: "it is a continuous process, but it’s not a continuous process with the idea that daily activity, or weekly activity, or monthly activity, is going to result. It’s just we want to just keep adding to our thinking and knowledge, refining it further about every business that we’re in." **[M2007-105]**.

**He acts promptly on a problem he knows.** "a pound of cure, promptly applied, is worth a ton of cure that’s delayed. It — problems don’t go away." **[M2017-007]**; "The cardinal sin is delaying the correction of mistakes or what Charlie Munger called "thumb-sucking." Problems, he would tell me, cannot be wished away." **[L2024-002]**. Against this, the same speakers accept being late as the price of not supervising: "That means we are sometimes late in spotting management problems" **[L2009-007]**. The pull is carried in section V.

**He keeps the measure of his own bias.** The holder is the one who wants the holding to be right. "I wanted the business to succeed and wished my way into a series of bad decisions." **[L2012-012]**; "We try and avoid the worst anchoring effect, which is always your previous conclusion." **[M2016-054]**; "approval is often counter-productive because it sedates the brain and makes it less receptive to new facts or a re-examination of conclusions formed earlier." **[L2008-021]**. Buffett names the effect even of affection: "one of the reasons I stuck with the textile business as long as I did was that I liked Ken Chase so much." **[M2024-031]**.

# II. THE QUESTIONS, IN ORDER

**The order, and where it comes from.** The rows give five passages that list what an owner asks of something already held, and they do not agree exactly. The 2007 letter sets the price aside first and then asks of earnings and the moat **[L2007-016]**. The 2000 meeting puts the moat first, above the year's figures: "the number one question you want to ask yourself is whether the — could the competitive advantage have been made stronger and more durable before — and that’s more important than the P&L for a given year." **[M2000-075]**. The 1995 castle passage, asked at purchase, runs castle, then the lord, then the proceeds **[M1995-038]**. The 2009 answer on selling runs management, advantage, mistake **[M2009-040]**. The 2020 answer runs the business, the management, and whether the business has fundamentally changed **[M2020-008]**. This draft takes the 2007 opening (separate the price from the business), then the 1995 sequence (castle, lord, proceeds), then the two reasons the selling rows add (a better use for the money **[M1998-012]**, **[M1998-013]**, and a mistake recognised **[M2009-040]**). The arrangement is the drafter's; the order inside it is the speakers'. Management second, as in M2009-040, would be equally sourced, and is recorded as an open question.

**The burden.** Before any question, the rows place the burden on the reason to sell: "when in doubt, keep holding. But it’s no inviolable rule." **[M2009-040]**; "the presumption should be that you just hold it and don’t worry." **[M1997-144]**.

## Q1 — WHAT HAS MOVED: THE BUSINESS, OR ONLY ITS PRICE? STOP against selling on the price alone; WEIGHING on a silly price.

**The question in the speakers' words.**

> "But the truth is, if you owned the businesses you liked prior to the virus arriving — it changes prices and it changes — but nobody’s forcing you to sell. And if you really like the business, and you like the management you’re in with, and the business hasn’t fundamentally changed" — **[M2020-008]**, 2020

> "if the stock goes down 20 or 30 or 40 percent, we don’t feel we’re poor in that situation. We felt we were poor, in terms of what had actually happened to those airline businesses, just as if we owned a hundred percent of them." — **[M2020-018]**, 2020

> "Just look at the business and judge how it’s doing." — **[M1997-144]**, 1997

### The tests

1. **Would I feel poorer if I owned all of it?** The loss is measured as an owner of the whole business would measure it, by what happened to the business, not to the stock **[M2020-018]**. A fall in the quote with no change in the business is no loss of this kind.
2. **Is this a fall, a gain, or a silly price?** Each has its own row.
   - *A fall* is not a reason to sell, and is a reason to buy more if money is available **[M1997-144]**, **[M2010-074]**: "When it goes down we love it, because we’ll buy more." **[M2010-074]**. "Selling fine businesses on "scary" news is usually a bad decision." **[L1996-009]**. The stop-loss is ruled out by name, by the picture of a house: "it’s worth $100,000 and you tell your broker, you know, if anybody ever comes along and offers 90, you want to sell it." **[M1994-064]**.
   - *A gain* is not a reason either. "we have a large, unrealized gain in it, but that doesn’t have anything to do with our decision-making." **[M2018-014]**. Growth into a large share of the portfolio is not a reason: "To suggest that this investor should sell off portions of his most successful investments simply because they have come to dominate his portfolio is akin to suggesting that the Bulls trade Michael Jordan" **[L1996-025]**. A price too high to add at is not a price to sell at: "We don’t find them uncomfortable, in the sense that we want to sell them." **[M1998-160]**; "neither one of those businesses got to the price that left us happy putting new money in. But we’re quite happy, very happy, owning those businesses" **[M1999-077]**; "If the choice is between a questionable business at a comfortable price or a comfortable business at a questionable price, we much prefer the latter." **[L1999-021]**.
   - *A silly price* is the one price the rows let count. "If you own some good businesses and you bought them at the right price, if they get to a silly price, you probably should sell them." **[M2007-089]**; the servant may "name a silly price that he’ll buy at, in which case you sell, if you want to." **[M2020-007]**; and Munger allows that "If you’re right about the companies, you can hold them at pretty high values." **[M1996-096]**.
3. **Is there a zone between buying and selling, and am I in it?** "we’re in a zone in some of those securities that — where we wouldn’t buy and we wouldn’t sell. [...] there is a zone, which because of size, because of taxes, where we would neither be a buyer nor a seller." **[M2005-071]**. Most of the time a liked holding sits in that zone, and the right act is none.
4. **Am I selling to buy back lower?** "You have to make two decisions right. You know, you have to buy — you have to sell it right first, and then you have to buy it right later on. And usually you have to pay some tax in-between." **[M1999-079]**; "We don’t think we’re very good at doing that. We’d rather just sit and hold the business and pretend the stock market doesn’t exist." **[M1996-097]**.
5. **Is a tax reason doing the deciding?** "I made a mistake a couple years ago when I sold some shares when I had certain reasons why gains were useful to take that year from a tax standpoint. But having heard me say that, it was a dumb decision." **[M2023-035]**; and the other way, "We own the businesses we want to own. We don’t own them because taxes have restrained us from selling them." **[M1998-060]**.

### Why it is a STOP on the price alone, and a WEIGHING on a silly price

A move in price, up or down, with the business unchanged, closes the question with no sale: the stock "does not know that you own it" **[M1995-022]**, and the rows name each form of price-driven selling as an error (**[L1996-009]**, **[M2018-014]**, **[L1996-025]**, **[M1999-079]**, **[M2023-035]**). The silly price is not a STOP. M2007-089 says "probably", M2020-007 says "if you want to", and the speakers' own record runs both ways. They call not selling in the bubble a mistake: "I made a big mistake in not selling several of our larger holdings during The Great Bubble." **[L2003-021]**; "I talked when I should have walked." **[L2004-011]**; "clearly, at 50 times earnings, it was a silly price on the stock. So we like it. We’ll own it ten years from now, in my view." **[M2006-029]**. And they call selling near the top a sign of the wrong game: "I would worry, frankly, if I sold a bunch of things right at the top, because that would indicate that, in effect, I was practicing the bigger fool-type approach to investing" **[M1998-101]**; "we’re not good at the game of, when it gets into the speculative area, figuring out how far a speculative boom will go." **[M2006-019]**. The rows give no multiple at which a price becomes silly; "if you ever attend a meeting here, and there are 60 or 70 times earnings, keep an eye on me." **[M1996-095]** and the "50 times" of M2006-029 are remarks about cases, and this draft writes no threshold.

### What it rules OUT

Selling on a fall; selling on scary news **[L1996-009]**; a stop-loss **[M1994-064]**; trimming a winner because it has grown **[L1996-025]**; selling to rebuy lower **[M1999-079]**; selling for a tax reason **[M2023-035]**; and selling to make a point about a company: "we don’t sell stocks, you know, basically to try and drive them down in price." **[M2007-137]**.

## Q2 — HAS THE CASTLE CHANGED? Is the moat wider or narrower, and has the business or its industry changed in a way that matters ten years out? WEIGHING, with action at the life-threatening grade.

**The question in the speakers' words.**

> "We love buying stocks where we think the businesses are so solid, have such economic advantage, that we can essentially ride with them forever. [...] So we will occasionally reevaluate the economic characteristics that we see 10 years out from the ones that we saw 10 years ago and maybe come to a somewhat different conclusion." — **[M2000-054]**, 2000

> "you have to be thinking all the time and alert to whether there’s been something that really changes the game in a big way. And that’s not only true for American Express, that’s true for other things we own, including things we own 100 percent of. And we’ll be wrong sometimes. We’ll be late sometimes, we’ll be wrong sometimes. But we’ll be right sometimes, too. But it’s not that we’re not cognizant of threats. Assessing the probabilities of those threats being a minor problem, or a major problem, or a life-threatening problem, you know, it’s a tough game" — **[M2016-081]**, 2016

> "So now we sell — really when we think that we’ve — when we’re reevaluating the economic characteristics of the business. [...] But we probably don’t think that their competitive advantage is as strong as we might have thought — as we thought it was — when we initially made the decision." — **[M2002-004]**, 2002

### The tests

1. **Did the moat widen this year?** The year-to-year question is the moat, ranked above the year's profit **[M2000-075]**, with earnings allowed for industry conditions as the companion test **[L2007-016]**. "what really tells you something is if you know how to figure out how wide the moat is and whether it’s likely to widen further or shrink on you." **[M1999-108]**.
2. **Has the long-term trend line of the core changed?** Buffett's Gillette answer is the form: "I see nothing that’s happened in the last year, in terms of the long-term trend line, of the blade and razor business" **[M1999-080]**. The horizon is the purchase horizon, ten years out **[M2000-054]**, and the definition of the risk is the purchase draft's: "what can happen — say five, 10, 15 years from now — that will destroy, or modify, or reduce the economic strengths that we perceive currently exist in a business." **[M2000-014]**.
3. **Is the change in the company, or in its industry?** The rows sell on an industry judgment where the companies are fine: the reinsurers, "It was not a negative judgment on their managements. But it was a — at least — a mildly negative judgment on the reinsurance business." **[M2016-008]**; the airlines, "I think it changed in a very major way." **[M2020-017]**. Values can be changed from outside the business, by courts and by society: "values change and they don’t always change upward." **[M2025-045]**; location can be re-judged soon after buying **[M2023-038]**.
4. **Is it a scandal or a break in the earning power?** The test for an event: "I don’t think the earning power of Walmart five years from now will be materially affected by the outcome of this situation." **[M2012-051]**. Problems at a great institution have been the occasion of the greatest purchases, not sales: "They cleaned it up, and look where American Express has moved since that time." **[M2018-013]**.
5. **Grade the threat.** Minor, major or life-threatening **[M2016-081]**. A slow decline is not on its own a reason: "we can’t change — make a portfolio change — every time something is a little less advantaged than it used to be." **[M2016-080]**. A major threat can be held through: of the self-driving car, "it would not cause us to be thinking for one second about selling GEICO." **[M2014-048]**. The life-threatening grade is the one the rows act on: "you do not want to try and fix something that’s unfixable." **[M2016-031]**; "those businesses will wither and die. And we’d better use the money someplace else." **[M2019-012]**.
6. **Is the change slow enough to miss?** "slow change can be much harder to perceive, and can lull you to sleep easier, sometimes than when rapid change is clearly in sight." **[M2014-038]**. The base rate is harsh: "most big businesses — eventually fall into mediocrity or worse." **[M2008-031]**; Munger, "the old moats, some of them are getting filled in." **[M2001-070]**. The newspaper case is the worked example of insiders who did not see their own erosion **[L2006-008]**, and the Kraft Heinz case of a brand whose protection against the retailer was misjudged **[M2019-041]**.
7. **Is the change for the better?** The castle question runs both ways. "We were slow to recognize the change, but better late than never." **[L2022-020]**.

### Why it is a WEIGHING, with action at the top grade

The rows grade rather than rule. The moat test is "more subjective" **[L2007-016]**; the grading is "a tough game" with "We’ll be late sometimes, we’ll be wrong sometimes." **[M2016-081]**; and a slow loss of advantage is expressly not a reason to change the portfolio **[M2016-080]**. At the top grade the rows act without weighing: where some force "you may not have the answer for it. And then, you know, you get out of that business." **[M2016-031]**, and for a business that will drain cash, "we will take prompt and decisive action." **[L2011-007]**. Between the grades, the change of industry M2016-008 and the change of the castle M2002-004 are reasons the rows act on for marketable holdings and not for controlled businesses (section III).

### What it rules OUT

Holding a business whose castle has been judged lost because it was once wonderful; feeding a business whose decline is life-threatening **[M2016-031]**, **[M2020-026]**; selling a business on a threat graded minor **[M2012-051]**, **[M2016-080]**; and treating a slow decline as no change at all **[M2014-038]**.

## Q3 — HAS THE MANAGEMENT CHANGED, OR CHANGED AFTER BEING PAID? WEIGHING on ability; on trust, the rows press prompt action.

**The question in the speakers' words.**

> "if we lose confidence in the management" — **[M2009-040]**, 2009

> "Assessing whether a manager, who I’m going to hand a billion dollars to, for his business, and he is going to hand me a stock certificate, assessing whether he’s going to behave differently in the future in running that business than he has in the past when he owned it, that’s incredibly important, but there’s no checklist in the world that’s going to answer that." — **[M2016-066]**, 2016

> "are they going to have the same passion for the business after they hand us the stock certificate and we hand them the money? Are they going to have the same passion that they had beforehand? And if we’re wrong on that, no contract is going to save us." — **[M2009-072]**, 2009

At purchase this is a forecast **[M2015-005]**; for a holder it is a record. The question is asked again with the evidence the years have supplied: did the manager, once paid, behave as before **[M2005-095]**: "how the people behave with us after we buy the business is an important part of how we feel about, you know, the whole relationship as well as the returns achieved." **[M2005-095]**.

### The tests

1. **Does he still love the business more than the money?** The purchase test, read now from conduct: "Does he love the business or does he love the money?" **[M2000-053]**; and the owner's duty not to kill that love: "all we have to do is avoid anything that, on our part, that diminishes that love of the business" **[M1998-061]**.
2. **Has my confidence in him soured, and on what?** The Tesco sale is the worked case: "In 2013, I soured somewhat on the company's then-management and sold 114 million shares, realizing a profit of $43 million. My leisurely pace in making sales would prove expensive. [...] You see a cockroach in your kitchen; as the days go by, you meet his relatives." **[L2014-003]**.
3. **Is it a business mistake or a matter of trust?** The rows split the two: "We are understanding about business mistakes; our tolerance for personal misconduct is zero." **[L2022-001]**; "we wouldn’t hire anybody, no matter how able, if we didn’t trust them." **[M2015-047]**; and the warning that applies to every holder who thinks he can watch a doubtful manager: "Never deal with a rascal under the expectation that you can prevent him from cheating you. [...] People are not that easy to read. Sincerity and empathy can easily be faked." **[L2023-003]**.
4. **When he found a problem, did he act?** The rows read management by its response: "the main problem was they didn’t act when they learned about it." **[M2017-005]**; Wells Fargo and Salomon, "he heard about a pyromaniac, and he let him keep the box of matches." **[M2019-005]**.
5. **Does he admit a mistake?** "I have also been a director of large public companies at which "mistake" or "wrong" were forbidden words at board meetings or analyst calls. That taboo, implying managerial perfection, always made me nervous" **[L2024-003]**.
6. **Is the successor the right one, and would the board change him?** "the number one risk factor is that this business gets the wrong management. And you get a guy or a woman in charge of it that are — they’re personable, the directors like them. They don’t know what they’re doing, but they know how to put on an appearance. That’s the biggest single danger that a business — and that that person stays and runs it for 10 or 15 years" **[M2021-042]**; and the difficulty the board has in replacing a likable mediocre chief executive **[M2013-071]**.
7. **How much does the business depend on him?** The weight given to a change of manager depends on the business. "it’s hard to judge successor management in a really good business. Because if they don’t show up at the office, it’ll keep working for a long time." **[M2023-045]**; Buffett on keeping a manager past his best, "our businesses are so good that they run better sometimes when — I mean, I’ve talked about Wesco" and "I don’t think it made any difference in the results." **[M2023-006]**. The other way: where the purchase rested on one man, his going matters more, "I wouldn’t have bought it without him in charge." **[M2018-007]**.

### Why it is a WEIGHING on ability, and what the rows press on trust

Ability is weighed against the business. The rows say the business decides more often than the manager: "We’ve been wrong about the economics of the business sometimes. But that’s our mistake, not theirs. We’ve seldom been wrong about the people." **[M2000-100]**; and a declining manager in a very good business did no visible harm **[M2023-006]**. Trust is not weighed in the same way. The rows put zero tolerance on misconduct **[L2022-001]**, call delay in correcting the cardinal sin **[L2024-002]**, and narrate the cost of dawdling once confidence soured **[L2014-003]**. Whether a loss of trust in the management of a marketable holding is a STOP on holding is not stated as a rule for holdings in the rows read for this draft; it is carried as an open question in section V. The rows say plainly that liking a manager is no reason to keep a business that should be given up **[M2024-031]**.

### What it rules OUT

Keeping a holding because the manager is likable when the business has failed **[M2024-031]**, **[M2022-039]**; believing a doubtful manager can be watched **[L2023-003]**; and slow action once confidence is lost **[L2014-003]**, **[L2024-002]**.

## Q4 — IS THE MONEY KEPT STILL BECOMING MORE THAN A DOLLAR? Retention, buybacks and issuance, judged over time. WEIGHING, with one STOP on further money into a business that chews it up.

**The question in the speakers' words.** For a holder the retention test is the test of what the company has done with his money while he held it.

> "We test the wisdom of retaining earnings by assessing whether retention, over time, delivers shareholders at least $1 of market value for each $1 retained." — **[R1995-009]**, 1995

> "the efficacy with which retained earnings will be deployed in the future. [...] Some companies will turn these retained dollars into fifty-cent pieces, others into two-dollar bills. [...] That's because an outside investor stands by helplessly as management reinvests his share of the company's earnings. If a CEO can be expected to do this job well, the reinvestment prospects add to the company's current value; if the CEO's talents or motives are suspect, today's value must be discounted." — **[L2010-002]**, 2010

> "if after three or four years, you’ve found that the dollars we’ve retained hasn’t created more of that in value, then the presumption becomes very strong at that point that we should start paying out money. But almost any management that wants to retain money is going to rationalize it by saying, “We’re going to do wonderful things with the money we retain.” And we think there should be checks on that" — **[M1998-110]**, 1998

### The tests

1. **Over the years held, did value per share rise by at least what was kept?** The span is "three or four years" **[M1998-110]** at the 1998 meeting and five years in the 1995 and 2009 reports **[R1995-009]**, **[R2009-002]**; the check is "I don’t mean every day or every week, but over time" **[M2010-046]**. The market leg alone can mislead in a falling market, which is why the 2009 report rewrote the test **[R2009-002]**; the holder reads the earnings progress against the capital retained: "the real test will be whether the earnings progress at a rate that’s commensurate with the amount of capital that’s being retained." **[M2011-072]**.
2. **Can the business still use what it earns?** "it would be an enormous mistake for See’s Candy to retain money." **[M2008-104]**; "if we really thought we never could use the money effectively in the business, we should get it out, one way or another." **[M2018-028]**. A business that grows its retained capital into a declining operation is the warned case: "A CEO with capital employed in a declining operation seldom elects to massively redeploy that capital into unrelated activities." **[L2014-016]**.
3. **Were shares bought back below value, and only then?** "Continuing shareholders are hurt unless shares are purchased below intrinsic value." **[L2011-003]**; "only when you think you’re doing it at a price where the remaining shareholders have had — are worth more the moment after you repurchased it than they were the moment before." **[M2019-002]**; the partner picture, "If you had a partnership and the partner wanted to sell out to you at 120 percent of what the business is worth, you’d say forget it." **[M2015-074]**. A programme that buys "regardless of price" is the sign to read: "I’ve been around a lot of managements that announce they’re going to buy X worth and then they buy it regardless of price." **[M2015-074]**.
4. **Were shares issued for as much as was given?** "The intrinsic value of the shares you give in an acquisition must not be greater than the intrinsic value of the business you receive." **[L2014-012]**. Buffett's own narration is the warning for a holder whose company pays in stock: "almost any time we’ve issued shares, it’s been a mistake." **[M2015-026]**.
5. **Did the company look back honestly at its deals?** "Post mortems of acquisitions, in which reality is honestly compared to the original projections, are rare in American boardrooms. They should instead be standard practice." **[L2014-013]**; the Washington Post's review "three years after they are made" is the named exception **[L2000-014]**.
6. **Is the company still treating the owners as partners?** "we’ll never do anything to make them sell it cheap. And we’ll tell them the truth about what the business is." **[M2023-089]**; decisions made "from the viewpoint of the shareholders who are staying" **[L2018-003]**.

### Why it is a WEIGHING, and the one STOP

The retention test raises a presumption **[M1998-110]** and shifts a burden of proof: "the burden of proof has shifted dramatically to us to explain why we would be retaining earnings at that point." **[M2005-021]**. Those are words of weight. The STOP is on further money: "Berkshire can sustain financial surprises but we will not knowingly throw good money after bad." **[L2023-010]**; "we will not fund a company that — where we think that it’s going to chew up money in the future." **[M2020-026]**; "we‘re not going to throw good money after bad." **[M2024-010]**. For a private holder the further money is his own add (section IV), and the money the company keeps from him in a business that will chew it up; the rows do not tell a minority holder he must sell on this ground, and that reading is the drafter's, flagged as such.

### What it rules OUT

Accepting retention because management promises to use it well **[M1998-110]**; a buyback read as good without a price **[M2015-074]**, **[L2011-003]**; and stock paid for a business worth less than the stock **[L2014-012]**.

## Q5 — IS THERE A PLAINLY BETTER USE FOR THIS MONEY? WEIGHING.

**The question in the speakers' words.**

> "So you sell if you need money for something else. You may sell if you believe the valuations between different kinds of markets are somewhat out of whack." — **[M1998-012]**, 1998

> "the sales that do happen, the ideal way is when you found something you like immensely better. Isn’t that obvious that’s the ideal way to sell?" — **[M1998-013]**, 1998

> "We like to add to present positions. I mean, those are companies we know, understand, obviously like to some degree. And if the price gets reasonably attractive and we’ve got money around, we will add. If we can find a good business to buy, you know, we will sell the least attractive." — **[M2007-107]**, 2007

### The tests

1. **What is the least attractive thing I hold?** That is the holder's opportunity cost: "my opportunity cost, as Charlie would put it, then, was the least attractive stock which I would give up to buy something more attractive." **[M2007-104]**. Every new use competes with "doing more of some of the things we’re already in." **[M2001-084]**.
2. **Is the better thing better by a wide margin, and surer?** "immensely better" **[M1998-013]**; Munger's comparison, "if I have something available that I think will give me 8 percent for sure and I can buy all I want of it, and you’ve got a perfectly good investment that I think will earn 7, I don’t have to waste 5 minutes with you." **[M2007-098]**. A swap of one wonderful business for a cheaper one carries the two-decisions cost of Q1 **[M1999-079]**.
3. **Do I have more ideas than money, or more money than ideas?** The reason changes with the holder's position: "Forty years ago my sales were all because I found something that I liked even better. I hated to sell what I sold, but I also didn’t want to borrow money, so I would reluctantly sell something that I thought was terribly cheap to buy something that was even cheaper. Those were the times when I had more ideas than money. Now I’ve got more money than ideas, and that’s a different equation." **[M2002-004]**; "my decision to sell almost always was based on the fact that I found something else I was dying to buy." **[M2000-055]**. With more money than ideas, "the option is cash" **[M2007-105]**, and a holding is not sold to raise cash that will sit.
4. **Is the boat sinking, and is the other seaworthy?** "Don't bail away in a sinking boat if you can swim to one that is seaworthy." **[L2022-014]**.

### Why it is a WEIGHING

The rows call this the ideal reason for a sale **[M1998-013]** and narrate its cost in the same breath: "the Disney sale in the ’60s was a huge mistake. I should have been buying, forget about holding, and — That’s happened many times. I mean, we think that anything we sell should go up subsequently, because we own good businesses and we may sell them because we need money for something else, but we still think they’re good businesses, and we think good businesses are going to be worth more over time." **[M1998-100]**; the GEICO stake sold early **[L1995-012]**. For controlled businesses the rows refuse this reason outright: "simply because we can use the money better someplace else, we’re not interested in it." **[M2001-097]**; "we’ll never sell a business to buy some other business." **[M2011-099]**. So the better use is weighed for marketable holdings and refused for whole businesses (section III).

### What it rules OUT

Selling a wonderful business for a marginally better idea **[M1999-079]**, **[L1995-012]**; selling to hold cash with nothing better in sight **[M2007-105]**; and a sale whose only ground is that something else is cheaper by a little **[M2007-098]**.

## Q6 — WAS IT A MISTAKE TO BUY, NOW RECOGNISED? WEIGHING on the finding; once found, the rows press action, whole and prompt.

**The question in the speakers' words.**

> "if we recognize we made a mistake when we went into it — we sell plenty of times." — **[M2009-040]**, 2009

> "I just decided that I’d made a mistake in evaluating — it was an understandable mistake. It was a probability-weighted decision when we bought that we were getting an attractive amount for our money" — **[M2020-017]**, 2020

> "sucking my thumb when new facts came in that should have caused me to re-examine my thinking and promptly take action." — **[L2008-003]**, 2008

### The tests

1. **Was the purchase analysis wrong, or did the world change?** The rows separate the two. Buffett's account of his acquisition errors: "it’s where I misgauged the competitive position of the business. It isn’t because of the faults of management. It’s because I just — either because I had too much money around or because I was — been drinking too much Cherry Coke or whatever it was — I assessed the future competitive position in a way that was really inappropriate. But it wasn’t because it really changed on me so much." **[M2012-031]**; "the mistakes are always about making an improper assessment of the economic conditions in the future of the industry of the company." **[M2016-065]**. A change in the world goes to Q2; a misjudgment at entry is this question.
2. **What new facts have come in, and have I written them down?** "he had to write it down in the first 30 minutes or the mind was such that it would reject contrary evidence to cherished beliefs." **[M1997-127]**; "when you find information that contradicts previously cherished beliefs, that you’ve got a special obligation to look at it and look at it quickly." **[M1998-004]**; Munger quoting Keynes, "we would reserve the right to change our mind." **[M1994-020]**.
3. **Am I working backwards from the answer I want?** "The usual cause of failure is that they start with the answer they want and then work backwards to find a supporting rationale." **[L2012-012]**; and the rows' antidote, "We really try and destroy our previous ideas." **[M2016-054]**.
4. **If I change my mind, is it the whole position?** "when we change our mind, we don’t take half measure or anything of the sort." **[M2020-020]**; "We don't back into decisions." **[L1998-021]**.

### Why it is a WEIGHING on the finding, and why the rows press action once it is made

Whether the purchase was a mistake is judged: the airlines were "a probability-weighted decision" **[M2020-017]**, and a bad result is not proof of a bad decision. Once the finding is made, the rows narrate only the cost of delay: textiles, "recognizing reality, when it’s really awful, and taking appropriate action, just involves, often, just the most elementary good sense." **[M2022-038]**; "I myself delayed abandoning our obsolete textile mills for far too long." **[L2014-016]**; Tesco **[L2014-003]**; "generally, you do it, probably, a little too late, even, because you keep hoping the business will bounce back up" **[M2009-090]**. The same rows also say, "Agonizing over errors is a mistake. But acknowledging and analyzing them can be useful" **[L2000-014]**.

### What it rules OUT

Keeping a recognised mistake to avoid admitting it **[L2014-016]**, **[L2012-012]**; a half measure once the mind is changed **[M2020-020]**; and selling a good holding because its price fell and calling that a recognised mistake (Q1).

# III. THE HOLDING NOT SOLD AT ANY PRICE

> "regardless of price, we have no interest at all in selling any good businesses that Berkshire owns, and are very reluctant to sell sub-par businesses as long as we expect them to generate at least some cash and as long as we feel good about their managers and labor relations." — **[R1995-011]**, 1995

> "when we buy a business it’s for keeps. And we make only two exceptions: when they promise to start losing money indefinitely or if we have major labor problems. But otherwise, we are not going to sell something just ’cause we get offered more money for it, even than it’s worth." — **[M2009-041]**, 2009

> "And we would never, never sell an operation like that, ever." — **[M2022-015]**, 2022

The rows hold this line in every era: "we don’t break off the relationships that we develop simply because we get offered a fancy price for something." **[M2000-052]**; "a fancy price will not tempt us." **[M2001-097]**; Munger's list of Berkshire's conduct, "Berkshire would almost never sell a subsidiary." **[L2014-037]**; "I want it to be for keeps." **[M2012-024]**. Munger: "We almost never sell an operating business. And when it does happen, it’s usually because we’ve got some trouble we can’t fix." **[M2000-056]**. The cost is named and accepted: "We don’t see any reason to make an extra half a percent a year or 1 percent a year" **[M1995-044]**; the policy is told in advance **[M2005-094]**. Buffett's advice to a family owner is the same: "So we never urge people to sell good businesses. We urge them to keep them." **[M2008-040]**; "Frequently, the best decision is to do nothing." **[L2014-017]**.

## The qualifications the rows carry

**It covers controlled businesses, not marketable securities.** "That principle covers controlled businesses, not marketable securities. This year I've added a final sentence to #11 to ensure that our owners understand that we regard any marketable security as available for sale, however unlikely such a sale now seems." **[L2016-010]** (also **[R2016-001]**); "at some price, you have to be willing to sell something that’s a marketable security, forgetting about a controlled business." **[M1996-095]**. Munger gives the reason the loyalty does not carry over: "I don’t think the loyalty effect in lots of public companies is nearly as important as it is with the private companies." **[M2001-099]**. What carries over to a part-interest is the tenacity, not the loyalty: an investor "should ordinarily hold a small piece of an outstanding business with the same tenacity that an owner would exhibit if he owned all of that business." **[L1993-014]**.

**The solvency qualification: it must not drain cash.** The promise has a floor in the cash the business yields. R1995-011 keeps sub-par businesses "as long as we expect them to generate at least some cash" **[R1995-011]**; the exception is "a permanent loss of cash for as far as the eye can see" **[M2001-097]**; "if the business will likely be a cash drain over the longer term, or if labor strife is endemic [...] we will take prompt and decisive action." **[L2011-007]**; "we think its prospects are that it will continually lose money in the future, that we will certainly — we’ll try to sell it to somebody else — but one way or another, we will not continue to hold it." **[M2020-028]**; and the condition "we’re not going to have to put money in incessantly" **[M2005-094]**. The promise never extends to feeding the business **[L2023-010]**, **[M2020-026]**.

**It is a policy for Berkshire, and Buffett says it would be wrong for a trustee.** "we will stick with the businesses, even though it would be a mistake if you were acting as a trustee for, you know, a bunch of crippled children or something of the sort. And that’s just our policy at Berkshire." **[M2009-046]**. The rows thus mark the no-price rule as a chosen cost of a particular owner with particular relationships, not as a rule of investing. Whether a private holder adopts it is carried in section V.

**The holder's own solvency.** The no-price rule also assumes an owner who is never forced: "you don’t want to put yourself in a position where you have to sell." **[M2020-007]**; "Just take the people that get very rich and then leverage themselves up in some way that they lose everything." **[M2016-062]**.

# IV. ADDS ARE PURCHASES

The rows carry the idea in part. They treat buying more of a holding as a purchase in two respects: it must clear a price, and it competes with every other use of the money.

> "Before looking at new investments, we consider adding to old ones.  If a business is attractive enough to buy once, it may well pay to repeat the process." — **[L1994-032]**, 1994

> "the ideal purchase is to find — is to have something that you already liked be selling at a price where you feel like buying more of it." — **[M1998-014]**, 1998

**The price is asked again.** A price that is fine for holding can be wrong for adding: "our enthusiasm for buying more of these wonderful companies is less than it was when the P/E ratios were substantially less." **[M1999-055]**; "That's why we haven't added to our present holdings." **[L1999-021]**; "neither one of those businesses got to the price that left us happy putting new money in." **[M1999-077]**; "if the price gets reasonably attractive and we’ve got money around, we will add." **[M2007-107]**. The asymmetry is the rows' own: the same price stops the add and does not start a sale **[M1998-160]**.

**The add competes with the alternatives.** "The cost of every deal we do is measured by the second best deal that’s around at a given time, including more — doing more of some of the things we’re already in." **[M2001-084]**; "Why would I rather have this than more Coca-Cola?" **[M1995-105]**. The comparison runs both ways: more of the holding is the yardstick for anything new, and anything new is the yardstick for more of the holding.

**What the add borrows from the first purchase.** The rows let the add rest on knowledge already earned: "those are companies we know, understand, obviously like to some degree." **[M2007-107]**. A row that says an add must pass again every question asked at purchase (understanding, the castle, the people, the use of money): no instance found in the v5 ledger, by a search of the quote text for "new money", "add to", "adding to", "buy more", "bought more" and "more of", and by reading the rows that search returned. The nearest are L1994-032 and M2007-107, which assume the knowledge carries over, and the questions of section II, which an owner asks of the holding whether or not he adds. This draft therefore reads an add as a purchase on price (purchase draft Q7) and on the comparison (purchase draft Q8), with the holding's answers to the questions of section II standing in for the purchase draft's Q1 to Q6; that reading is the drafter's.

**The add the rows refuse.** No further money into a business that will chew it up **[L2023-010]**, **[M2020-026]**; and the textile lesson, buying more of what is failing: "in a final burst of brilliance, I went out and bought another textile company." **[L2010-004]**. The sizing rows of the purchase draft (Q10) govern the size of an add: "if we like a business, we’re going to buy as much of it as we can and keep it as long as we can." **[M2020-020]**.

# VII. THE REVIEW

1. Start from the default: the position is kept unless a reason the rows name is found; "when in doubt, keep holding" **[M2009-040]**.
2. Confirm the owner cannot be forced to sell: no borrowed money behind it, nothing that can call it **[M2020-007]**, **[M2020-022]**.
3. Q1: separate what the quote did from what the business did; a move in price alone closes Q1 with no sale, and only a silly price is weighed **[M2020-018]**, **[M2007-089]**.
