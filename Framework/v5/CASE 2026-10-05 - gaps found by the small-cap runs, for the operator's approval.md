## A. Q4's two-tell convention: STOP, or weigh?

### What the runs found
ENSG closed OUT at Q4 on the convention "The make-the-numbers habit, tested": a record of raising guidance every year and
landing above the first range, plus earnings releases that feature an adjusted figure excluding stock pay. The same run found
no confusion and no accounting tell in the statements: operating cash above net income every year read, receivables falling
against revenue, GAAP first in every headline, every adjustment reconciled, and management charging stock pay back into its
own bonus base. Its own words: the convention "cannot tell a company whose cash confirms its earnings [...] from one massaging
numbers", and a business found IN at Q1 and Q2 "closes on its press-release habits before its management (Q5) or its price
(Q7) is judged". It also found that the Q4 STOP names no box (it wrote OUT). The convention itself came from the TJX pair of
Test A' (gap (j) of the adoption ruling case), where the two analysts split on whether a run of "above plan" releases alone
was the tell.

4. **The habit is a Q5 fact.** Q4 stops only on what the accounts show **[M1995-063]**, **[M1995-065]**, **[M2003-029]**; the

### What the corpus decides, and what it leaves open

"a total exclusionary factor" **[M1994-018]**, and that confusion or suspicion about the accounts closes the file
**[M1995-063]**, **[M1995-065]**, **[M2003-029]**. **It does not decide** how many tells make suspicion: no row counts them, and
the rows on the habit range from "incredulous" **[L2002-041]** to "they’re deluded" **[M2013-087]**. The two-tell count is ours,
and reading 1 rests on it. What the rows do show is where the habit rows point: at the CEO and the culture, which is Q5's
object. That supports readings 3 and 4 over 1 and 2, but it is a reading of where the rows point, not a row that says so.

### The readings
1. **The whole.** Q2 asks of the castle of the company taken whole; the parts are evidence. Supported by **[M2023-031]**.
   HNI and IOSP would read the dominant castle; MTRN would turn on the whole's pricing power.
2. **By parts, the decisive part.** Each reported segment (or each business inside one, where the filing separates them) is read
   for its castle **[L2008-005]**. A part whose castle is open does not close the whole; it is carried to Q7 as an ordinary
   business, at no growth. The whole closes on the castle of the part or parts that carry most of the owner cash. This is what
   IOSP and HNI did.
3. **By parts, any part that matters.** As reading 2, but a part that matters and whose castle is tenuous closes the whole TOO

   whole OUT. This is Q1's holding-company paragraph carried to Q2, and what MTRN did at Q1.

### What the corpus decides, and what it leaves open
**It decides** that parts with different economics are analysed separately **[L2008-005]**, **[R1996-006]**, and that a whole
worth owning can contain marginal parts **[L2021-001]**. **It does not decide** whether a weak part closes the whole, or how large
a part must be to decide it: no row found. **[M2023-031]** stays OPEN against any by-parts rule, as it already does at Q1.
Reading 1 is the least supported by **[L2008-005]**; between 2 and 3 the rows give no ground, and the size threshold is

## C. A durable castle that protects only ordinary returns

### What the runs found
OSIS: a certified oligopoly that stands, but whose largest player earns ordinary returns, a well-funded entrant wrote off a
large investment, and a state-financed rival bids in the same tenders. The run closed OUT on the moat's definition and recorded
the alternative, TOO HARD (NATURE) on the tenuous-moat row, as a choice a second analyst could make. AROC: a compression-fleet
industry that has just consolidated and raised prices; the filer states low barriers to entry, and returns through the cycle
were ordinary; closed OUT, with the note that no row says how many years of changed price behaviour would count as a change
for the better rather than a cycle. GENC raised the same question from the other side: a small maker that beats its rival over
the span and loses to it in the downturn, closed OUT.

### What the v5 rows say
> "A truly great business must have an enduring "moat" that protects excellent returns on invested capital. The dynamics of
> capitalism guarantee that competitors will repeatedly assault any business "castle" that is earning high returns."
> **[L2007-004]**, 2007

> "And in some businesses, you can have only two competitors and they’re still terrible businesses, they beat each other’s
> brains out." **[M2013-052]**, 2013

> "We have found in a long life that one competitor is frequently enough to ruin a business." **[M2012-108]**, 2012

> "if you have a business that’s earning 5 or 6 percent on equity and you hold it for a long time, you are not going to do well
> in investing. Even if you buy it cheap to start with." **[M1998-006]**, 1998

> "When we look at what’s a good business, we’re defining it in terms of the capital actually needed in the business. Whether
> it’s a good investment for us depends on how much we pay for that in the end." **[M2010-090]**, 2010

**The second-best rows.** "The second-best business is a business that also gives you more and more money. It takes more money,
but the rate at which you invest [...] the money to get that growth is a very satisfactory rate." **[M1998-081]**; "We
expect only that these businesses have reasonable expectations of earning decent returns on the incremental sums they invest."
**[L2009-012]**; "as the second-best choice, still a good choice, the answer is yes" **[M2018-055]**; "The good one pays an
attractive rate of interest that will be earned also on deposits that are added." **[L2007-010]**; of the regulated utility,
"there’s no way we get rich on our utility investments. But there’s no way we get poor, either." **[M2009-104]**.

**Why the tenuous moat goes to TOO HARD.** "it’s just too risky. We don’t know how to valuate that, and therefore we leave it
alone." **[M2000-019]**. And on change in either direction: "the railroads were a terrible business for decades and decades
and decades and then they got good" **[M2017-026]**; "you learn a lot about the durability of the economics of a business by
observing the behavior of [...] the price behavior" **[M2005-020]**; a decline is sorted by whether "we at least maintained, and in
some instances widened, our competitive superiority" **[L1995-022]**.

### The readings
1. **OUT at Q2.** A moat is what protects excellent returns **[L2007-004]**; a field where several durable players earn
   ordinary returns has a standing structure but no protected return, the mark of the commodity field **[M2013-052]**,
   **[M2012-108]**. Price does not reopen it **[M1998-006]**. What OSIS and AROC did.
2. **TOO HARD (NATURE) at Q2,** as a tenuous moat **[M2000-019]**. What OSIS recorded as the alternative.
3. **IN at Q2, weighed at Q3, decided at Q7.** The castle stands; the ordinary return is a Q3 matter, where the second-best rows
   admit decent returns **[L2009-012]**, **[M2018-055]**, and the price decides **[M2010-090]**.

### What the corpus decides, and what it leaves open
**It refuses reading 2.** **[M2000-019]** gives its own reason for the box, "We don’t know how to valuate that"; a castle whose
ordinary returns are shown on the evidence can be valued, so the reason does not apply. **Between 1 and 3 it leans to 1** for an
unregulated field: the rows that define the moat do so by the return it protects **[L2007-004]**, and the rows that say few
rivals are no cure **[M2013-052]**, **[M2012-108]** describe exactly a durable structure with ordinary returns. The second-best
rows do not reach it: each is about capital intensity, a business with a castle that must put in more money and earns a
"decent" or "very satisfactory" rate on it **[L2009-012]**, **[M1998-081]**, or about a regulated return **[M2009-104]**. **It
does not decide** where "ordinary" ends and "decent" begins: no row gives a figure (Q3 already records "A required numeric
return on capital: no instance found"). **It does not decide** AROC's second question, how many years of firmer pricing turn a
cycle into a change for the better: **[M2017-026]** and **[M2005-020]** say such change happens and how to watch for it, and
**[L1995-022]** sorts a decline, but no row gives a span. Searches for a span: "terrible business", "got good", cycle, cyclical,
"price behavior", "price increase"; none states one.

### The change proposed, if approved
Add to Q2 "What it rules OUT", after the commodity item:

> - **The standing castle that protects only ordinary returns** *(added at the operator's approval of
>   `Framework/v5/CASE 2026-10-05 - gaps found by the small-cap runs, for the operator's approval.md`, section C)*: a field whose
>   durable players earn ordinary returns over a full cycle, on the evidence of the filer's and the rivals' own filings, is OUT,
>   not TOO HARD. The moat is what "protects excellent returns" **[L2007-004]**; "you can have only two competitors and they’re
>   still terrible businesses" **[M2013-052]**; and the tenuous-moat row sends a castle to TOO HARD for a reason that does not
>   apply when the returns are known, "We don’t know how to valuate that" **[M2000-019]**. A return set by a regulator is the
>   exception, carried to Q3 as a second best **[M2009-104]**, **[L2009-012]**.

Two CONVENTION options for "ordinary", for the operator to choose or refuse:

> **CONVENTION (ours), option (i):** returns on tangible capital, averaged over the last full cycle for the field's leaders, at or
> below the Q7 floor of about ten percent pre-tax. Rationale: the floor is already the line below which a purchase is quit on
> **[M2003-149]**, and a business that cannot earn it on its own capital can clear it for a buyer only by being bought below
