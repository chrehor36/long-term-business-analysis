# PLAIN ENGLISH — what this is and how it works
**2026-08-28.** The non-technical front door. The governing document is
`THE FRAMEWORK v4.md`; where this summary and that document disagree, the document wins.

## What we built

We took 2.4 million words of Warren Buffett's and Charlie Munger's own writing and
speech — sixty years of shareholder letters, meeting transcripts, partnership letters,
talks and essays — and distilled it into a decision system for buying (and holding, and
selling) pieces of businesses. Not a summary of what people SAY Buffett does; a system
where **every rule carries an exact quote, with the year and the source file**, and a
machine check fails the whole build if even one rule cites nothing or one number appears
from nowhere.

The evidence base is a ledger of **267 rows** *(this said 261 from 2026-08-28 until 2026-09-20; count the file, never the pointer)*, each verified against its source
line by line. The framework cites 184 of them. Where something useful could not be sourced,
it is labelled a CONVENTION and confesses itself. Where the corpus contradicts itself, the
framework carries the tension openly (four open questions) instead of picking a side.

## How it works — six questions, in order, stop at the first failure

1. **Can I understand how this makes money?** If truly understanding it would take months,
   the answer is no — no amount of research fixes standing outside your circle.
2. **Is it a franchise?** Does it have a durable advantage — the ability to price without a
   prayer session, a position competitors demonstrably cannot take? This requires reading
   the *competitors'* filings too. Most businesses fail here, and that is the system
   working.
3. **Are the managers honest, and are they rational?** Read the accounting and disclosure
   conduct, not the press releases. Ten specific red flags, each a prompt to read deeper,
   never an automatic verdict. Integrity failures are permanent disqualifiers.
4. **Will it survive?** Compute what the business really earns for its owners (cash flow
   minus what it must spend to stay in place — a disclosed judgment, not a formula), check
   it can withstand the worst case, and name the specific way this business dies.
5. **What is it worth against a government bond?** The one honest yardstick. The earnings
   yield is set beside the bond of the currency the business earns in. Below roughly a 10%
   expected return, the corpus quits rather than reaches. No spreadsheet theater — the
   arithmetic is simple enough to check by hand, on purpose.
6. **What would prove me wrong, and when do I sell?** Written down BEFORE buying: the
   tripwires, the sell triggers, the bear case stated well enough that a bear would accept
   it.

Two design rules hold it together. **Arithmetic is automated; judgment is never.** Tools
fetch filings and compute; no screen output is ever a verdict — every flag is an
instruction to go read. And **most names end in "no"** — Buffett attributes his record to
about a dozen good decisions in fifty-eight years.

## What it has done so far (honest record)

- Blind-tested on five disguised real companies: zero false passes; it refused IBM with
  the same reasoning Buffett later gave for selling, and named the Kraft Heinz writedown
  mechanism thirteen months early.
- Two independent analysts running the same company reach the same verdicts.
- The one promise the corpus actually makes — avoiding permanent loss, not beating the
  market — passed its pre-registered test, thinly.
- **The market-beating claim is UNPROVEN.** Every backtest that beat the market was later
  found to contain a look-ahead bug and was withdrawn. The only clean full test
  underperformed over 32 years. The corpus itself attributes the Berkshire record to the
  man, the system, luck and devotion — not to a method anyone can run.

## What it is for

Not a stock-picking machine. A discipline for saying **no** almost always, saying **yes**
only on evidence that survives its own audit, writing down in advance what would prove the
yes wrong — and keeping the analyst honest about the difference between what is known,
what is knowable, and what is neither.
