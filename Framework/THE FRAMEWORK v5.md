# I. THE THREE BOXES

The rows give three verdicts.

> "Charlie says, you know, “We’ve got three boxes at the company: in, out, and too hard.” (Laughter) And a lot of things end up in the “too hard” pile, and it doesn’t bother us. You know, we don’t have to be able to do everything well." — **[M2006-013]**, 2006

**IN** means a question has been answered for the name, and it passes to the next; a name reaches the end of the purchase questions only if it is IN at every STOP. **OUT** means a question has been answered against it: the sections send a castle shown to be open to OUT (Q2), a price that does not clear the floor or a case too close to need no pencil (Q7), and a loss to the alternatives on the day (Q8). **TOO HARD** means the question cannot be answered from where the analyst stands: a business whose economics cannot be foreseen (Q1), a castle whose future cannot be judged (Q2), a cash stream that cannot be estimated (Q7), a financial institution whose risk cannot be known from its disclosures (Q9). Q4 closes OUT on suspicion and TOO HARD on confusion, WORK or NATURE by whether the confusing item is knowable from a filing not yet read *(named 2026-10-06, section A of the gaps case)*. The other STOPs (Q5 on integrity, Q12 on the named businesses, and the narrower STOPs inside Q3, Q6 and Q9) are stated without naming the box. Neither OUT nor TOO HARD is a pass, and TOO HARD is not a judgment of quality: "It doesn’t mean it isn’t a good buy. It doesn’t mean it isn’t selling for a fraction of its worth. It just means that we don’t know how to evaluate it." **[M2000-038]**. A WEIGHING fills no box by itself, and a failed foundation is a defect in the analyst, not an OUT for the company (section II).

A verdict for a document not yet read: no instance found in the v5 ledger, by the Q1 drafter's reading of the rows for that section, and none in the four slice theme maps (merged map, section 6, item 8). The rows place the reading inside the quick decision **[M2008-069]**, **[M2005-015]**. If a run needs such a verdict, it is a CONVENTION, to be confessed in section VI.

**The two causes of TOO HARD** *(added 2026-10-05 at the operator's approval of `Framework/v5/CASE 2026-10-05 - deeper research on the too-hard names, for the operator's approval.md`; the two paragraphs above stand as the record)*. The rows name two causes of the box and treat them differently: "Sometimes our own intellectual shortcomings would stand in the way of understanding, and in other cases the nature of the industry would be the roadblock." **[L1993-023]**; "I haven’t done the work and I’m not sure if I did the work I would understand them." **[M1994-026]**. Every TOO HARD therefore records its cause (CONVENTION, ours: the rows name both causes and give them no labels; rationale: the first cause is cured by work and the second is not, so a box that does not say which cannot be acted on):
- **TOO HARD (WORK):** the deciding question is knowable and important **[M2006-076]**, and the work has not been done. An open file, not a verdict. It gets the research pass of Part VII, written down before any reading: the list of "What do I not know that I need to know?" **[M1999-129]**; for each knowable question, the evidence that would answer it and the answer that would close the file OUT, so that the research is "to possibly reject your original hypothesis" **[M1998-144]**; the reading, primary documents first, then competitors, customers, suppliers and employees as far as they are on the public record **[M1998-144]**. The pass closes IN, OUT or TOO HARD (NATURE), never TOO HARD (WORK) a second time on the same question **[M2008-086]**. For a name the operator holds, a second session that does not see the holder's list or the holding review runs the same pass, and the two are compared **[M2002-092]**.
- **TOO HARD (NATURE):** the deciding question is a forecast the industry's own insiders would not write down **[M2000-105]**: "We couldn't solve this problem, moreover, even if we were to spend years intensely studying those industries." **[L1993-023]**; **[L1999-018]**. The closed box. No further work is done, and a lower price does not reopen it **[M2000-038]**.
The pass never widens the circle to find something to buy: "if we have trouble finding things within our circle, we will not enlarge the circle. You know, we’ll wait." **[M1995-018]**; and it never outvotes a doubt: a pass that ends unsure ends TOO HARD (NATURE) **[M2002-092]**.

# II. THE FOUNDATIONS

## THE FOUNDATIONS (the preamble, not a gate)

Before they name any test of a business, the speakers name the ground the whole activity stands on. Buffett calls three ideas, to look at stocks as businesses, to have the proper attitude toward the market, and to operate with a margin of safety, "the three basic ideas that underlie successful investing" **[M1997-135]**; he sets them out one by one from the book he learned them in **[M1996-085]** and returns to them a decade later as "three big lessons" **[M2008-001]**. To the three ideas the rows add a condition of the person: "the temperament is all important" **[M2004-035]**. None of these is a step in a purchase. They decide how the analyst reads every price, forecast, adviser and conclusion that the questions after them will meet, and no business passes or fails on them. The merged map adds four companions the rows press as generally: no macro forecast, who is paid to tell you, the index, and the analyst's habits.

### A share is a business

> "And I really think it is not a bad mindset, whenever you buy a stock to say, “**Would I be happy buying this stock if the market closed for five years?**” Because then **you’re buying a business**, if you say yes to that." — **[M1997-109]**, 1997 annual meeting

A share is bought for the cash the asset itself will produce, "Not by selling it to somebody else" **[M1997-052]**. The speakers call the other focus, on what the next fellow will pay, speculation, and decline to play it because they "bring nothing to the party" **[L2000-027]**; Buffett says he is "unable to speculate successfully" **[L2013-007]**. What decides the outcome is "the prospects of the businesses" **[M1998-099]**, so the analyst asks what will happen to the business and never when the price will move **[M1994-038]**, and stands to be right or wrong about the business, not about the quotation **[M1997-052]**.

**The test:** would I be content to own this if the market closed for years **[M1997-109]**, or if the stock were delisted and reopened long after **[M1996-067]**? A "no" means "you may not be focusing on the proper thing" **[M1997-109]**.

**Rules out:** buying because the price has risen, since appreciation "is never a reason to buy it" **[L2013-007]**, **[M2011-028]**; buying on what the next buyer will pay **[L2000-027]**; reading potential from a low price per share **[M1997-134]**.

### The market serves, it does not instruct

> "the market is there to serve you and not to instruct you. **That’s of enormous importance.** [...] The market doesn’t instruct us; the market is there to serve us. If it does something silly, we get a chance to do something because it’s doing something silly. We do it. But **it doesn’t tell us anything. It just tells us prices.** And if the price is out of line where the facts and reasoning lead you, then you — then action is called for." — **[M2006-077]**, 2006 annual meeting

The quotation carries no instruction about value **[M2006-077]**. To steer by it is to assume "that the stock market knows more than you do", and the remedy is to "formulate your ideas on price and value" and buy more when the price falls and funds allow **[M2003-046]**. The speakers do not try to pick bottoms: "Pricing is our game" **[M2009-101]**. The servant is useful only to an owner who is never forced to sell to it: "you don’t want to put yourself in a position where you have to sell" **[M2020-007]**, and an owner on borrowed money "could have been cleaned out" through falls in which nothing was wrong with the business **[M2020-022]**.

**The test:** is the price out of line with where the facts and reasoning lead **[M2006-077]**? Am I prepared to see it "go down 50 percent — or more — and be comfortable with it" **[M2020-022]**, and if a fall of 15% makes a difference to me, do I need "a somewhat different investment philosophy" **[M2025-025]**?

**Rules out:** momentum and charting, which treat the market as instructing **[M2006-077]**; dumping "a solid business with good long-term prospects" because others are frightened **[L2013-011]**.

### Margin of safety

> "if you have to actually do it on — with pencil and paper, **it’s too close to think about**. I mean, it ought to just kind of scream at you that you’ve got this **huge margin of safety**." — **[M1996-084]**, 1996 annual meeting

Buffett's picture is a truck that weighs nearly what the bridge is rated for: go down the road and find a bridge rated well above it **[M1996-085]**; the 2008 statement is "always leaving some extra room and things" **[M2008-001]**. The margin is set so wide that no calculation "to three decimal places" is needed, because to think one can pinpoint the value is "kidding yourself" **[M1995-115]**; they want the decision "obvious enough to us that it doesn’t require making a detailed calculation" **[M1995-032]**. The margin widens as understanding of the business falls and its volatility rises **[M1997-080]**. In arithmetic it is taken as "a big discount" from a present value at the risk-free rate, not as a higher rate **[M1997-126]**; that arithmetic belongs to the value question (Q7), and what the foundation carries is the attitude.

**The test:** if the decision needs pencil and paper, it is too close **[M1996-084]**.

**Rules out:** the close call that needs a spreadsheet to clear **[M1995-115]**, **[M1996-084]**.

### No macro forecast enters

> "I can’t recall a time when Charlie and I have looked at a business, either buying it in its entirety or buying pieces of it through the stock market. I just — **macro conclusions are — just never enter into the discussion.**" — **[M2000-094]**, 2000 annual meeting

The speakers hold no market opinion "because it wouldn’t be any good and it might interfere with the opinions we have that are good" **[M1994-035]**. The rule cuts both ways: no great business was passed over for external conditions, and nothing mediocre was bought because the world looked wonderful **[M2004-102]**, **[M2005-055]**. What counts instead is "the average profitability of the business over time and how strong its competitive mode is" (the transcript's word) **[M2015-016]**; Munger: "Microeconomics is what we do, and macroeconomics is what we put up with." **[M2016-053]**. Fear helps the buyer: the best purchases came "when apprehensions about some macro event were at a peak" **[L1994-004]**. Inflation enters only as a property of the business, its freedom to price without heavy added capital **[M2011-054]**, **[M2005-051]**, never as a forecast **[M2000-136]**. A currency holding is "a bet on how government now, and in the future, will behave" **[M2011-025]**.

**The test:** answer a forecast with "So what?" **[M2000-095]**; ask whether the decision would change if the next stretch of months were known, for a business held a lifetime **[M2015-016]**.

**Rules out:** sitting out a good purchase "because the world’s going to go to hell" **[M2005-055]**, **[L1994-004]**; buying the mediocre on a bright outlook **[M2004-102]**; currency-related investments unless "getting paid extremely well" for them **[M2011-025]**.

### Who is paid to tell you

> "you do not get **impartial advice** from Wall Street — (laughs) — when there’s (an) **enormous amount of fees possible from one action, and no fees applicable from another action**." — **[M2020-037]**, 2020 annual meeting

Owners as a group can earn no more than their businesses earn, and less by the "frictional" costs **[L2005-016]**; "the enemy of investment performance is activity" **[M2005-066]**. The information reaching the investor is unbalanced "because the money is in believing something different" **[M2001-052]**. Asset allocation advice is "just merchandising" sold to people who must first be convinced they have a problem **[M2004-046]**; the money manager's conflict is that asset gathering can pay more than asset managing **[M2010-054]**; the banker's projection of what he is selling always rises **[M2011-083]**; "fees too often lead to transactions" **[L2014-018]**; Munger names "fee-driven buying" **[M2021-028]**. The speakers trace most of this to "subconscious poor cognition", not malice **[M2010-072]**: "most good salespeople believe their own baloney" **[M2020-009]**. The rule is applied to Berkshire's own commentary when it has "a dog in this fight" **[L2009-015]**.

**The test:** who gains by persuading me I have a problem **[M2004-046]**? Is the speaker paid more for one answer than another **[M2020-037]**? Would the manager accept pay only for what he delivers "beyond what you can get yourself" **[M2017-060]**? "don’t ask the barber whether you need a haircut" **[M2011-083]**.

**Rules out:** fee-driven buying **[M2021-028]**, a seller's projection of what he sells **[M2011-083]**, and managers paid for gathering assets **[M2010-054]**.

### Temperament

> "**And the temperament is all important.** I mean, if you can’t control yourself, no matter what the intellect you bring to the process, you know, you’re going to have disasters. [...] It’s not a business that requires extraordinary intellect. **It does require extraordinary discipline.**" — **[M2004-035]**, 2004 annual meeting

Temperament is "the most important", but Munger adds that alone it "won’t do it": it needs "the right basic idea" and long curiosity **[M2004-115]**. A genius IQ is not needed and "can hurt"; what is needed is "an emotional stability", "an inner peace about your decisions", and the ability "to think for yourself" **[M2009-020]**. Overrating one's own ability is "a disaster" **[M2009-021]**; the ability "to detach yourself from the crowd" is "a quality you need" **[M2002-039]**; independent thinking and emotional stability are "vital to long-term investment success" **[L2006-013]**. Buffett calls sanity "the main requirement in this business" **[M2022-012]** and the game "easy" for one who can "control your emotions" **[M2015-041]**. The record is credited to doing "fewer dumb things than most people" **[M1999-109]**, and a loss is not chased where it was taken **[L1994-036]**.

**The test:** when others are fearful, do I get scared, and do I need someone else to tell me when to buy **[M2010-073]**? Does a fall in price change how I feel about what I own **[M2025-025]**?

**Rules out:** IQ or higher mathematics as the edge **[M2009-020]**, **[M2009-007]**; courage borrowed from a broker **[M2010-073]**.

### The index for the non-professional

> "under the conditions you name, I’d probably have it **all in a very low-cost index fund** [...] And because you postulated that **you’re not going to become a professional investor**, I would recognize the fact that I’m an amateur investor" — **[M2008-055]**, 2008 annual meeting

This is a fork before any question: the investor who will not be a professional should own a low-cost index and stop here **[M2008-055]**, **[M2011-039]**. Such an investor "must recognize your limitations and follow a course certain to work reasonably well" **[L2013-005]**; Buffett's own instruction for his trustee is the index plus short government bonds **[L2013-014]**. The reason is arithmetic: passive investors earn the average less low costs, active investors the average less high costs, so the passive group, the "know-nothings", must win **[L2007-018]**, **[M2016-035]**. The index is accumulated over time rather than bought all at once, with attention to cost **[M2004-059]**, **[M2002-010]**. For the one who does know what he is doing the rows turn the other way: wide diversification for the skilled is "madness" **[M2004-041]**, and two or three stocks suffice "if you know what you’re doing" **[M2019-080]**; but for the many Buffett does not "think most people are in a position to pick single stocks" **[M2020-009]**.

**The test:** will I become a professional, or stay an amateur **[M2008-055]**? Is the aim the largest result, or "peace of mind on something that cannot get a bad result" **[M2014-016]**?

**Rules out:** high-fee managers for the investor who cannot pick **[L2013-014]**, **[M2008-057]**.

### The analyst's habits

> "Darwin used to say that any time he got any evidence that flew in the face of his previous convictions, he had to **write it down in the first 30 minutes** or the mind was such that it would **reject contrary evidence to cherished beliefs**." — **[M1997-127]**, 1997 annual meeting

The rows carry the analyst's habits as rules of conduct, not as gates. Write contrary evidence down at once **[M1997-127]**; "destroy our previous ideas", since the worst anchor "is always your previous conclusion" **[M2016-054]**; earn the right to disagree by stating the other side's case better than its holder **[M2016-055]**, **[M2025-033]**; look in every business for "what’s wrong" and "what you’re missing" **[M2025-013]**. Scuttlebutt comes last and is aimed "to possibly reject your original hypothesis" **[M1998-144]**. Do one's own reading and checking: decisions are not made "based on what other people think" **[M1994-014]**, ratings are not used because "we don’t believe in outsourcing investment decisions" **[M2009-011]**, and projections are not consulted, with the projector's past projections asked for when they are offered **[M1995-050]**, **[M2003-065]**. Formulas are distrusted where the assumptions behind the symbols go unexamined **[L2008-018]** or the problem is twisted "so they fit the solution" **[M2012-083]**. Decisions are graded afterwards against what the alternatives did **[M1999-125]**, **[L1995-027]**, and other people's errors are studied as the cheaper teacher **[M2012-104]**.

**The test:** "What do I not know that I need to know?" **[M1999-129]**; what competitor do they fear most, and why **[M1998-145]**, **[M1999-130]**; "And then what?" to every economic assertion **[M1997-110]**, **[M2018-062]**; pick the extreme example and ask "what in hell is going on here" **[M1997-129]**; can I write the story of why it is worth this amount, easily **[M1999-131]**?

**Rules out:** a decision resting on projections **[M1995-050]**, ratings **[M2009-011]**, history-based models **[L2008-016]**, or the opinion poll **[M1994-014]**. A "what would prove me wrong" gate committed before buying: no instance found in the v5 ledger (a text sweep for "prove me/us wrong" and for a written sell rule found none) or in the four theme maps; the nearest rows are these habits, and the after-purchase triggers belong to Q11 **[M2009-040]**, **[M2016-081]**.

### What the foundations do not do

None of the foundations admits or rejects a business. The speakers call them the "basic ideas that underlie" the activity **[M1997-135]** and "lessons" about one's attitude toward stocks and markets **[M2008-001]**, and their verdict boxes, "in, out, and too hard" **[M2006-013]**, are filled by the questions that follow, not here. The margin of safety is an attitude here and an arithmetic only at the value question **[M1997-126]**. The index fork decides whether the questions are used at all, not how a business answers them **[M2008-055]**. A failed foundation is a defect in the analyst, to be repaired; it is not an OUT for the company.

# III. THE STANDING RULE

> "We are **never going to risk what we have and need for what we don’t have and don’t need**. We’ll still find things to do where we can make money, but we don’t have to stretch to do it. [...] But it’s our job to figure out what can really go wrong with this place" — **[M2012-081]**, 2012 annual meeting

> "One investment rule at Berkshire has not and will not change: **Never risk permanent loss of capital.**" — **[L2023-005]**, 2023 letter

> "we will **never engage in operating or investment practices that can result in sudden demands for large sums**. That means we will not expose Berkshire to short-term debt maturities of size nor enter into derivative contracts or other business arrangements that could require large collateral calls. [...] Moreover, we will not write insurance contracts that give policyholders the right to cash out at their option." — **[L2014-024]**, 2014 letter

The rule is stated in every era of the meetings, in nearly the same words: "I've never believed in risking what my family and friends have and need in order to pursue what they don't have and don't need." **[R1996-009]**; "it is insane to risk what you have and need in order to obtain what you don't need." **[L2017-005]**; "it is madness to risk losing what you need in pursuing what you simply desire." **[L2014-025]**; "always be sure you can play the next day." **[M2025-015]**. It binds the buyer, and it sits above the questions because it governs how any purchase is financed, sized and held, whatever the questions conclude about the business.

## The forms it takes in the rows

**No leverage that can call.** Borrowing is named as the one road from a good position to the zero: "It’s the one thing that forces you — it’s the one thing that ends — or can prevent you from playing out your hand." **[M2004-065]**; "it would be almost impossible to go broke without borrowed money being in the equation." **[M1999-005]**. The test is who holds the switch: "You never get in a position, obviously, where the other fellow can call your tune." **[M2006-079]**; see also **[M2009-030]**, **[M2013-081]**. Stated for every investor, not only Berkshire: "borrowed money has no place in the investor's tool kit: Anything can happen anytime in markets." **[L2014-005]**; **[L2017-008]**.

**Cash as oxygen.** "Borrowers then learn that credit is like oxygen." **[L2010-020]**; "It’s like oxygen, you know? It’s there all the time. But if it disappears for a few minutes, it’s all over." **[M2022-006]**; **[M2014-020]**. The conduct that follows: "We never want to count on the kindness of strangers in order to meet tomorrow's obligations." **[L2008-019]**; "we will always arrange our affairs so that any requirements for cash we may conceivably have will be dwarfed by our own liquidity." **[L2009-006]**. The reserve is held in the plainest form, "It is a parking place." **[M2011-088]**, and "we don't rely on bank lines" **[L2010-021]**.

**No practices that bring sudden demands for large sums.** Collateral, short maturities and cash-out features are refused by name **[L2014-024]**, because "a requirement to collateralize things means that you are putting yourself in a position where you may have to come up with some cash tomorrow morning" **[M2012-032]**; "We aren’t going to have insurance policies that can be cashed in en masse." **[M2023-024]**; **[L2008-024]**, **[M2016-019]**, **[M2020-027]**. The general form: "you always want to accept an option; you never want to give an option." **[M2013-076]**.

**One event or one counterparty cannot take us out.** The insurer's rule, "no aggregation of losses from a single event or from related events that will threaten their solvency." **[L2001-008]**, is the whole company's rule: "we’re willing to lose a lot of money in one day, but we’re not willing to do anything that causes us any discomfort, in terms of writing checks the following morning." **[M2005-027]**. Every exposure is capped, "we would never issue a policy that lacked a cap." **[L2000-005]**. Dependence on others' promises is cut: "At Berkshire, we retain our risks and depend on no one." **[L2001-018]**; "it pays to minimize links of any kind." **[L2002-017]**. And the worst case is plural: "we prepare ourselves for problems that sometimes create their own momentum." **[M2020-021]**.

**The asymmetry of ruin against gain.** "The added money has no utility whatsoever. And the money that was lost had enormous utility." **[M1999-004]**. So the odds do not decide it: "We will never play financial Russian roulette with the funds you've entrusted to us, even if the metaphorical gun has 100 chambers and only one bullet." **[L2014-025]**; "But I don’t want to have it slim. I want to have it none." **[M2008-061]**; **[M2012-080]**; "I would rather be, you know, a hundred times too cautious than 1 percent too incautious" **[M2015-027]**. The inversion is the method: "If we can't tolerate a possible consequence, remote though it may be, we steer clear of planting its seeds." **[L1996-014]**. What the rule does not forbid is loss: a risk that is paid for and cannot end the company is accepted, "if we have been paid properly for assuming the risk that caused the loss" **[L2001-013]**, and never a class of bets "has an expectancy of loss" **[M1994-045]**.

**"anything times zero is zero."** "You know, anything times zero is zero. And I don’t care how many wonderful figures are in between." **[M2007-050]**; "a long string of impressive numbers multiplied by a single zero always equals zero." **[L2005-021]**; "just need one zero in there, and the answer is zero." **[M2020-003]**; **[L2010-019]**. Time turns the remote into the expected: "what's a small probability in a short period approaches certainty in the longer run." **[L2015-009]**; **[M2004-062]**.

**Who owns it, and what it costs.** The rule is not delegated: "But we are not bearing any risks, in my view, ever, that threaten the enterprise." **[M2011-079]**; "It cannot be farmed out." **[M2008-084]**; **[M2012-001]**, **[M2021-005]**. Its price is stated and accepted: "That reduces our returns in 99 years out of 100. But we will survive in the 100th while many others fail." **[L2012-005]**; **[M2012-082]**, **[R1995-007]**.

# IV. THE QUESTIONS, IN ORDER

## Q1 — CAN I UNDERSTAND IT? Where its economics will be in five, ten or twenty years, inside the perimeter of what I know. STOP.

**The question, in the speakers' words:** "can I understand it?" **[M1995-051]**, where understanding means "a reasonable fix on about what the earning power and competitive position will look like in five or 10 years" **[M2012-065]**, asked by someone who knows "where the perimeter is" **[M2010-083]**.

> "No, the **first question is, can I understand it?** And unless it’s going to be in a business that I think I can understand, **there’s no sense looking at it.**" — **[M1995-051]**, 1995 annual meeting (AM)

> "The answer is we will never buy anything we don’t think we understand. And our **definition of understanding** is thinking that we have a reasonable probability of being able to asses **where the business will be in 10 years**." — **[M2000-037]**, 2000 annual meeting (AM); "asses" is the transcript's spelling

> "**The main thing** is you have — are you **reasonably sure that you know what you’re doing?** And if it gets past that hurdle, then we go on to figure out the math of gain versus loss" — **[M2019-077]**, 2019 annual meeting (PM)

### What understanding means

**It is foreseeing the economics, not knowing the product.** The rows define the word three times in the same way across fifteen years.

> "my idea of understanding a business is that you’ve got a **pretty good idea where it’s going to be in ten years**." — **[M1997-021]**, 1997 annual meeting (AM)

> "Oh, **we understand the product.** We understand what it does for people. **We just don’t know the economics of it 10 years from now.** [...] It’s the predictability of the economics of the situation 10 years out." — **[M2000-104]**, 2000 annual meeting (PM)

> "it isn’t that I don’t understand, you know, what a certain business does. But when I say understand, it means that I think I have a reasonably — a reasonable fix on about **what the earning power and competitive position will look like in five or 10 years.** So I’ve got some notion of **how the industry will develop and where the company will stand within the industry.**" — **[M2012-065]**, 2012 annual meeting (PM)

The converse holds: the product can stay opaque if the economics are clear. Of a petroleum-additives business, "I never would understand the chemistry of it, but I — but that’s not necessarily vital. What is important is that **I understand the economic dynamics of the industry.**" **[M2011-014]**. The reason the horizon matters is stated once: "that’s what investing is, is seeing out. You don’t get paid for what’s already happened." **[M2007-025]**. The horizon runs "five or 10 or 20 years" **[M2005-089]**, and the precision asked is low: "that didn’t mean we had to do it to four decimal places or anything of the sort, but we had to have a feel for it, and we had to know our limitations." **[M2015-086]**.

**Seeing the industry is not seeing the company.** "there’s industries we know that may have a wonderful future, but we don’t have the faintest idea who the winners will be, so we don’t think about those, either." **[M2012-067]**. The 2009 letter says why growth does not settle it: "Just because Charlie and I can clearly see dramatic growth ahead for an industry does not mean we can judge what its profit margins and returns on capital will be as a host of competitors battle for supremacy." **[L2009-005]**.

**Change is the enemy of the forecast.** "We view change as more of a threat into the investment process than an opportunity." **[M1999-063]**; "a business that must deal with fast-moving technology is not going to lend itself to reliable evaluations of its long-term economics." **[L1993-023]**. The later rows move the object from the technology to the customer: Apple is "much more of a consumer products business" and "in terms of laying out what their prospective customers will do in the future, as opposed to, say, IBM’s customers, it’s a different sort of analysis." **[M2017-019]**; "we know what we think we can project out in terms of consumer behavior and threats to a business." **[M2023-030]**. Rapid change is owned by Q2 (see Q2, Change, and what it does to a castle); Q1 points to it, and where it puts the forecast out of reach the box is TOO HARD (see What it rules OUT, below). *(settled 2026-10-04 at the reconciliation, which found rapid change carried in both Q1 and Q2.)*

**The routing, fixed.** A business whose ten-year economics cannot be foreseen because its industry changes fast closes here, at Q1, in TOO HARD: technology that could hurt the business "won’t make it through the filter" **[M1998-008]**, and that filter is the first. Q2's castle tests are reached only by a business Q1 has passed; there, a castle shown on the evidence to be filling in closes OUT, and a castle whose future cannot be judged closes TOO HARD (see Q2, Why it is a STOP). *(correction pass 2026-10-05: gap (a) of the ruling case, section 5; Test A' and the regression found the same name closing at Q1 TOO HARD, Q1 OUT or Q2 TOO HARD. The sentence above it, which gave Q2 ownership, is kept as the record and is read through this one.)*

**The "too hard" box is a verdict, not a judgment of quality.** "Charlie says, you know, “We’ve got three boxes at the company: in, out, and too hard.” (Laughter) And a lot of things end up in the “too hard” pile, and it doesn’t bother us." **[M2006-013]**. Munger: "We throw almost all decisions into the too hard pile, and we just sift for a few decisions that we can make that are easy." **[M2007-046]**. A business in the box may be cheap and good: "It doesn’t mean it isn’t a good buy. It doesn’t mean it isn’t selling for a fraction of its worth. It just means that we don’t know how to evaluate it." **[M2000-038]**.

**The perimeter.** "The biggest thing is not how big your circle of competence is, but knowing where the perimeter is." **[M2010-083]**. Knowing the edge is self-realism: "that ability to know when you’re playing the game in which you’re going to win, and playing outside of that game, is a huge asset." **[M2014-051]**.

**The five-minute rule is in the rows.** "if we can’t make a decision in five minutes, we can’t make it in five months." **[M2008-086]**; "We probably know in the first five minutes or less whether something is likely to — or has a reasonable chance of happening." **[M2017-046]**; "we know instantly" **[M1997-148]**; "we can also tell in ten seconds whether it’s something of interest." **[M2023-030]**. The quick decision is made from the documents, not instead of them: "So if you can’t make a decision on something like PetroChina off the figures, forget about going further" **[M2008-069]**, and the PetroChina report "it’s right there in black and white, in a report that anybody can get. **What cannot be judged from the outside (M20).** Financial institutions are the named case. "You spot troubles in financial institutions late. Understanding each transaction is not understanding the whole: of a finance company, "even though I could understand every individual transaction they did, I don’t regard the whole enterprise, or the operation of it, necessarily as being within my circle of competence." **[M2002-094]**. ### The tests

Each is a question the speakers put, with the row that puts it.

1. **Where will it be in ten years?** "What’s that chart going to look the next 10 years? [...] You’re trying to print the next 10 years of Value Line in your head." **[M1999-132]**; with **[M1997-021]**, **[M2000-037]**.
2. **What are the key variables, and how predictable are they?** "trying to identify the key variables in that particular business, and evaluating how predictable they were first, because that is the first step." **[M1998-044]**. 3. **Do the past statements tell me the future ones?** "do I understand enough about this business so that the financial statements will tell me the information that’s useful to me in making a judgment about what the future financial statements are going to look like." **[M2008-033]**.
4. **Is it important and knowable?** "If something’s important but unknowable, forget it." **[M2006-076]**.
5. **Would the insiders write it down?** "they would not want to put down on paper their predictions about where 10 companies you would choose in the tech field would be in 10 years, in terms of their economics. They would say, “That’s too hard.”" **[M2000-105]**.
6. **Can I name the winner, not just the industry?** **[M2012-067]**; "seeing which ones, ten years from now, might be the best ones to be in" **[M1997-119]**.
7. **Is the forecast about customers or about technology?** **[M2017-019]**, **[M2023-030]**.

9. **Do I doubt it is inside?** Then it is not. **[M2002-092]**.
10. **Do I only think I understand it?** "what would bother me is if I think I understand a business and I don’t." **[M1997-024]**; ask friends: "Charlie’s given me a few contributions occasionally, saying, “What the hell do you know about that?”" **[M2014-053]**.
11. **Abroad: do I understand the governance and the tax?** "where we think we understand the nuances of the corporate governance system and taxation and that sort of thing." **[M2000-141]**.
12. **For a lender or a derivatives book (M20):** when both sides of a zero-sum trade "put on the books a profit that day" **[M2004-026]**; interest "accrued but was not paid" taken "as if it were paid" **[M2006-025]**; whether the payment fits "their actual � not hoped-for � income" (the replacement characters are the transcription's) **[L2008-011]**; whether funds come "from a natural customer base" or "on a wholesale basis, and that money can run pretty fast" **[M2012-012]**; and "what it achieves after operating for an extended period in a no-growth mode" **[L2004-009]**.

## Q2 — WHY IS THE CASTLE STILL STANDING? And what keeps it standing ten or twenty years? STOP.

The question, in the words of the row that asks it most plainly: "why is that castle still standing? And what’s going to keep it standing or cause it not to be standing five, 10, 20 years from now." **[M1995-038]**

> "But we are trying to figure out what is keeping — **why is that castle still standing?** And what’s going to keep it standing or cause it not to be standing **five, 10, 20 years from now.** What are the key factors? And **how permanent are they?** How much do they depend on the genius of the lord in the castle?" — **[M1995-038]**, 1995 annual meeting, AM

> "A truly great business must have an **enduring "moat"** that protects excellent returns on invested capital. The dynamics of capitalism **guarantee that competitors will repeatedly assault** any business "castle" that is earning high returns." — **[L2007-004]**, 2007 letter

> "when we bought See’s Candy in 1972, I said to myself, **if I had a hundred million dollars and I wanted to go in and take on See’s Candy, could I do it?** And I came to the conclusion, no, so we bought See’s Candy. **If the answer had been yes, we wouldn’t have done it.**" — **[M2011-015]**, 2011 annual meeting, AM

The question starts from the attacker, not from the business. Every castle is assumed to be under attack; what the analyst must find is why this one has not fallen, and whether the reason will hold. "all moats are subject to attack in a capitalistic system" and "most moats aren’t worth a damn" **[M1995-038]**; "there are going to be marauders. And they’ll never go away." **[M2017-012]**

---

### What a moat is, in the rows

**The definition.** A moat is whatever protects high returns on capital from competitors for a long time. The plainest definition is the 2007 letter's, quoted above **[L2007-004]**; the earliest names its parts:

> "The might of their **brand names**, the attributes of their **products**, and the strength of their **distribution systems** give them an enormous competitive advantage, setting up a **protective moat around their economic castles**. The average company, in contrast, does battle daily without any such means of protection." — **[L1993-021]**, 1993 letter

The sources are open-ended. Buffett lists "the low-cost producer in some area", "a natural franchise because of surface capabilities", "its position in the consumers’ mind", "a technological advantage, or any kind of reason at all" **[M1995-037]**. Munger puts them in economists' terms: "the microeconomic business advantages are, by and large, advantages of scale" **[M1995-039]**. Small size can be one too: "very small markets that aren’t really too attractive to anybody with any sense to enter, and fanaticism in service" **[M2011-017]**; "they’re of a size and have specialized skills that other organizations just can’t get into it" **[M1997-071]**. A culture can be one: "we have a culture and a business model, which people are going to find very, very difficult to copy, even semi-copy" **[M2009-038]**.

**What it protects is a return over time, not a size.** "a great business, which means that business is going to earn a high return on capital employed for a very long period of time" **[M2007-023]**; "by definition, a great company is one that’s going to remain great for 30 years. If it’s going to be a great company for three years, you know, it ain’t a great company." **[M1996-054]**; "businesses that are resistant to effective competition" **[M1996-042]**.

**It is judged, not measured.** "there’s no formula that gives you that precisely, you know, that says that the moat is 28 feet wide and 16 feet deep [...] You have to understand the businesses." **[M1999-108]**; "when we see a great business, it’s like if you see some — somebody walk in the door, you don’t know whether they weigh 300 pounds or 325 pounds. You still know they’re fat" **[M2007-024]**. Berkshire buys moats; it does not dig them: "We sort of buy barriers; we don’t build them." **[M2012-105]**

**What the speakers call the primary criterion.** This is the only element of the purchase that the rows call by these names:

> "we think in terms of that moat and the ability to keep its width and its impossibility of being crossed as **the primary criterion of a great business**." — **[M2000-018]**, 2000 annual meeting, AM

> "if you are evaluating a business year-to-year, you want to — **the number one question** you want to ask yourself is whether the — could the competitive advantage have been made **stronger and more durable** before — and that’s **more important than the P&L** for a given year." — **[M2000-075]**, 2000 annual meeting, AM

The same is said in other years: "But the most important thing you can" **[M1995-037]** opens the 1995 castle passage; "that is the key to investing" **[M2000-076]**; a formidable barrier "is essential for sustained success" **[L2007-004]**; "durable competitive strengths" stand first among the five qualities of the 2017 letter **[L2017-002]**; "We are looking for things when we do get into the durability of the competitive advantage" **[M2018-084]**.

**Its place after Q1.** The structure decision left open whether the lists that name the advantage first change the order of Q1 and Q2. One row answers it in so many words: "The first filter we probably put it through is whether we think — and we know instantly — whether it’s a business we’re going to understand, and whether it’s a business that — if it passes through that, it’s whether a company can have a sustainable edge, you know. [...] And if we can’t understand it, obviously, it’s not going to have — we can’t make a decision as to whether it has a sustainable edge." **[M1997-148]**. M2017-008 joins the two: "we can look out five, or 10, or 20 years and decide that the competitive advantage that it had at the present would last over that period" **[M2017-008]**. My reading, not a speaker's: the lists that open with the advantage (**[M2008-039]** among them) take understanding for granted rather than reverse the order, because the advantage cannot be judged without it **[M1997-148]**. The order stays Q1 then Q2.

### The tests

The speakers state the tests below in their own words. None is a score. Each is a way of asking the 1995 question **[M1995-038]** from a different side. Where a test was asked of a business that failed it, the failing answer is quoted.

#### 1. The castle questions: what keeps it standing, and how permanent is it?

> "What are the **key factors**? And **how permanent** are they? How much do they **depend on the genius of the lord** in the castle?" — **[M1995-038]**, 1995 annual meeting, AM

> "I was trying to figure out **why people would insure with GEICO rather than with the companies that they were already insuring with**, and **how permanent that advantage was**." — **[M1998-147]**, 1998 annual meeting, PM

The answer is a reason the customer comes, and a reason that reason will last. For See's: "the reasons why people today buy boxed chocolates, and why they buy them from us rather than from someone else, are virtually unchanged from what they were in the 1920s [...] Moreover, these motivations are not likely to change over the next 20 years, or even 50." **[L1996-028]**. The 2017 form asks about the company, the product and the management together: "you make a judgment as to the ability of your particular company and product and management to ward off competitors." **[M2017-012]**

*A failing answer:* the business leads now but has no reason that lasts. "Leadership alone provides no certainties [...] for every Inevitable, there are dozens of Impostors, companies now riding high but vulnerable to competitive attacks." **[L1996-031]**; "Business history is filled with "Roman Candles," companies whose moats proved illusory and were soon crossed." **[L2007-004]**

#### 2. Would it stand without the lord?

> "if a business **requires a superstar** to produce great results, **the business itself cannot be deemed great**. [...] The partnership's moat will go when the surgeon goes. You can count, though, on the moat of the Mayo Clinic to endure, even though you can't name its CEO." — **[L2007-006]**, 2007 letter

> "The really great business is one that **doesn’t require good management**. [...] And the poor business is one that can only succeed, or even survive, with great management." — **[M1996-037]**, 1996 annual meeting, PM

The test is the third castle question **[M1995-038]** asked on its own. It recurs in every era: "If you have a big enough moat, you don’t need as much management." **[M1999-106]**; "you want to buy stock in a business that’s so good that an idiot can run it because sooner or later one will." **[M2008-045]**; "if it can’t stand a little mismanagement, it’s no business." **[M2018-059]**; "A great company keeps working after you are not; a mediocre company won't do that." **[L2022-015]**; "We try to find something that a guy with Alzheimer’s can run, actually." **[M2022-001]**. The 1995 form separates "businesses where you have to have been smart once and businesses where you have to stay smart" **[M1995-040]**.

*A failing answer:* "In retailing, to coast is to fail." **[L1995-008]**; for a retailer, hiring the backward nephew "would be an express ticket to bankruptcy" **[L1995-009]**.

#### 3. The money test: could a well-funded attacker take it?

> "if you gave me a hundred billion dollars [...] and you told me to **displace the CocaCola Company** as the leader in the world in soft drinks, you know, **I wouldn’t have the faintest idea of how to do it**. And those are the kind of businesses we like." — **[M1997-103]**, 1997 annual meeting, PM

The test is stated in 1997, 2011 and 2012 with sums from a hundred million against See's **[M2011-015]** to "10, 20, $30 billion" against Coca-Cola **[M2012-106]**. Its kin: "why are there no new entrants into the field? [...] Normally, if you’ve got a profitable business, you know, a dozen people want to go into it." **[M2000-077]**; "it isn’t like the world — the capitalist world — is unaware of the money that could be made if they could knock off Gillette. But they can’t knock off Gillette" **[M2002-051]**; "you can’t crack our custom picture frame business" **[M2002-032]**; "it’s an asset that couldn’t be duplicated for, you name it, three, four, five, six times, you know, what it’s selling for." **[M2012-030]**

*A failing answer:* "CHARLIE MUNGER: You really couldn’t create another railroad and — [...] — and you can create another airline. WARREN BUFFETT: Very easily, and you have people that like to do it. CHARLIE MUNGER: That’s what we don’t like about it." **[M2013-054]**. "there are some industries that are just never going to have barriers to entry. And in those industries, you better be running very fast" **[M2012-106]**. And one attacker can be enough: "We have found in a long life that one competitor is frequently enough to ruin a business." **[M2012-108]**

#### 4. Pricing power, and the agony before a price rise

> "it’s not a great business when you have to have **a prayer session before you raise your prices a penny**. [...] you can almost **measure the strength of a business over time by the agony they go through** in determining whether a price increase can be sustained." — **[M2005-020]**, 2005 annual meeting, AM

> "one of the questions we asked ourselves [...] was, you know, **if we raised the price 10 cents a pound, would sales fall off a cliff?**" — **[M2005-019]**, 2005 annual meeting, AM

The positive form: "Anytime you can charge more for a product and maintain or increase market share against wellentrenched, well-known competitors, you have something very special in people’s minds." **[M2000-031]**. The limit case is the business school, "because the more they increase the price, the more people want to get in" **[M2000-085]**. Munger names what such power implies: "If you name some business that has incredible pricing power, you’re talking about a business that’s a monopoly or a near monopoly." **[M2010-092]**. Later cases: a card whose price went "from 450 to $550 during a competitive battle, and retention improved" **[M2019-074]**; Buffett on a large customer, "there was no way in the world that he could get rid of American Express, or even get them to cut their fees. That was my kind of business." **[M2013-043]**. A strong position passes cost increases through: "over time the businesses with strong competitive positions manage to pass through increases in raw material costs" **[M2005-017]**. The See's letter names the power as the asset the balance sheet did not show: "a broad and durable competitive advantage that gave it significant pricing power" **[L2014-010]**.

*A failing answer:* the prayer session itself **[M2005-020]**; and the newspaper whose "ability to price evaporates" as the advertiser stops needing it **[M2010-053]**. The test also cuts the other way: price past the moat and the moat is shown to be smaller than thought. "they maybe just pushed their pricing too far to the point that they lost market share without getting — without having — the moat that they thought they had" **[M2001-087]**; "if you establish too wide a differential between Coke and a private label product, you will change consumption patterns somewhat." **[M2001-088]**

#### 5. Unit volume and share of mind

> "what do people think about Coca-Cola now, compared to 10 years ago or 20 years ago? What are they going to think about it 10 years from now? [...] **We measure it by unit cases sold** and by shares outstanding. And **we want a lot more unit cases sold**." — **[M1999-054]**, 1999 annual meeting, AM

> "It’s **share of mind**. It’s not share of market. It’s share of mind that counts. [...] You can’t do it by a billion dollar advertising budget" — **[M1997-099]**, 1997 annual meeting, PM

Volume is the evidence that the place in the mind holds, even when the year's profit does not: "They’re selling more units than any year in history. But certain other factors hurt their business" **[M2000-013]**; "if share of mind is there, market will follow." **[M2000-030]**. The question looks ten years out: "10 years from now is that number even larger, and is the impression just a slight bit more favorable" **[M1999-107]**; "what place in the mind of billions of children around the world, and their parents, does Disney itself have" **[M1996-063]**; "what is likely to be in their mind five or ten or 20 years from now?" **[M1997-098]**

*A failing answer:* "Fixed costs are high in the newspaper business, and that's bad news when unit volume heads south." **[L2006-009]**. Volume decline is not by itself a fail: the battery "will have unit declines over a period of time, but I think we’ll do fine with the Duracell investment." **[M2015-066]**

#### 6. The low-cost position

> "The company's competitive strength flows directly from its position as **a low-cost operator**.  Low costs permit low prices, and low prices attract and retain good policyholders." — **[L1996-015]**, 1996 letter

> "Being the low-cost producer, for example, is **a terribly important moat**." — **[M2018-043]**, 2018 annual meeting, AM

The low-cost position is the second of the two examples in the 2007 definition, "the low-cost producer (GEICO, Costco)" **[L2007-004]**, and the speakers say why it holds where other advantages are copied: "Others may copy our model, but they will be unable to replicate our economics." **[L1999-006]**; the agency insurers had "a form of distribution so ingrained in the business of these insurers that it was impossible for them to give it up" **[L1995-011]**. It is strongest in something people must buy: "being a low-cost producer of something that’s essential to people, it’s going to be a very good business usually." **[M2004-091]**; "if you can offer somebody a good product cheaper than the other guy then everybody practically has to buy it." **[M2024-013]**. The advantage is kept by passing it on, not by banking it: "Our goal, however, is not to widen our profit margin but rather to enlarge the price advantage we offer customers." **[L1996-016]**; Costco's "extreme ethical duty, self-imposed, to take all its cost advantages as fast as it could accumulate them and pass them onto the customers" **[M2011-063]**.

*A failing answer:* "it was able to operate with 12 cents a seat mile of cost because Southwest hadn’t gotten there yet with their 8 cent costs. But they get there. And you do not want to have something whose competitive position is going to erode over time." **[M2007-117]**; "we do not want to buy into a business that has a very high labor content and that has a product that can be shipped in from abroad very easily." **[M2007-116]**

#### 7. The brand in the customer's mind

> "they say that **a brand is a promise**. I mean, there’s a promise involved in picking up a Milky Way, or picking up a CocaCola, as to what it’s going to deliver to you." — **[M2008-075]**, 2008 annual meeting, PM

> "the retailer is going to use all the pressure they’ve got and, therefore, **the brand has to stand for something in the consumer’s mind**. [...] If you protect a brand — if you got a terrific brand and you protect it, it’s a fabulous asset." — **[M2015-038]**, 2015 annual meeting, AM

The test is whether the customer asks for it by name: "you’re probably going to get better gross of margins if they ask for you by name." **[M2023-073]**; the bottler is valuable because the product is one "which hundreds of millions of people every day are going to go in and ask for by name" **[M2002-091]**; ""Buy commodities, sell brands" has long been a formula for business success." **[L2011-008]**. Two further questions sit inside it. Does the brand hold against the retailer? "to the extent that people trust Costco or Walmart more than they — or as much as — they trust the brand, then the value of having the brand moves over to the retailer from the product itself." **[M2001-090]**; "the brand is our protection against the intermediaries making all the money." **[M2019-041]**. Does it travel? "Gillette travels. Disney travels. McDonalds travels. Coke travels. You know, See’s Candy doesn’t travel as well." **[M1997-068]**; "their products "traveled."" **[L2023-006]**

*A failing answer:* "if you’re just another cola, and there have been hundreds of them, and even if you have distribution through something like Walmart, who has Sam’s Cola, it just doesn’t, it’s not the same." **[M2023-073]**

#### 8. Would the customer still choose it over the low bid?

> "the question when we looked at See’s Candy in 1972 was, **would people still want to be both eating and giving away that candy in preference to other candies?** And it wouldn’t be a question of people buying candy for **the low bid**." — **[M2017-009]**, 2017 annual meeting, AM

> "who is going to, you know, **buy some substitute cola for a penny a can less**, or two cents a can less, than Coca-Cola" — **[M2008-076]**, 2008 annual meeting, PM

The test runs from the 1990s to the 2020s: "we can charge a little more for it because people are not interested in taking the low bid." **[M1997-102]**; "I don’t think if you were buying a parachute you’d want to take the — necessarily take the low bid" **[M2001-014]**; Precision Castparts, "where people don’t simply just take the low bid in order to get this gadget" **[M2016-006]**, whose customers "would be totally crazy to hire some other supplier" **[M2016-009]**. Its strongest forms ask what the customer would give up first: "if they had to give up a second car or give up their iPhone, they’d give up their second car." **[M2023-034]**, and again **[M2024-053]**; or whether the customer stayed through a scandal: "nobody quit using the card. Nobody quit using the traveler’s checks. And they charged a premium price" **[M2018-073]**; "if you have a business where your customers are mad at you and you’re growing, you know, that has met a certain test, in my mind, of utility." **[M2000-032]**

*A failing answer:* "most insureds don't care from whom they buy." **[L2004-003]**; a product "that has a whole bunch of competitors" sells volume without the money **[M2023-074]**.

#### 9. Ask the competitors

> "If you had a silver bullet and you could only put it through the head of one of your competitors, **which one would it be and why?**" — **[M1999-130]**, 1999 annual meeting, PM

> "if they were to be stuck on a desert island and they had to own only one of their competitors’ stock during the 10 years they were going to be on that island, **which one would it be, and why?** And then after they give you that answer, you ask the same thing if they were to short one of their competitors’ stock" — **[M2025-043]**, 2025 annual meeting, PM

The same questions are asked in 2017 **[M2017-022]** **[M2017-091]**. Munger's form, put to a Berkshire manager, is the shortest: "would you trade our operation for theirs?" **[M2022-021]**. The value of the test is that the answers, gathered across an industry, give "a better fix on the economics of the coal industry than any one of those individuals has." **[M2017-091]**

*A failing answer:* the business at which every rival would aim. "both in the cloud and in retail, there are a lot of people that would aim that silver bullet at Jeff." **[M2017-022]** (the rows do not say the target fails; they say where the threat is).

#### 10. Is the moat widening or narrowing?

> "what really tells you something is if you know how to figure out **how wide the moat is and whether it’s likely to widen further or shrink on you**." — **[M1999-108]**, 1999 annual meeting, PM

> "Every day, in countless ways, the competitive position of each of our businesses **grows either weaker or stronger**. [...] When our long-term competitive position improves as a result of these almost unnoticeable actions, we describe the phenomenon as "**widening the moat**."" — **[L2005-010]**, 2005 letter

The instruction to managers is the same from 2000 to 2018: "to our managers, we say we want the moat widened every year." **[M2000-018]**; "we do tell them to try and not only protect, but enlarge, the moat. And if you enlarge the moat, everything else follows." **[M2001-071]**; "We call it “widening the moat.”" **[M2016-030]**; "certainly should be working at improving your own moat and defending your own moat all of the time." **[M2018-042]**. It outranks the year's earnings: "when short-term and long-term conflict, widening the moat must take precedence." **[L2005-011]**; see also **[M2000-075]** above. A bad year is sorted by this test: "we at least maintained, and in some instances widened, our competitive superiority. [...] you should view last year's figures as reflecting a cyclical problem, not a secular one." **[L1995-022]**

*A failing answer:* the notch. "newspapers had lost a notch in their economic attractiveness [...] Today, the industry retains its excellent economics, but has lost still another notch." **[L1995-023]**; the credit card "is not what it was 20 years ago, relative to the competition." **[M1996-036]**; "we do not think the franchise in 2002 is the same as it was in 1970." **[M2002-005]**

#### 11. What could destroy, modify or reduce it, five to fifteen years out?

> "we think of business risk in terms of what can happen — say five, 10, 15 years from now — that will **destroy, or modify, or reduce the economic strengths** that we perceive currently exist in a business." — **[M2000-014]**, 2000 annual meeting, AM

> "if cable and satellite broadcasting, as well as the internet, had come along first, **newspapers as we know them probably would never have existed**." — **[L2006-008]**, 2006 letter

The second row turns the first into a test the analyst can run: would the business be started today against its substitutes? The 2007 form imagines Gutenberg's successor pitching the paper newspaper now: "Well, I don’t think we would be backing him, you know." **[M2007-133]**. The same question about strength rather than threat: "if you’re really thinking of, you know, what bad can happen to you, I think that you would regard Moody’s as a considerably stronger franchise" **[M2000-083]**.

*A failing answer:* "If the technology had not changed, they’d still be impregnable franchises. But the technology did change." **[M2006-065]**

### The commodity business and its one exception

**What a commodity business is.** The rows define it by the customer's indifference:

> "Insurers have generally earned poor returns for a simple reason: **They sell a commodity-like product.** Policy forms are standard, and the product is available from many suppliers [...] Moreover, **most insureds don't care from whom they buy.** Customers by the millions say "I need some Gillette blades" or "I'll have a Coke" but we wait in vain for "I'd like a National Indemnity policy, please."" — **[L2004-003]**, 2004 letter

Its marks, in the rows: average is terrible and does not leave ("Average is going be terrible in insurance over time. [...] average is not going to go away, either." **[M2000-072]**); every improvement goes to the customer ("the improvement you get one day, your competitor gets the next day. And it very much tends to work to the benefit of consumers but not to increase overall profitability." **[M2004-053]**; the textile mill's new machines, "everybody in the crowd is up on tiptoes and they’re not seeing any better" **[M2004-054]**); any strategy is copied ("anything you do, your competitors can copy" **[M1996-017]**; "the industry factors will, in my view, just overwhelm any specific strategy" **[M2000-069]**); and the rival sets your price ("whatever he charged for gas was my price." **[M2012-109]**; "he determined our profit, because we looked at his price every day." **[M2023-079]**). Fewness of rivals does not cure it: "you can have only two competitors and they’re still terrible businesses, they beat each other’s brains out." **[M2013-052]**, and again **[M2018-088]**. Munger's summary of the policy: "We’re going to be investors in businesses, not commodities, by and large." **[M2007-131]**

**The exception: the low-cost operator.**

> "**Another way to prosper in a commodity-type business is to be the low-cost operator.** Among auto insurers operating on a broad scale, GEICO holds that cherished title." — **[L2004-007]**, 2004 letter

> "when a company is selling a product with commodity-like economic characteristics, **being the low-cost producer is all-important**." — **[L2000-017]**, 2000 letter

> "commodity businesses have risk **unless you’re the low-cost producer**, because the low-cost producer can put you out of business. Our textile business was not the low-cost producer." — **[M1997-010]**, 1997 annual meeting, AM

The cost is measured against the competitor, not in absolute terms: "if your costs are on parity or less — labor costs — than your other major competitors, that is much more important to you than the absolute level." **[M2001-013]**; "It’s like comparing a copper producer whose costs are $2.50 a pound with a copper producer whose costs are $1 a pound. Those are two different kinds of businesses." **[M2009-059]**. The low-cost operator welcomes the hard market: "a tough market helps the low-cost operator, which is what we are and intend to remain." **[L1997-021]**; "We want the most efficient to be the ones that do well and the less-efficient ones to have plenty of problems." **[M2000-127]**. And it does not coast: "a company holding a low-cost advantage must pursue an unrelenting foot-to-the-floor strategy." **[L2004-007]**

*The high-cost operator's end:* "In an unregulated commodity business, a company must lower its costs to competitive levels or face extinction." **[L1994-035]**; "sooner or later, the nature of a capitalist society is that the guy with the lower cost comes in and kills you." **[M2001-013]**

Two notes on "one exception". First, L2004-007 opens with "Another way", and the same row names "an ebb-and-flow business model" for NICO; the first way is not quoted in that row, so the rows carry at least one other route through a commodity field, not the low-cost position alone. Second, service is named as an escape from the commodity class: "fractional ownership is not a commodity business. I mean, the people care enormously about service and the assurance of safety." **[M2001-014]**; "The sustainable competitive advantage at GEICO is to be the low-cost producer providing very good service." **[M2000-146]**

### Change, and what it does to a castle

**Change works against us.** The rows treat change as the threat, not the opportunity:

> "We view **change as more of a threat** into the investment process than an opportunity. [...] we’re looking for **the absence of change** to protect ways that are already making a lot of money [...] And whenever we look at a business and we see lots of change coming, **9 times out of 10, we’re going to pass on that**." — **[M1999-063]**, 1999 annual meeting, PM

"Most of our decisions relate to things where we expect the future not to change much." **[M2000-034]**. "technology is sometimes your friend and it’s sometimes your bitter enemy." **[M1995-061]**. Even the safest-looking position can go: "they moved the streetcars." **[M1999-016]**

**Industries prone to rapid change are ruled out.**

> "Our criterion of "enduring" causes us to **rule out companies in industries prone to rapid and continuous change**. Though capitalism's "creative destruction" is highly beneficial for society, it precludes investment certainty." — **[L2007-005]**, 2007 letter

The reason is that the moat cannot be judged there: "there are many businesses — industries where it’s very hard to evaluate moats. There — those are the businesses of rapid change." **[M2001-069]**; "We think Microsoft is a sensational company run by the best of managers. But we don’t have any idea what that world is going to look like in 10 or 20 years." **[M1996-059]**. And what is gained fast is lost fast: "usually if something can gain competitive advantage very quickly, you have to worry about them losing it quickly, too." **[M2002-050]**; See's could not have been built "in times measured shorter than decades" **[M2002-048]**.

**Slow change is still change.** "they’re all subject to change, and of course, slow change can be much harder to perceive, and can lull you to sleep easier" **[M2014-038]**. Munger adds that it can come slower than forecast: "they’ve come, but it was 30 years later." **[M2014-049]**. And the speakers record change for the better, which the analyst can also miss: "the railroads were a terrible business for decades and decades and decades and then they got good." **[M2017-026]**; "We were slow to recognize the change, but better late than never." **[L2022-020]**

A change for the better is credited only on the evidence of a full cycle in which the price behaviour held **[M2005-020]**, **[L1995-022]**; until then the file is TOO HARD (WORK) on that question, not IN *(CONVENTION, ours, adopted 2026-10-06 at the operator's approval of `Framework/v5/CASE 2026-10-05 - gaps found by the small-cap runs, for the operator's approval.md`, section C; the rows say the change happens **[M2017-026]** and give no span)*.

**The maintained lead against the rebuilt one.**

> "**A moat that must be continuously rebuilt will eventually be no moat at all.**" — **[L2007-005]**, 2007 letter

The rows set this beside the daily widening of tests 6 and 10 **[L2005-010]** **[L2004-007]**. The distinction the rows draw is between work that adds to a standing advantage ("If we are delighting customers, eliminating unnecessary costs and improving our products and services, we gain strength." **[L2005-010]**) and work that only replaces what competitors take each year ("you better be running very fast because there are a lot of other people that are going to be running and looking at what you’re doing" **[M2012-106]**). GEICO's channel changed from mail to phone to internet while the advantage stayed the same: "the key is to be able to save people money and give them good service. And whatever way does that in the most effective way is going to be what wins" **[M2015-036]**. Whether a given business is maintaining or rebuilding is a judgment; no row gives a test that separates them beyond these.

**The moats themselves may be filling in.** Munger in 2001: "the old moats, some of them are getting filled in. And the new moats are harder to predict than some of the old moats." **[M2001-070]**; in 2009: "a lot of moats have been filling up with sand lately" **[M2009-106]**; Buffett in 2018: "There’s been more moats that have been - become susceptible to invasion - than seemed to be the case, earlier." **[M2018-042]**; and the base rates: "most big businesses — eventually fall into mediocrity or worse." **[M2008-031]**; "how few of them have really gotten big and stayed big." **[M2014-071]**

### Why it is a STOP

The rows state the consequence of a failing answer as a refusal, not a discount:

> "when we see a **moat that’s tenuous in any way** — getting back to your question — **it’s just too risky. We don’t know how to valuate that, and therefore we leave it alone.**" — **[M2000-019]**, 2000 annual meeting, AM

> "**If the answer had been yes, we wouldn’t have done it.**" — **[M2011-015]**, 2011 annual meeting, AM

> "Our criterion of "enduring" causes us to **rule out** companies in industries prone to rapid and continuous change." — **[L2007-005]**, 2007 letter

## Q3 — HOW MUCH CAPITAL MUST GO IN to get the earnings out, and what does the added capital earn? WEIGHING.

In the speakers' words: "does it look like it has good economics? Has it earned high returns on capital?" **[M1995-051]**, and, of any growth, "whether that’s good or bad depends on what we earn on that incremental $130 million over time" **[M2001-019]**.

> "No, the first question is, can I understand it? [...] And then the second question is, you know, does it look like it has **good economics**? **Has it earned high returns on capital?**" — **[M1995-051]**, 1995 annual meeting

> "**The best business is one that gives you more and more money every year without putting up anything to get it, or very little.** [...] The second-best business is a business that also gives you more and more money. It takes more money, but the rate at which you invest — reinvest — the money to get that growth is a **very satisfactory rate**. **The worst business of all** is the one that grows a lot, and where you’re forced [...] forced to grow to stay in the game at all, and where you’re reinvesting the capital at a **very low rate of return**." — **[M1998-081]**, 1998 annual meeting

> "In earlier days, Charlie and I shunned capital-intensive businesses such as public utilities. Indeed, the best businesses by far for owners continue to be those that have **high returns on capital** and that **require little incremental investment to grow**. [...] we are today quite willing to enter businesses that regularly require large capital expenditures. We expect only that these businesses have reasonable expectations of earning **decent returns on the incremental sums they invest**." — **[L2009-012]**, 2009 letter

The question stands second in the 1995 ordering, after understanding **[M1995-051]**, and third in the 2017 list of "key qualities", as "good returns on the net tangible assets required to operate the business; opportunities for internal growth at attractive returns" **[L2017-002]**. Munger reports it as Buffett's lifelong test of good against bad: "the good business just throws up one easy decision after another" **[M1998-131]**.

### The tests

**1. What has it earned on the capital it needs, over time?** "we always look at what a business does in terms of what it earns on capital" **[M1998-005]**, and "the best businesses, by definition, are going to be businesses that earn very high returns on capital employed over time" **[M1995-047]**. The capital is the capital the business needs, not what a buyer paid: "we’re defining it in terms of the capital actually needed in the business. Whether it’s a good investment for us depends on how much we pay for that in the end" **[M2010-090]**. The measure is "return on tangible assets" **[M2011-060]**, the way "to measure profitability overall" **[M2014-007]**; book value is ignored, while "We do look at what a company is able to earn on invested assets and what it can earn on incremental invested assets" **[M2000-065]**. Return on sales is not the measure: a thin margin "only works in terms of return on capital if you turn your equity extraordinarily fast" **[M2017-096]**.

A high reported return is read with care before it is credited. Rule out "a cyclical peak in earnings, a monopolistic position, or leverage" **[L1994-009]**. Return on equity can be manufactured: "if you keep the equity low enough by buying shares back, why, you could make return on equity whatever you want" **[M1998-017]**; "we could make it almost any number you want if we just used enough leverage" **[M2001-054]**; repeated special charges flatter it, and AT&T's return computed with them in: "it’s not been good at all" **[M1999-033]**. Published averages "overstate what’s earned anyway" because the low-return companies drop out **[M1995-087]**. And unprecedented returns are not assumed to last: "we would not want to buy things on the basis that these returns would be sustained" **[M1998-016]**. The reason the test governs is time: "if you have a business that’s earning 5 or 6 percent on equity and you hold it for a long time, you are not going to do well in investing. Even if you buy it cheap to start with. Time is the enemy of the poor business, and it’s the friend of the great business" **[M1998-006]**; also **[M1998-120]**.

**2. How much must be reinvested to stand still, and how much to grow?** "Every year we spend amounts equal to our depreciation charge simply to stay in the same economic place" **[L2000-035]**. The speakers name the two kinds of need: "expenditures that a company must make to maintain its competitive position" and "optional outlays, aimed at business growth, that management expects will produce more than a dollar of value for each dollar spent" **[L1999-024]**; the owner is entitled to the manager's best guess of the first and to "know how much we are spending, beyond that maintenance cost, to build the business for the future" **[M2000-144]**. The tests inside it: look at "the amount of capital required to produce incremental revenues" **[M2000-088]**; does the profit end the year as cash, or "there’s your profit sitting in the yard" **[M2008-036]**; does the business report "the 12 percent on capital but there’s never any cash" **[M2003-122]**. Where the spending is compulsory, the business is a trap: "you have to spend money like crazy if it’s attractive to spend money, and you have to spend it the same way if it’s unattractive" **[M1998-128]**, and Munger: "we have tried to avoid places where there was a lot of compulsory reinvestment just in order to stand still" **[M1997-016]**. Inflation is the same test sharpened: the best business "retains its earning power in real dollars without commensurate investment" **[M2005-049]**; of the utilities, "To earn more, their owners must invest more" **[L2011-019]**; also **[M2004-008]**, **[M2015-049]**, **[M2022-050]**.

**3. The growth arithmetic: where growth adds and where it destroys.** "There’s a huge difference in the business that grows and requires a lot of capital to do so, and the business that grows, and doesn’t require capital" **[M1994-081]**; a business that does not grow at all can be the better investment **[M1994-080]**, **[M1994-081]**. "growth has been a curse in the airline business because more and more capital has been put into the business at inadequate returns. Now, growth is wonderful at See’s Candy, because it requires relatively little incremental investment to sell more pounds of candy" **[M2001-019]**. The 2007 letter gives the scale: "nothing shabby about earning $82 million pre-tax on $400 million of net tangible assets. But that equation for the owner is vastly different from the See's situation" **[L2007-008]**, and the gruesome case, where investors were "attracted by growth when they should have been repelled by it" **[L2007-009]**. The three are summed as savings accounts: the great one's rate rises, the good one pays "an attractive rate of interest that will be earned also on deposits that are added", and "the gruesome account both pays an inadequate interest rate and requires you to keep adding money at those disappointing returns" **[L2007-010]**. Price and reinvestment are read together: "the current multiple interacts with the reinvestment of capital and the rate at which that capital’s invested" **[M1995-113]**; also **[M1999-039]**.

For retained earnings the same test applies per share: "we do expect that normalized earning power to increase over time. And if it doesn’t, you know, one way or another, we’re failing you because we’re retaining those earnings" **[M2018-045]**; also **[M2016-059]**, **[M2018-046]**, **[M2023-003]**. Rising earnings alone prove nothing: "we’re not earning a higher rate of return on capital than we were when we started. We just put way more capital into the business" **[M2023-081]**.

The rate cannot run past its base. "an abundance of funds tends to dampen returns" **[L1996-002]**; GEICO is "not a business where, if you double the capital, you can double the earnings easily" **[M1995-069]**; "Most of the great businesses generate lots of money. They do not generate lots of opportunities to earn high returns on incremental capital" **[M2003-120]**. Munger: "what 15 percent per annum compounded will do is grow way faster than the economy can grow" **[M1997-096]**; Buffett: "when the compound rate becomes higher than the discount rate, you get into infinite numbers" **[M1997-095]**. The test: "if you trace out the mathematics of it, you bump into absurdities, then you better change expectations somewhat" **[M1999-067]**; compare the earnings a price implies with how many companies ever reach them **[M1999-076]**, **[L2000-038]**; doubt "companies out there that are implicitly promising you [...] that they’re going to earn at these rates of 20 percent-plus" **[M1999-024]**.

**4. The capital-heavy business as a second best.** The rows accept it, named as such: "as the second-best choice, still a good choice, the answer is yes. It’s not as good as the best choice" **[M2018-055]**; Munger: "they’re good enough" **[M2018-056]**. The condition is a decent return on the added sums **[L2009-012]**: "cash-consuming businesses, by their nature, are unattractive unless the cash they consume gets to earn a reasonable return" **[M2012-057]**. Regulated returns are fair, not rich: "deploy large sums at fair returns" **[L2005-005]**; "there’s no way we get rich on our utility investments. But there’s no way we get poor, either" **[M2009-104]**; "if they earn a decent return on capital, it can be a good business over time" **[M2007-112]**. The capital-heavy businesses that frighten are those "where you just pump in more money without knowing" the return **[M2009-104]**; the same figures that please from a growing utility would be hated from "a declining department store" **[M2014-088]**. The speakers state its costs plainly: BNSF must spend "more than its depreciation charge to simply maintain its present level of business", and earns "a pittance on the replacement value of the property" though "an acceptable return on its purchase price" **[L2023-009]**; also **[M2024-058]**, **[M2024-011]**, **[M2023-101]**.

### Why it is a WEIGHING, and the one place it is a STOP

It is a WEIGHING because the rows grade businesses on it rather than exclude them. The 1998 passage ranks three grades and admits the second **[M1998-081]**; the 2009 letter enters capital-heavy businesses on decent returns **[L2009-012]**; the 2018 answer names them second best **[M2018-055]**; and a non-growing business can be the better buy **[M1994-080]**. Return on capital also describes the business, not the purchase, so price can change the verdict on the investment while leaving the verdict on the business **[M2010-090]**, **[M2018-070]**. The low-return business held long comes nearest to a stop **[M1998-006]**, **[M1998-120]**, but the rows say it will not do well, not that it is barred. A required numeric return on capital: no instance found in the v5 ledger for this section's rows; the nearest are a 15 percent charge for capital to Berkshire's own managers, "it shouldn’t be such a high-hurdle rate that things that we want to do don’t get done" **[M1996-075]**, and "we’ve never used the term" for a hurdle rate **[M2007-096]**.

The one STOP is the acquisition criteria, which ask for businesses "that meet all of the following criteria", among them "(3) Businesses earning good returns on equity while employing little or no debt" **[R1997-001]**. For a whole business offered to the buyer, a business that fails (3) is not a candidate. The criteria name no figure for "good"; the judgment of the return remains a weighing even inside the stop. The STOP itself, the "little or no debt" criterion **[R1997-001]**, is owned by Q9 and applied there: see Q9. Q3 reads the return half of criterion (3) and does not apply the stop a second time. *(settled 2026-10-04 at the reconciliation, which found the same criterion carried as a STOP in both Q3 and Q9.)*

## Q4 — DO THE NUMBERS SHOW WHAT IT EARNS after every real cost, and can its condition be known from them? STOP on confusion, otherwise WEIGHING.

In the speakers' words: "you should understand what the figures are saying and what they don’t say and what they can’t say and what the management would like them to say that the auditors wouldn’t like them to say" **[M2025-032]**.

> "The numbers in any accounting report **mean nothing, per se, as to economic value**. They are guidelines to tell you something about how to get at economic value. [...] And of course, if we find numbers where it looks like people are taking the **most optimistic interpretation** of things that they can under GAAP and all of that, we get **very worried** about people who look like they massage the numbers in any way." — **[M1994-079]**, 1994 annual meeting

> "if I don’t understand it, I figure it’s probably because **the management doesn’t want me to understand it**. And if the management doesn’t want me to understand it, there’s probably something wrong going on. I mean, people don’t obfuscate with numbers, usually, without a purpose. And when you run into that **the best thing to do is you stay away**." — **[M2003-029]**, 2003 annual meeting

> "Depreciation charges are a more complicated subject but are **almost always true costs**. Certainly they are at Berkshire. I wish we could keep our businesses competitive while spending less than our depreciation charge, but in 51 years I've yet to figure out how to do so." — **[L2015-004]**, 2015 letter

### What the figures do and do not tell

The accounts are where the reading starts and never where it ends. Munger: "they’re a place to start economic analysis. The figures are frequently quite silly on a functional basis" **[M1994-017]**; Buffett: "you start with the accounting figures as the raw material of understanding a business, but you have to bring something additional to that" **[M2003-028]**, and "we sort of rewrite the accounting for any business that we’re looking at" **[M1994-016]**; also **[M2002-066]**. Accounting is "the language of business", but not "a perfect language" **[M1998-093]**; it "doesn’t reflect value" **[M2022-070]**, yet is "a terribly useful tool, if understood, in order to estimate value" **[M2017-064]**. The economics, not the treatment, decide: the speakers would "much prefer to purchase $2 of earnings that is not reportable by us under standard accounting principles than to purchase $1 of earnings that is reportable" **[R1995-006]**; and a cost left uncounted is still a cost, as GM and Ford learned of pensions: "believe me, it’s real" **[M2005-057]**.

The rows name what to read and how. Read "balance sheets over an 8 or 10 year period before I even look at the income account because there are certain things it’s harder to hide or play games with on the balance sheet" **[M2025-032]**. Read operating earnings, not a net income swung by securities gains: "Ignore our net income figure, however" **[L2010-017]**; net income is "sometimes [...] the all-deceptive figure" **[M2011-003]**; also **[M2019-001]**, **[M2023-001]**. Check the beginning and terminal years of any growth rate, since "a base year in which earnings were poor can produce a breathtaking, but meaningless, growth rate" **[L2005-003]**; also **[L2003-004]**. Separate businesses with different balance sheets, since "lumping them together [...] impedes analysis" **[L2008-005]**. Ask whether a subsidiary's results would stand without a parent's credit **[L2010-010]**, what a seller kept after tax ("What they got was about $2.5 billion") **[M2010-066]**, and whether the cash is free to use: "off-shore cash is simply not worth as much as cash held at home" **[L2016-009]**. Book value tells little of a good business: "a low price-book ratio means nothing to us" **[M1998-116]**; also **[L2018-001]**. Purchased goodwill is read separately: forget it when judging the business, include it when judging the capital allocation, "because we paid for it" **[M2011-060]**.

### The costs that are real

- **Depreciation.** "With rare exceptions, depreciation is an economic cost every bit as real as wages, materials, or taxes" **[R1996-023]**; "the cash outlay it represents is paid up front, before the asset acquired has delivered any benefits to the business" **[L2002-040]**; "It’s reverse float" **[M2003-106]**, "the worst kind of an expense" **[M2017-099]**. It is "not inappropriate in most companies to use as a proxy for required capital expenditures" **[M1998-127]**, but where it falls short the shortfall is subtracted: at railroads, "merely spending their depreciation expense will not keep them in the same place" **[M2016-092]**, and the reader "should also subtract something to reflect BNSF's inadequate depreciation charge" **[L2015-004]**; in heavy-capital businesses "your depreciation charges are inadequate and you’re kidding yourself as to your real economic profits" **[M2015-049]**.
- **EBITDA, therefore, is not earnings.** "The one figure we regard as utter nonsense is the so-called EBITDA" **[M1998-086]**; "does management think the tooth fairy pays for capital expenditures?" **[L2000-036]**; Munger: substitute "the phrase, “bullshit earnings.”" **[M2003-109]**, and "Earnings before what really counts in costs" **[M2012-035]**. The test: "I’ll look at that figure when you tell me you’ll make all the capital expenditures" **[M2003-108]**. Coverage is measured as "pre-tax earnings/interest, not EBITDA/interest" **[L2012-002]**.
- **Stock pay.** "Stock-based compensation" is "the most egregious example" of real expenses owners are told to ignore **[L2015-003]**; people found prices easier to inflate "if they didn’t count compensation expenses" **[M2003-112]**; a seller once proposed adding earnings "by sort of lying about our accounting" through options **[M2019-078]**. Dilution from options is computed, not waved off **[M2014-004]**. The speakers' own definition of earnings: "a figure calculated after interest, taxes, depreciation, amortization and all forms of compensation" **[L2021-003]**.
- **Restructuring and special charges.** "a large chunk of costs that should properly be attributed to a number of years is dumped into a single quarter" **[L1998-031]**; telling owners year after year to ignore necessary business adjustments "is misleading" **[L2016-007]**; "we do not ask you to forget about those costs" **[M1999-023]**.
- **Catastrophe losses.** Excluding them from "true" earnings "is deceptive nonsense" **[L2002-002]**; "this “except for” stuff doesn’t mean much in insurance" **[M2018-024]**.
- **Pensions and retiree promises.** "The real lollapalooza is pension fund accounting" **[M2003-110]**; assumed returns are chosen for the earnings they produce **[M2001-026]**, **[L2007-017]**, **[L2007-020]**; a pension book is "a big annuity company on the side", whose "liabilities are a lot more certain than the assets over time" **[M2013-075]**.
- **Taxes.** The government holds "what I call the Class AA stock" **[M2021-035]**; of deferred taxes in a utility, "I don’t think I would look at that as a hidden form of equity" **[M2015-030]**; also **[M2016-058]**.
- **Not every charge is real.** Amortization of purchased intangibles is often added back **[M1997-015]**, **[M2017-061]**, **[M2018-006]**, but "Some truly deplete over time while others never lose value. With software, for example, amortization charges are very real expenses" **[L2012-003]**.

### The tells

"accounting can offer you a lot of insight into the character of management. [...] Obviously, if some prepaid expense, deferred asset accounts start building up suspiciously high, and inventories look out of line, you know, with sales [...] you want to look twice at companies like that" **[M1995-064]**. The rows add their kin:

- **Weak accounting where it is visible.** "If a company still does not expense options, or if its pension assumptions are fanciful, watch out. [...] There is seldom just one cockroach in the kitchen" **[L2002-039]**.
- **Predictions and the habit of beating them.** Predicting growth rates is "both deceptive and dangerous" **[L2000-037]**; predictions "corrode CEO behavior" and "can turn fudging into fraud" **[L2000-039]**; "we become downright incredulous if they consistently reach their declared targets" **[L2002-041]**; beating expectations: "It requires no talent to manipulate numbers: Only a deep desire to deceive is required" **[L2022-006]**; also **[M2005-038]**, **[M2010-001]**.
- **Adjusted earnings featured.** "a management that regularly attempts to wave away very real costs by highlighting "adjusted per-share earnings" makes us nervous. That's because bad behavior is contagious" **[L2016-006]**; also **[M2016-095]**.
- **Big baths, merger reserves, reserves that move with a stock sale.** "When deals occur in which liabilities are increased immediately and substantially, simple logic says that at least one of those virtues must have been lacking" **[L1998-034]**; the abuse is in "the subsequent release back into earnings" **[M1999-048]**; reserves "down very suspiciously" before an offering; "They give themselves away. But I can’t give you a checklist of 40 items" **[M2013-085]**; also **[L1998-033]**, **[M2001-027]**.
- **Profits that may not exist.** "when you see a company with lots of gains on securitization, you ought to get a little suspicious" **[M2003-101]**; both sides of one contract booking a profit **[M2013-091]**, marks on both sides that "don’t equate out to zero" **[M2007-037]**, **[M2022-072]**; assets "good until reached for" **[M2008-064]**; earnings that rise "as your credit is destroyed" **[M2009-003]**.
- **Clear speech.** A plus: a management "that doesn’t care about the accounting but does explain to you in clear terms what’s going on" **[M1994-018]**; "we expect a company's CEO to explain in his or her own words what's happening" **[L2000-036]**.

The tells do not always read as fraud. Munger: "they’re not being deliberately fraudulent, because they’re deluded" **[M2013-087]**; Buffett on decent neighbours who "play games with any number that came to them" **[M2022-070]**; and the practice spreads by imitation: "Bad accounting drives out good" **[L1998-030]**; also **[L1998-029]**, **[M1999-049]**. The verdict is on the accounts either way.

### Why confusion is a STOP

"when the accounting confuses you, I would just tend to forget about it as a company. I mean, it’s probably — it may well be intentional, and in any event, you don’t want to go near it. I — we have never had any great investment results from companies whose accounting we regarded as suspect" **[M1995-063]**. Confusion is treated as a signal of purpose, "people don’t obfuscate with numbers, usually, without a purpose" **[M2003-029]**, and suspicion is enough: "If you ever get suspicious about accounting, just go onto the next company" **[M1995-065]**. On EBITDA talk the stop is stated as a count: "the number of times we’re going to buy into a company [...] where people are talking about EBITDA, is going to be about zero" **[M2002-026]**. The cost is named and accepted: "that may mean we pass up a whole lot of other opportunities, too" **[M2002-026]**. Where revenues and expenses are not matched "on something close to cash in the short-term", the opportunity for games is larger **[M1995-065]**; the speakers name construction in progress and financial institutions as "particularly, probably, prone to it" **[M2013-086]**. Whether a financial institution's condition can be known at all is the merge's separate theme (M20) and is not drafted here.

The STOP has an edge the rows draw: a management merely "preoccupied with accounting considerations" is "a negative", but "we can’t afford to use it as a total exclusionary factor" **[M1994-018]**. Short of confusion or suspicion, Q4 is a WEIGHING: the earnings are recast and carried forward.

### How Q3 and Q4 feed Q7

Q7 asks how much cash, how sure and how soon. Q4 supplies the cash figure the speakers would accept: "regular pretax earnings", not EBITDA **[M2012-034]**, after "interest, taxes, depreciation, amortization and all forms of compensation" **[L2021-003]**, less any shortfall of depreciation against what keeps the business in place **[L2015-004]**, plus amortization that does not deplete **[L2012-003]**. Q3 supplies what must be put back to get it: the maintenance need as a stated guess **[M2000-144]**, the growth outlays and what each dollar of them earns **[L1999-024]**, **[M2001-019]**, and the rate at which retained capital is redeployed, which "interacts" with the price paid **[M1995-113]**. Where reinvestment eats the reported figure, the cash the owner receives "will regularly fall considerably short" of reported earnings **[L2023-009]**. Q3 also caps the arithmetic Q7 may use: no growth rate above the discount rate carried forever **[M1997-095]**, and no result that traces to an absurdity **[M1999-067]**. And Q4's figures are only the beginning, since "to figure out that answer, you have to understand something about business" **[M1994-079]**.

**Equity-method income.** At Q3, Q4 and Q7, a share of an investee's profit carried under the equity method is counted as the cash dividends actually received from that investee, unless the investee's own accounts are read, in which case its retained earnings are judged as the rows judge any retained earnings, by what the money kept becomes **[L1994-024]**, **[L1996-038]**, **[M2018-045]**. Rationale: value is the cash "that can be taken out of a business" **[R1996-018]**, and a share of profit the holder neither receives nor has read is not yet shown to be that cash; the dividends are the cash that comes out. This is how the MITSY regression run counted it (operating cash, which takes in the dividends received, not the share of profit). *(correction pass 2026-10-05: gap (g) of the ruling case, section 5. The rule is ours, stated as the drafters applied it; it is a CONVENTION, confessed.)*

## Q5 — WHO RUNS IT? Able, honest, in love with the business rather than the money, and the same once paid. STOP on integrity, WEIGHING on ability.

The question, in the words of the row that asks it whole: "do we really want to be in partnership with this person and count on them to behave in the future when they don’t own the business, as they behaved in the past when they do own the business. And that stops a fair number of deals." **[M2015-005]**

> "Well, I think you judge management by **two yardsticks**. One is **how well they run the business** [...] You have to have some understanding of **the hand they were dealt** when they themselves got a chance to play the hand. [...] And then the second thing you want to figure out is **how well that they treat their owners**." — **[M1994-008]**, 1994 annual meeting, AM

> "I don’t know how to tell someone else how to select managers using those criteria: **do they love the business or do they love the money?** It’s very, very important. I mean, **it’s crucial.**" — **[M2000-098]**, 2000 annual meeting, PM

> "And the decision we have to make is, **are they going to have the same passion for the business after they hand us the stock certificate and we hand them the money?** [...] And if we’re wrong on that, **no contract is going to save us.**" — **[M2009-072]**, 2009 annual meeting, PM

> "And that **trustworthiness is more important than the brains.** It’s not that they don’t have the brains, but **we wouldn’t hire anybody, no matter how able, if we didn’t trust them.**" — **[M2015-047]**, 2015 annual meeting, PM

The rows ask four things of the people: can they run it, will they treat the owners right, do they love the work more than the cheque, and will that survive the cheque. Integrity is the one the rows make absolute. Ability they weigh, and they weigh it against the business, which they put first: "we still look to the underlying business, though." **[M1996-037]**

---

### The two yardsticks

The first yardstick is the record read against the circumstances. Buffett reads "what they’ve accomplished and what their competitors have accomplished, and seeing how they have allocated capital over time" **[M1994-008]**; the capital half is Q6's subject (see Q6). The second is the treatment of owners, and the two travel together: "how often the ones that, in my view, are the poor managers also turn out to be the ones that really don’t think that much about the shareholders, too. The two often go hand in hand." **[M1994-009]**

The 1993 letter states the same pair as two of the five factors of real risk: "The certainty with which management can be evaluated, both as to its ability to realize the full potential of the business and to wisely employ its cash flows" and "The certainty with which management can be counted on to channel the rewards from the business to the shareholders rather than to itself" **[L1993-020]**. Munger names the cost of failing either: "One, the guy favors himself at the expense of the shareholders, and the other is he’s — he does foolish things. [...] Either way, it’s very costly to you, as the shareholder." **[M1995-109]**

The formula that carries both is the one-line purchase rule: "businesses that have good underlying economics and are run by **honest and able people**" **[L1994-001]**. The traits under the two words recur in every era: "brains and energy and integrity" **[M1997-115]**; "integrity, intelligence, and experience, and dedication" **[M1998-062]**; "brains, passion and integrity" **[L2007-014]**.

### Integrity, the STOP

Integrity is not one trait among the others; without it the others turn against the owner. The habits recorded at Q4 as a weighing (targets made or beaten, guidance, an adjusted figure featured in place of GAAP) are judged here with the rest of the integrity record, as "things that they do in public in relation to their investors and the promises they make" **[M2004-067]**; doubt closes the file **[M2013-088]** *(2026-10-06, section A of the gaps case)*.

> "We’re looking for energy. And we’re looking for integrity. And we tell them, **if they don’t have the last, the first two will kill you.** Because if you hire somebody without integrity, you really want them to be dumb and lazy, don’t you?" — **[M2005-003]**, 2005 annual meeting, AM

> "His instructions included this warning: "**Never deal with a rascal under the expectation that you can prevent him from cheating you.**" [...] People are not that easy to read. **Sincerity and empathy can easily be faked.**" — **[L2023-003]**, 2023 letter

Kiewit's version is repeated in 2016, with the front-page test added: someone "pursuing a course of action which, if put on the front page, you know, would make you very unhappy, you can get in a lot of trouble. It may take a while." **[M2016-033]** The rule is older than the phrase: "you can’t make a good deal with a bad person. And you can — that means we just forget about it. I mean, we don’t try and protect ourselves by contracts" **[M1995-084]**. And it is applied on doubt, not on proof: "If you’ve got doubts, forget it." **[M2013-088]**; "we rule out 90 percent of the times. And we may be wrong about a fair number that we’re ruling out. The important thing is whether the ones we’re ruling in we’re right about." **[M2007-092]**

**What "no contract is going to save us" means** **[M2009-072]**. The rows offer no legal or structural safeguard for character. After the sale "they have no financial need to work. They have to want to work. And we can’t stand there with a whip. We don’t have any contracts at Berkshire. They don’t work" **[M2005-002]**; passion "Can’t be done by contract." **[M2008-005]**; "Our model is a seamless web of trust that’s deserved on both sides." **[M2009-073]** Due diligence cannot reach it either: "how are you going to check that as — by due diligence, you know?" **[M2016-067]**. Nor can ownership change it afterwards: "We think it’s like marrying somebody to change them. It just doesn’t work very well." **[M2012-050]**; "you better not count on the fact that you’re going to change their course of action." **[M2009-009]** So the only protection is the judgment made before buying, and a doubt there is a no.

**The tells of dishonesty the rows name.**
- Too good to be true: "they usually are people that tell you things that are too good to be true, for one thing." **[M2002-028]**; "we’re deeply suspicious when the proposition is too good to be true." **[M2007-091]**
- Reports that dance: "We can usually tell when somebody’s dancing around something, or where their — when the reports are essentially a little dishonest, or biased" **[M1995-111]**; the industry's key figure left out, or "touched on, it was done in a dishonest manner" **[M2007-082]**; numbers obscured on purpose, "people don’t obfuscate with numbers, usually, without a purpose." **[M2003-029]**
- A management fixed on its stock price: "when we see the price of a stock posted in the lobby of the headquarters or something, you know, things like that make us nervous." **[M2004-067]**; "if you’re trying to get your stock to be overpriced, you’re very likely to cheat on your earnings and cheat on projections. Cheat on everything." **[M2014-010]**
- Taking credit: the people wanted "don’t take credit for things that they didn’t do" **[M1998-167]**, repeated as "they don’t claim credit for things they don’t do" **[M2015-045]**.
- Owners as marks: leaders who "view shareholders as patsies, not partners" **[L2001-002]**; the contrast is those who "have gotten rich with their shareholders and not off their shareholders" **[M2003-055]**.
- What they talk and laugh about: "people give themselves away fairly often. [...] what they regard as important and not important, there are a lot of clues" **[M2007-090]**; "It’s like they got a sign on their chest that just says, “Jerk. Jerk. Jerk.”" **[M1997-152]**
- Professed, not held: "everybody mouths the integrity, even when it’s lacking." **[M2010-069]**
- Serial issuance of shares, "one of the surest indicators of a promotion-minded management, weak accounting, a stock that is overpriced" **[L2014-015]**: see Q6, Part A, Issuance and deals, where it is carried as a candidate second STOP. *(cross-reference added 2026-10-04 at the reconciliation.)*

Reputation is not a tell of honesty. Lloyd's "had this terrific reputation — behavioral reputation — over centuries. And I think that they coasted for a while on that." **[M1995-066]**; Royal Dutch "would have been near the top of my list" before "the oil reserves phonied for years" **[M2004-068]**. Some fields fail the test as a whole: "there are whole fields that you can just quit playing because it looks like there’s too much fraud in it." **[M2002-025]**

### Love of the business, and after the sale

The test is the primacy of the love, not the absence of interest in money: "Nothing wrong with liking the money. In fact, we’d be a little disappointed if most of them didn’t like the money. But in terms of whether the primacy is loving the money or loving the business, that’s very important to us." **[M2000-053]** The reason is practical: "If they love the money, there’s nothing wrong with that, but they probably wouldn’t be running the business for us a year or two down the road." **[M2003-060]** Munger ranks it above talent: "I would argue that probably the passion is more important than the brainpower." **[M2003-063]**

The cheque is the hazard. "three times out of four, thereabouts, the manager is the owner and is receiving tens of millions, maybe hundreds of millions of dollars. So they don’t have to work. And we have to decide in that time when we meet them whether they love the business or love money." **[M1998-061]** The question becomes whether the behaviour survives the payment: "will they be feeling the same way after the deal as they did the day before the deal?" **[M2009-096]**; "we count on them to run the business with as much enthusiasm after they have the money as they did before." **[M2013-084]** The speakers concede there is no instrument for it: "assessing whether he’s going to behave differently in the future in running that business than he has in the past when he owned it, that’s incredibly important, but there’s no checklist in the world that’s going to answer that." **[M2016-066]** Love cannot be supplied after the fact: "you can’t put that into somebody. But we do recognize it when it’s there." **[M2009-098]**

The seller's conduct at the sale is read as evidence:

> "We find it meaningful when an owner **cares about whom he sells to**. [...] When this emotional attachment exists, it signals that important qualities will likely be found within the business: **honest accounting, pride of product, respect for customers, and a loyal group of associates** [...] When an owner auctions off his business, exhibiting a total lack of interest in what follows, you will frequently find that it has been **dressed up for sale**, particularly when the seller is a "financial owner."" — **[L2000-008]**, 2000 letter

The reverse case is put bluntly: a business offered for resale "it’s a piece of meat to them. And if it’s a piece of meat to them, you know, what am I going to do with it?" **[M2003-061]** The positive signs are small acts: a seller who "shared a meaningful part of his proceeds from the sale with a large number of his associates" **[L1995-007]**; one who "cared about who bought it, and was realistic as to price" **[L2001-004]**; one who did not want to "leave the employees behind" **[M2002-030]**. For a hired executive the same love reads as "What can I do for Berkshire," not, "What can Berkshire do for me?" **[M2017-070]**, as being "all in" for the company, not for himself **[L2014-028]**, and in what he wants for himself: the banker who lost interest when the new building had to be shorter than a competitor's "taught me a lot about the guy’s motivations in life." **[M2023-056]**

### Ability, the WEIGHING

The rows rank ability below the business, in plain words:

See Q2, test 2, for **[M1996-037]**.

> "And Tom Murphy, you know, he said **the real key was buying the right business.** [...] we could have brought in Tom Murphy and told him his job was to run the textile business. And it would have done a little bit better. **But it still would have failed.**" — **[M2024-030]**, 2024 annual meeting, AM

"If you have a big enough moat, you don’t need as much management." **[M1999-106]** "there are some businesses so good that they’ll easily stand a lot of folly in the managerial suite." **[M1995-110]** Ability therefore enters as a weight on value, not as a gate: "The moat and the management are part of the valuation process, in that they enter into our thinking as to the degree of certainty that we attribute to the stream of income" **[M1999-104]**; "if the CEO's talents or motives are suspect, today's value must be discounted." **[L2010-002]**

The weight is heavy where the business is weak or commodity-like. "Buying a retailer without good management is like buying the Eiffel Tower without an elevator." **[L1995-006]** For an insurer, "No installed base, key patents, critical real estate or natural resource position protects an insurer's competitive position. [...] The critical variables, therefore, are managerial brains, discipline and integrity." **[L2003-014]** For a whole-business purchase, management must already be there: "Management in place (we can't supply it)" **[R1995-014]**. And the wrong person can sink a good one: "the number one risk factor is that this business gets the wrong management." **[M2021-042]**

**What ability shows in, as the rows read it.**
- A long record, not a promise: "we don’t like banjo hitters who suddenly proclaim that they can become power hitters." **[M1996-038]**; "we’ve actually seen they batted .350, or whatever it may be, in the major leagues." **[M2005-039]**
- How the record was made: "we’ve always been more concerned about how our record is achieved than the precise record itself." **[M2012-089]**; "a product of sound thinking rather than simply being in tune with a trend or simply just being lucky." **[M2013-068]**
- Facing reality: "tell us the truth but tell themselves the truth, which is even more important." **[M2000-153]**; "He never engages in wishful thinking or otherwise distorts reality" **[L2000-018]**.
- Focus: "Loss of focus is what most worries Charlie and me when we contemplate investing in businesses that in general look outstanding." **[L1996-033]**; "He has no ego compulsions forcing him into some sort of action." **[M1996-092]**
- Sense in everything, not brilliance sometimes: "everything Tony says and does makes sense." **[M2000-130]**; "IQ does not always translate into rationality and behavioral success or wisdom" **[M2020-049]**.
- Knowing the limits: "It's important as well that he knows his limits." **[L2014-027]**
- A money mind, shown in how a management reasons about buying in its own stock **[M2017-056]** (the buyback rule itself: see Q6).

Schooling is not evidence: "I never look at where a candidate has gone to school. Never!" **[L2024-004]**. And ability, once missing, is not supplied: "the unable people by and large you can’t fix." **[M2016-069]**

### Culture, read from outside

Most M07 rows are Berkshire describing its own conduct as an owner (the merged map counts 190 of its 251 rows as rules): "our chief contribution to the businesses we acquire is what we don’t do" **[M1995-090]**; "We buy to keep." **[L2005-006]**; "We are understanding about business mistakes; our tolerance for personal misconduct is zero." **[L2022-001]**; "our culture is 99.9% of running the business" **[M2022-037]**. These are not tests of a target as stated. The rows below become tests of another company only by inversion, and I mark which are which.

Stated by the speakers as a test of another company:
- "There are also readings in corporate culture that have to be made. Owning stock in a corporation where you know that if shareholders or somebody else has to suffer, the choice is likely to be that somebody else will be chosen." **[M1998-121]**
- A bank's culture before a firm buy decision: "I think you should know something about the culture of the management and the institution to make a firm buy decision on a bank" **[M2008-090]**; "I am deeply distrustful of situations in which everybody wants to get rich and envies everybody else. I regard that atmosphere as utterly toxic." **[M2023-055]**
- A culture that does not match its talk: "you don’t find people saying, you know, “We’re a wonderful partnership,” and then voting themselves, you know, huge options." **[M2018-069]**
- An organization that lies to itself: "And people know it throughout the organization. And they adopt the norms of what they think is happening up above them." **[M2000-154]**

Berkshire's own conduct, turned into a question by inversion:
- Figures changed at the top. Berkshire's rule: "we’ve never told anybody to change a figure. And we never will." **[M2022-055]** Inverted: does this headquarters demand numbers its units cannot honestly make? The rows name the symptom elsewhere: divisions forced to beat the prior quarter, where "a lot of terrible decisions and terrible practices creep into those businesses" **[M2009-039]**; managers "chewed them out, you know, monthly for not making their projections, so they learned to make them whether they were actually really making them or not." **[M2011-035]**
- Acting on bad news. Berkshire's rule: "a pound of cure, promptly applied, is worth a ton of cure that’s delayed." **[M2017-007]** Inverted: when this management learned of a problem, did it act? "the main problem was they didn’t act when they learned about it." **[M2017-005]**
- The structure that kills passion. Berkshire's rule: "I can certainly create a structure that will take that passion away from them." **[M2012-093]** Inverted: is this company's best talent yearning for another job? "the truly talented divisional managers seldom have the job they really want." **[L2000-002]**
- Decay at the top. Berkshire's successor test, "the ABCs of business decay, which are arrogance, bureaucracy and complacency" **[L2014-029]**, reads as a question of any management.
- Cost cutting. "it can be done in a dumb way or a smart way." **[M2016-073]**

What the inversion rules out is stated directly: "we really don’t believe in buying into organizations to change them." **[M2000-152]**; "it would be hard to turn Salomon into a Berkshire." **[M2015-009]**; "it’s a lot easier to build a new organization around a culture than it is to change the culture of an existing organization." **[M2010-021]**

### The tests

As the speakers phrase them. They also warn that none is a formula: "I can’t write you out a set of questions that you should ask somebody." **[M2002-125]**

1. **The record against the hand dealt.** Read what they and their competitors accomplished **[M1994-008]**.
2. **The proxy.** "read the proxy statements, see what they think of — see how they treat themselves versus how they treat the shareholders" **[M1994-009]**. (What the pay itself should be: see Q6.)
3. **The letters and reports.** "whether the management is telling us about the things that we would want to know about if we owned a hundred percent of the company" **[M1998-036]**; "if it’s clearly the product of some investor relations department or outside consultant or something of the sort, you know, that tells you something about the individual." **[M2007-083]**; "a standardized bunch of popular jargon that looks like it came out of the same consulting firm, I do think it’s a big turnoff." **[M1998-038]** The accounts as well: "accounting can offer you a lot of insight into the character of management." **[M1995-064]**
4. **How they talk about mistakes.** "mistake" or "wrong" were forbidden words at board meetings or analyst calls; "That taboo, implying managerial perfection, always made me nervous" **[L2024-003]**; against it, owners kept "in sync" by being told "when you make mistakes" **[M2010-081]**, and "tell us the bad news immediately" **[M1998-039]**.
5. **The owner question.** "“What would you be doing differently if you owned it all yourself?”" **[M2017-029]**, also **[M2009-042]**.
6. **The succession question.** "“Who besides you would be the ideal person to run this, and why?”" **[M2014-059]**
7. **The trustee test.** "managers whom I would love to have as a sibling, in-law, or trustee of my will" **[L1995-031]**; repeated in 2016 **[M2016-032]**.
8. **Love or money, and whom the seller cares to sell to** **[M2000-098]**, **[L2000-008]**.
9. **Scuttlebutt, last and to refute.** "talking to competitors, and customers, and suppliers, and ex-employees, and current employees [...] But it should be the last 20 percent or 10 percent." with the purpose "to possibly reject your original hypothesis." **[M1998-144]** And of a good partner: "Are there any more at home like you?" **[L1997-025]**
10. **Behaviour over time, not one meeting.** "we have to have some evidence of behavior in front of us." **[M1998-166]**; "people leave track records in life." **[M1998-168]**

For marketable stocks the speakers read rather than meet: "In terms of sizing up managements — obviously if we’re going to buy the whole business, that’s a different question." **[M2007-081]** The after-the-sale test (section above) belongs to whole-business purchases; for a stock, the yardsticks and the reading tests carry the weight.

- The seller who loves the money first **[M2003-060]**; the auction and the "financial owner" whose business is "dressed up for sale" **[L2000-008]**; the business that is "a piece of meat" to its seller **[M2003-061]**.

- A whole-business purchase that needs management supplied **[R1995-014]**.
- A management whose reports mislead on the figure that matters **[M2007-082]**.
- A management whose habit of making or beating its numbers, carried from Q4, leaves doubt when read with the rest of the record **[L2002-041]**, **[L2022-006]**, **[M2013-088]** *(moved from Q4's list 2026-10-06, section A of the gaps case)*.

Not ruled out by Q5 alone: a great business under ordinary management. That is the WEIGHING **[M1996-037]**, **[M1995-110]**.

## Q6 — WHAT WILL THEY DO WITH THE MONEY AND WITH THE OWNERS? WEIGHING.

**The question in the speakers' words.** The 1993 letter names it as a factor of its own, split off from the manager's
ability to run the business **[L1993-020]**:

> "2) The certainty with which management can be evaluated, both as to its ability to realize the full potential of the business and to wisely employ its cash flows; 3) The certainty with which management can be counted on to channel the rewards from the business to the shareholders rather than to itself" **[L1993-020]** (1993)

And the meetings return to it as the central job and the central judgment:

> "it is, if not the key function of the CEO, it’s one of two or three key functions at say 80 or 90 percent of all companies. And if you can’t do it yourself, you’re going to make a lot of mistakes." **[M1994-082]** (1994)

> "And then the biggest judgment you have to make is how well capital will be deployed in the future. [...] the question becomes, “What do we do with the money, as it comes in?” And that will have a huge impact on the value 10 years from now." **[M2001-111]** (2001)

**Why it has its own place.** Three reasons, each from the rows. First, L1993-020 lists the employment of cash flows and
the channelling of rewards as items separate from the evaluation of the business, and puts them before price **[L1993-020]**.
Second, the speakers rank it: "test number one" **[M1998-033]**, "the biggest judgment" **[M2001-111]**, "The first law of
capital allocation" **[L2011-003]**. Third, the speakers say it can be judged apart from the present value of the
businesses: "it is not only a question what the present businesses are worth. It’s a judgment on the efficiency or the
effectiveness with which retained earnings will be used." **[M2007-031]** (2007). A business can pass Q2 and Q3 and still
have its surplus spent badly; L1994-016 says the really good business is precisely the one that throws off more than it
can use **[L1994-016]**, so the better the business, the larger the question.

### Part A: the money

#### The retention test

The rule, stated in every era:

> "And any time we thought that we weren’t going to get more than a dollar of value per dollar retained, that, obviously, would be the course to follow." **[M1994-061]** (1994)

> "We test the wisdom of retaining earnings by assessing whether retention, over time, delivers shareholders at least $1 of market value for each $1 retained." **[R1995-009]** (1995)

> "Obviously, if we can keep a dollar and it becomes, on a present-value basis, worth more than a dollar, it’s foolish to pay it out." **[M2000-049]** (2000)

> "The test about whether to pay dividends is whether you can continue to create more than one dollar of value for every dollar you retain." **[M2008-104]** (2008)

> "over time, if we’re going to keep your money, we have to earn a better-than-average return on that money we keep and that has to translate into the stock selling at a premium over the money we retain from you." **[M2011-072]** (2011)

The test has two halves, keep or return, and then the best use:

> "The first question is, is when you have capital, is it better to keep it or return it to shareholders? It’s better to return it to shareholders when you cannot create more than a dollar of value with that capital. That’s test number one. And if you pass that threshold, [...] then you simply look around for the thing that you feel the surest about, and that promises the greatest return weighted for that certainty." **[M1998-033]** (1998)

The yardstick is opportunity cost, not a formula. "The cost of every deal we do is measured by the second best deal
that’s around at a given time, including more — doing more of some of the things we’re already in." **[M2001-084]** (2001).
Munger: "it’s your alternatives that are competing for the use of your time or money" **[M2003-117]** (2003); Buffett:
"we measure alternatives against each other, and we measure alternatives against dividends, and we measure alternatives
against repurchase of shares." **[M2003-116]** (2003).

The check is made after the fact and over a span: "if after three or four years, you’ve found that the dollars we’ve
retained hasn’t created more of that in value, then the presumption becomes very strong at that point that we should
start paying out money." **[M1998-110]** (1998). The 1995 report put the span at "a five-year rolling basis" **[R1995-009]**,
and the 2009 report rewrote the test because a falling market could fail a good allocator: "(1) during the period did our
book-value gain exceed the performance of the S&P; and (2) did our stock consistently sell at a premium to book" **[R2009-002]**
(2009). The speakers also name the retainer's bias: "almost any management that wants to retain money is going to
rationalize it" **[M1998-110]**, and of themselves, "there’ll be a certain — denial — we’ll go through" **[M1997-094]** (1997).

**The test as the analyst runs it.** Take the earnings the company has kept over a span of years (three or four in
**[M1998-110]**, five in **[R1995-009]** and **[R2009-002]**). Ask whether value per share rose by at least that much,
read in market value (**[R1995-009]**, **[M2011-072]**) and against intrinsic value, since the market leg alone misleads
when prices fall **[R2009-002]**. Then ask the forward question: "Can you keep using all of the capital you generate,
effectively, for a very long time?" **[M2010-097]** (2010). Where the business cannot use its earnings, as with See's,
"it would be an enormous mistake for See’s Candy to retain money" **[M2008-104]**. Where a manager keeps capital in a
declining operation, the 2014 letter expects him to stay: "A CEO with capital employed in a declining operation seldom
elects to massively redeploy that capital into unrelated activities." **[L2014-016]** (2014).

#### Buybacks

**Only below value, and only with money the business does not need.**

> "There is only one combination of facts that makes it advisable for a company to repurchase its shares: First, the company has available funds -- cash plus sensible borrowing capacity -- beyond the near-term needs of the business and, second, finds its stock selling in the market below its intrinsic value, conservatively-calculated. To this we add a caveat: Shareholders should have been supplied all the information they need for estimating that value." **[L1999-023]** (1999)

> "Continuing shareholders are hurt unless shares are purchased below intrinsic value. The first law of capital allocation [...] is that what is smart at one price is dumb at another." **[L2011-003]** (2011)

The needs of the business come first even when the stock is cheap: "One is when a business both needs all its available
money to protect or expand its own operations and is also uncomfortable adding further debt. Here, the internal need for
funds should take priority." **[L2016-003]** (2016). The second exception is a better use elsewhere **[L2016-003]**; the
same two tests appear at the meeting: "if you’ve got other things that are more intelligent, you don’t do it. And if it
isn’t intelligent on an absolute basis, you also don’t do it." **[M2022-083]** (2022).

**The owner's view against the manager's.** The speakers frame the buyback as one partner buying out another:

> "If you had a partnership and the partner wanted to sell out to you at 120 percent of what the business is worth, you’d say forget it. And if he’d want to sell out to you for 80 percent of what it’s worth, you’d take it. It’s not complicated." **[M2015-074]** (2015)

> "The real thing is to buy stock — repurchase shares — only when you think you’re doing it at a price where the remaining shareholders have had — are worth more the moment after you repurchased it than they were the moment before." **[M2019-002]** (2019)

The manager's view, as they report it, is a fixed sum, a show of confidence or a prop: "repurchase announcements almost
never refer to a price above which repurchases will be eschewed" **[L2016-002]** (2016); "dictated by management's desire to
"show confidence" or be in fashion rather than by a desire to enhance per-share value" **[L1999-028]** (1999); "a lot of
share buying is designed to, sort of, prop the stock price. In other words, it’s not bargain-seeking." **[M2006-041]** (2006).
Munger on the motive: "if you’re repurchasing stock, just a bull it higher, it’s deeply immoral. But if you’re repurchasing
stock because it’s a fair thing to do in the interest of your existing shareholders, it’s a highly moral act." **[M2021-031]**
(2021). Buying to cover options is ruled out by name **[M2014-008]**, and so is buying to prevent dilution: "dilution by
itself is a negative and buying back your stock at too high a price is another negative. So it has to be related to
valuation." **[M2016-049]** (2016).

**What a buyback at a high price does.** "If you’re repurchasing shares above a rationally calculated intrinsic value, you
are harming your shareholders, just as if you issue shares beneath that figure" **[M1996-013]** (1996). "if you buy a dollar
bill for 90 cents, you’re doing your shareholders a favor. And if you buy it for $1.10, you are doing them no favor at all."
**[M2014-008]** (2014). Companies that issue options and then repurchase "sell low and then they buy high" **[M1998-027]** (1998).

**The speakers' own practice.** A demonstrable margin, not a fine one: "it should be by a demonstrable margin." **[M2016-048]**
(2016); "we would not want to buy a dollar bill for 95 cents, or 94 cents, or 93 cents." **[M1999-101]** (1999). Sellers told:
"we want to be very sure that every shareholder that sells to us knows that we think that it’s dramatically — or
significantly — undervalued" **[M2012-010]** (2012). A floor of cash kept first: "as long as we don’t take our cash position
below 20 billion" **[M2012-010]**. Never a prop: "We will not, however, prop the stock at any level." **[L2019-010]** (2019).
And the error they will accept: "We will never buy in our stock at a silly price. We may make a mistake by not buying it at
a cheap price." **[M2009-049]** (2009). For the analyst, a target's programme is read against these: does it name a price
**[L2016-002]**, does it serve the business's needs first **[L2016-003]**, and are the sellers told **[L1999-023]**.

**The buyback with no stated price, tested.** A programme that names no price above which it stops weighs against, because the rows find value "entirely purchase-price dependent" **[L2016-002]**, unless the prices actually paid sit at or below the bottom of the Q7 range for the business, the "intrinsic value, conservatively-calculated" of **[L1999-023]** and "the bottom boundary of our estimate" of **[L2013-012]**; then the purchases are read by what they did, not by what the announcement left out. *(correction pass 2026-10-05: gap (j) of the ruling case, section 5; the two TJX analysts of Test A' split on a fixed-sum buyback at rising prices. Reading the prices paid against the Q7 range is a CONVENTION, ours, drawn from these rows.)*

#### Issuance and deals

**Value given against value got.** The rule is the same whatever the currency:

> "Only if we receive less in value than we give. That’s the key to it. [...] If we give 200 million of cash for a business that’s worth a 150 million, we are worse off. We may not have issued a share of stock. But we have diluted the value of your stock if we do that." **[M1995-001]** (1995)

> "Too often CEOs seem blind to an elementary reality: The intrinsic value of the shares you give in an acquisition must not be greater than the intrinsic value of the business you receive. [...] You can't get rich trading a hundred-dollar bill for eight tens (even if your advisor has handed you an expensive "fairness" opinion endorsing that swap)." **[L2014-012]** (2014)

> "Most stock deals, they think about what they’re getting and they don’t think about what they’re giving. [...] I’ve heard a lot of discussion about dilution and when dilution will be overcome and all that sort of thing, but that is not the question." **[M2007-123]** (2007)

Berkshire's own rule of issue: "We will issue common stock only when we receive as much in business value as we give. This
rule applies to all forms of issuance" **[R1995-010]** (1995). Why acquirers lose: "the acquirer typically gives up more
intrinsic value than it receives." **[L1994-015]** (1994). A premium is defensible only if "a) its stock is overvalued
relative to the acquiree's or b) the two enterprises will earn more combined than they would separately" **[L1997-031]**
(1997), and the speakers "never count on synergies" **[L2016-008]** (2016).

**Stock deals.** "When a management proudly acquires another company for stock, the shareholders of the acquirer are
concurrently selling part of their interest in everything they own." **[L2005-004]** (2005). The base rate is set by the
forces around the chief executive: "So there are all these forces that push toward deals, and if you try to push toward
deals, you’re going to get a lot of dumb deals." **[M2014-076]** (2014). The serial issuer is a warning sign of character:
"At both BPL and Berkshire, we have never invested in companies that are hell-bent on issuing shares. That behavior is one
of the surest indicators of a promotion-minded management, weak accounting, a stock that is overpriced and [...] outright
dishonesty." **[L2014-015]** (2014).

**The right tests of a deal.** Per-share value years out, not the first year: "it is our judgment on a 10-year basis."
**[M1999-061]** (1999); "we have rejected many merger and purchase opportunities that would have boosted current and
near-term earnings but that would have reduced per-share intrinsic value." **[L1994-014]** (1994). Debt does not rescue a bad
price: "even a high-priced deal will usually boost per-share earnings if it is debt-financed. At Berkshire, in contrast, we
evaluate acquisitions on an all-equity basis" **[L2017-004]** (2017). And afterwards: "Post mortems of acquisitions, in which
reality is honestly compared to the original projections, are rare in American boardrooms. They should instead be standard
practice." **[L2014-013]** (2014).

**The one STOP the rows carry in Part A.**

> "If we wouldn't dream of selling Berkshire in its entirety at the current market price, why in the world should we "sell" a significant part of the company at that same inadequate price by issuing our stock in a merger? [...] If shares of a prospective acquirer are selling below their intrinsic value, it's impossible for that buyer to make a sensible deal in an all-stock deal. You simply can't exchange an undervalued stock for a fully-valued one without hurting your shareholders." **[L2009-019]** (2009)

Stated as a stop for the analyst: a company whose stock sits below its intrinsic value and pays for an acquisition wholly
in that stock has made a deal the rows call "impossible" to make sensibly **[L2009-019]**. No further weighing of that deal
is done. Everything else in Part A is graded (see below).

Serial issuance stays here as a candidate second STOP: "we have never invested in companies that are hell-bent on issuing shares" **[L2014-015]**, quoted under Stock deals above. Whether it closes the file or is weighed is unresolved and carried in section VI; Q5 names it among the integrity tells and points here. *(settled 2026-10-04 at the reconciliation as to placement only.)*

#### Dividends and the use of cash

**When a dividend is right.** When the dollar cannot be used at more than a dollar: "a company that expects to regularly earn
more than it can profitably employ in its business, should be paying out dividends." **[M2004-089]** (2004); "there’s no
reason to keep a dollar in the business that’s worth 90 cents if you keep it in the business." **[M2000-050]** (2000). Not by
ratio: "the idea of paying out 20 percent of your earnings, or 10 percent, or 30 percent of earnings in dividends strikes us
as nuts." **[M2000-051]** (2000). The speakers prefer, where retention passes the test, that owners sell a little stock
rather than receive a dividend, because "dividends impose a specific cash-out policy upon all shareholders" **[L2012-014]**
(2012). Whatever the policy, "dividend policy should always be clear, consistent and rational." **[L2012-015]** (2012).

**Cash as the default, not the goal.** "Cash at Berkshire is a residual. [...] if we have cash around, it’s simply because we
haven’t found anything we like to do" **[M1995-057]** (1995); "if there’s nothing smart to do, cash is the default option."
**[M2003-072]** (2003); "the option is cash and not doing something that we really are excited about." **[M2007-105]** (2007).
Idle cash is not to be cured by a marginal deal: "we'll try to resist the temptation to do something marginal simply because
we are long on cash." **[L1993-007]** (1993); "We like even less doing dumb deals because we do them forever." **[M2006-028]**
(2006). A description of cash as a call option: no instance found in the v5 ledger by a search for that phrase. Cash also
has a floor that is a matter of survival, not of use: "available cash or credit — is a lot like oxygen" **[M2014-020]** (2014);
that row belongs to the standing rule and Q9, and is named here only so the floor is not read as idle money.

**The order of uses the rows give.** First the business itself: "A company's management should first examine reinvestment
possibilities offered by its current business [...] to otherwise widen the economic moat separating the company from its
competitors." **[L2012-011]** (2012). The 2021 letter gives Berkshire's full order: "The first is always front and center in
our minds: Increase the long-term earning power of Berkshire's controlled businesses through internal growth or by making
acquisitions. [...] Our second choice is to buy non-controlling part-interests [...] Our final path to value creation is to
repurchase Berkshire shares." **[L2021-005]** (2021). The 2001 meeting gives the gate before the order: keep or pay out
first, then dividend or repurchase **[M2001-082]**. The order is subordinate to price: the 2016 letter lets a cheap stock
yield to "far greater value" elsewhere **[L2016-003]**, and M2003-116 measures every use against every other.

#### Mistakes the speakers narrate here

From group 9 (stock-paid deals, value given over value got) and group 12 (retention and buybacks) of section 3 of the merged map.

- **Dexter.** "I clearly made a mistake in paying what I did for Dexter in 1993. Furthermore, I compounded that mistake in a

  world. And I did it all by myself." **[M2007-124]** (2007); "Trading shares of a wonderful business [...] for ownership of

- **BNSF's stock leg.** Accepted in 2009 as "a close one" **[L2009-021]**, called a mistake in 2012: "we would not use our stock

- **The small value-destroying deal.** "We need to show that we are in the hunt. Besides, it's only a small deal," and

#### Why Part A is a WEIGHING

The tests are stated as judgments, not thresholds. "you don’t need a mathematical answer" **[M1998-033]**; "That’s
subjective, but any given decision like that is subjective. Over time, you get an objective test" **[M2000-050]**; "And you
never know the answer to that." **[M2001-082]**. The retention test runs "over time" **[R1995-009]**, and its failure raises
a presumption **[M1998-110]** or shifts a burden of proof **[M2005-021]**, words of weight, not of exclusion. The buyback
rules carry their own allowance for error: "they’ll make mistakes in that, but you make mistakes in a lot of businesses.
But over — that should be the guiding principle." **[M2020-044]** (2020). The one place the rows close the door is the
all-stock deal by an undervalued acquirer **[L2009-019]**, stated above as a STOP. A company that has made such a deal is
not cleared by the rest of Part A.

### Part B: the pay, the board and the owners

Part A carries the 1993 factor that splits this question off from the manager's ability: "The certainty with which management can be counted on to channel the rewards from the business to the shareholders rather than to itself" **[L1993-020]**. Part B is where that certainty is read. The speakers say where to look, and they rank it below the manager himself:

> "So it — you know, read the proxy statements, see what they think of — see how they treat themselves versus how they treat the shareholders, look at what they have accomplished, considering what the hand was that they were dealt when they took over compared to what is going on in the industry." — **[M1994-009]**, 1994

> "There are more problems with having the wrong manager than with having the wrong compensation system. [...] any compensation sins are generally of minor importance compared to the sin of having somebody that’s mediocre running a huge company." — **[M2007-006]**, 2007

So Part B is a WEIGHING inside a WEIGHING: no row found makes a pay plan alone a stop, and **[M2007-006]** says the person outranks the plan. What the rows do make decisive is what the plan reveals about the person: "The acid test for reform will be CEO compensation. [...] What many will fight, however, is a hard look at their own pay and perks." **[L2002-029]**.

#### Pay tied to what the person controls

> "So we believe in paying for performance, but we believe in tying performance to what is actually under the reasonable control of the person that’s being measured." — **[M2003-019]**, 2003

> "And the basic rule on incentives is you get what you reward for. So, if you have a dumb incentive system, you get dumb outcomes." — **[M2016-083]**, 2016

**Against the capital employed.** "When capital invested in an operation is significant, we also both charge managers a high rate for incremental capital they employ and credit them at an equally high rate for capital they release." **[L1994-019]**. The reason: "a batting average that does not include a cost of capital is a phony batting average" **[M1995-010]**; managers using other people's money "start thinking of it a little bit like free money" **[M1995-009]**. It is fitted to the business, not stamped on it: "In a capital-intensive business, you have to have something that [...] includes a capital cost element" **[M2009-081]**, while at See's "to put a cost of capital factor in or something like that, what the consultant would tell you, it’s nonsense" **[M2010-030]**.

**Options and their flaws.** The 1998 letter's three questions govern every option discussion after it: "If options aren't a form of compensation, what are they? If compensation isn't an expense, what is it? And, if expenses shouldn't go into the calculation of earnings, where in the world should they go?" **[L1998-028]**. The flaws the rows name are four. No step-up: "the combination of a ten-year option, a low dividend payout, and compound interest can provide lush gains to a manager who has done no more than tread water in his job" **[L1994-021]**; "a royalty on money that you left with me" **[M1997-043]**. The market's ride: an option on one's own stock that rises with the S&P is the same as "an option on $300 million worth of S&P futures" **[M2000-062]**. The dividend conflict: the holder "benefits from retained earnings and benefits not at all from dividends. And that puts his interest, maybe, quite contrary to that of the shareholders" **[M2003-018]**. And the strike: "the base price should be what the business is worth at the time you start" **[M2011-067]**; "They should not be granted at below the intrinsic value of the company" **[M2002-071]**. Options are not condemned as such: "I’ve never taken a blanket position that options are sinful or anything of the sort. I just say they are an expense." **[M2002-084]**; the fair form has "an appropriate strike price, 2) an escalation in price that reflects the retention of earnings, and 3) a ban on his quickly disposing of any shares purchased through options" **[L2004-016]**, and shares held "for a couple years after retirement" **[M2017-050]**. The cost is valued as warrants sold to the public would be **[M1999-025]**, and when Berkshire buys an option-issuing company "we promptly substitute a cash compensation plan having an economic value equivalent to that of the previous option plan" **[L1997-032]**, **[M1999-017]**.

**The hurdle.** "you would probably figure that a chimpanzee could run the place, and have 35 percent pretax margins. And you might want to pay for performance above some number like that. [...] And a lousy manager will always suggest an arrangement like that." **[M2004-097]**. The base is set by the business: "we make it — the base — very high if they’re in a very easy business" **[M2004-095]**. At Berkshire's own capital charge, "we find that 15 percent gets their attention" **[M1996-075]**; that number is Berkshire's for its own units, and no row found offers it as a test of another company. Once set, the bar stays: "we never greet good work by raising the bar" **[L1996-019]**.

**"Heads I win, tails you lose."**

> "In our book, alignment means being a partner in both directions, not just on the upside. Many "alignment" plans flunk this basic test, being artful forms of "heads I win, tails you lose."" — **[L1994-020]**, 1994

Its later forms: plans that "payoff no matter what happens" **[M1999-018]**; managers "who have received huge payouts on the upside and who then, when bad results occurred, have walked away rich" **[L2010-015]**; "the override on other people’s capital without putting up any of their own capital to speak of" **[M2025-050]**. For an institution society must rescue, the downside should leave "their CEO, basically, and his spouse, dead broke" **[M2011-049]**; also **[M2023-050]**.

**Restricted stock.** The rows mention it twice, both times as Berkshire's own refusal: "the company will issue me neither restricted shares nor stock options - my gain-loss equation will always match that of all other owners" **[L1993-004]**; "We will not take cash compensation, restricted stock or option grants that would make our results superior to yours" **[L2001-001]**. As a test of others, the nearest row is about directors: shares bought "with their savings, rather than simply having been the recipients of grants" **[L2019-008]**. No instance found in the v5 ledger of a rule on restricted stock for another company's managers.

**What Berkshire pays its own managers on.** Goals "(1) tailored to the economics of the specific operating business; (2) simple in character so that the degree to which they are being realized can be easily measured; and (3) directly related to the daily activities of plan participants" **[L1996-018]**. "We distribute merit badges, not lottery tickets" **[L1997-022]**. At GEICO, growth in policies in force and the profit of seasoned business, because "you don’t want to award profits, alone. It’d be the dumbest thing you could do" **[M2016-084]**, **[M2016-085]**; in oil, low finding costs, "which is under their control, and which is important" **[M2007-009]**; above all, "widening the moat that separates our business from our competitors’ businesses over time" **[M2010-030]**. No company-wide formula **[M2016-086]**, **[M2012-094]**, and no consultants **[M2004-016]**, **[M2010-029]**. These are Berkshire's own practices; they test another company by contrast. The size of the number is not the sin: "the real sin is having a mediocre manager" **[M1997-045]**; "it's time for directors to shout "Less!"" for anything short of exceptional **[L2002-032]**, **[M1998-064]**.

**Who designs the plan, and how to read the proxy.** "it’s a very strange form of negotiation when the beneficiary is the one that also really does all the design" **[M1997-041]**; "The people who have their hands on the switch are the beneficiaries of the system" **[M1998-066]**. The directors deal in "play money" while the comparables ratchet the pay **[M2004-017]**: "ratchet, ratchet, and bingo" **[M2012-095]**; also **[M2003-022]**, **[M2007-008]**. In the proxy itself the rows give three things to read: how the managers treat themselves against the owners **[M1994-009]**; whether a fixed-price option plan names the dividend conflict, which Buffett has "never, however, seen [...] referenced in proxy materials that request approval of a fixed-priced option plan" **[L2005-014]**; and the length: "If you take 100 pages to explain how you’re paying the people of the place, something is wrong." **[M2009-087]**. Hidden incentives count too: forecasts and ego make "really decent people misbehave" **[M2015-033]**; "I hate, with a passion, rewarding anything that can be easily faked" **[M1996-089]**.

#### The board

> "overwhelmingly, the job of the board of directors is to have the right CEO. I mean, if you’ve got the right CEO, 90 percent of it takes care of itself. [...] I think you have an obligation on the board to make sure that there’s not overreaching by the CEO [...] And I think the third thing that the board does — should do — is they really should bring some independent judgment in on major acquisitions." — **[M2007-120]**, 2007

**What it can do.** The three jobs recur: the right chief executive, no overreaching, independent judgment on acquisitions **[M2006-010]**, **[M2009-084]**, **[L2004-014]**; and a successor, "the number one obligation of the board" **[M2012-116]**, **[L2011-001]**. If those are right, "you can forget about all this other check the list — checklist stuff" **[M2009-084]**; "checklists are no substitute for thinking" **[M2004-005]**.

**What it cannot do.** It cannot master the business from outside: "the directors are not going to be able to read some book or anything like that. But they do have the ability to hold the CEO accountable" **[M2023-050]**. It struggles to remove anyone: "It's almost impossible, for example, in a boardroom populated by well-mannered people, to raise the question of whether the CEO should be replaced." **[L2002-024]**; boards are "part business organizations and part social organizations" **[M2014-012]**. Hardest of all when the chief executive also chairs: "I've seen how hard it is to replace a mediocre CEO if that person is also Chairman." **[L2014-026]**; also **[M2011-010]**, **[M2013-071]**.

**Independence.** The label is rejected for the substance: "A director getting $150,000 a year from a company, who needs it, is not an independent director." **[M2005-090]**; "when compensation is a very important part of a director’s wellbeing, you do not have an independent director" **[M2009-086]**; "the independent directors, in many cases, are the least independent" **[M2019-058]**; also **[M2022-084]**, **[M2009-085]**. The 2002 letter states the positive: directors "who have huge and true ownership interests (that is, stock that they or their family have purchased, not been given by Berkshire or received via options)" **[L2002-033]**.

**The compensation committee and the cocker spaniel.**

> "I have been on one comp committee out of 19 boards. I mean, people are not looking for Dobermans. They’re looking for Cocker Spaniels." — **[M2009-082]**, 2009

The 2019 letter repeats it for directors in general: "When seeking directors, CEOs don't look for pit bulls. It's the cocker spaniel that gets taken home." **[L2019-007]**. Committees "have become slaves to comparative data" **[L2005-015]**, and Buffett goes further: "I don’t think there should be a comp committee. I think the board as a whole actually should thrash this sort of thing out." **[M2009-083]**.

**What the owner looks for.** "The requisites for board membership should be business savvy, interest in the job, and owner-orientation." **[L1993-028]**; business savvy is the rarest **[L2003-012]**, **[M2016-044]**. The question nobody asks: "Does he think like an intelligent owner?" **[L2006-014]**. Whether directors bought shares themselves **[L2019-008]**, **[M2017-053]**, and whether a director's stance on an offer squares with his own buying, "given that self-interest inevitably blurs introspection" **[L2004-015]**. Where the board fails, "the very large shareholders" are "The only cure" **[M2006-011]**; outside directors who cannot get change "should resign" **[L1993-029]**.

#### Owners as partners

The merged map counts M15 as rule rows almost entirely, and nearly all of them state Berkshire's own conduct (see the CONVENTION note in section VI). Said plainly: these are Berkshire's promises to its own owners. They become tests of another company only by inversion, by asking whether that company does the opposite. Three rows apply them outward directly, and they are named at the end of this sub-section.

> "Although our form is corporate, our attitude is partnership. Charlie Munger and I think of our shareholders as owner-partners, and of ourselves as managing partners." — **[R1995-001]**, 1995

> "Our intent is to supply you with the financial information that we would wish you to give us if our positions were reversed." — **[L1994-023]**, 1994

**Candour.** The positions-reversed sentence recurs **[L1995-021]**, **[M1997-054]**, **[M2003-071]**, **[M2004-012]**, **[M2006-080]**. Candour is also self-protection: "the CEO who misleads others in public may eventually mislead himself in private" **[R1995-012]**. Bad news first: "Just tell me the bad news; the good news will take care of itself" **[L1995-021]**. No number to hit: "We don’t give out earnings guidance. We think it’s silly." **[M2016-002]**; "the parent company has never had a quarterly "number" to hit" **[L2018-003]**; and of forecasting earnings, "I can’t imagine anything more destructive" **[M2022-054]**.

**The same information.** "In all of our communications, we try to make sure that no single shareholder gets an edge" **[R1996-016]**; "so that nobody has a jump on any information" **[M2002-118]**; "we regard the holder of one share of B stock as the equal of our large institutional investors" **[L1998-036]**; also **[L1995-034]**, **[M2022-002]**, **[M2021-015]**. Terms once set are kept **[M2002-131]**, **[M2003-002]**, and the company never recommends its own stock **[M2001-112]**.

**No splits.** "we do not consider splitting the stock a pro-shareholder move. If we did, we’d do it." **[M1997-019]**; the reason is the owners it draws, "a better class of shareholders, in aggregate" **[M1998-114]**, **[M2004-074]**, and a price that tracks value: "anything that artificially stimulated the price in one period simply means that some other period’s shareholders are going to be disappointed" **[M1995-118]**.

**Their money as our own.** "We will look at our shareholders as partners and we will be trying to do with their money exactly what we’d do with our own, not seeking to get an edge on them." **[M2018-032]**; "the company's money is the owners' money" **[L1993-030]**, so no pet charities **[M1999-069]**, **[M2015-068]** and no political giving: "I would regard that as a breach of trust with Berkshire" **[M2017-103]**. The root: "we don’t want to disappoint the people who left their money with us, and things flow out of that" **[M2021-065]**.

**Where the speakers turn it outward.** On Google's owner's manual: "you’ll know the kind of people you’re associating with. You’ll know what they will do and won’t do." **[M2004-092]**. On analyst courting: "Which of the present ones are you trying to get rid of?" **[M2022-053]**. On reporting: "I think every management actually has an obligation to that" **[M2023-104]**.

#### The tests

The analyst's questions, as the speakers phrase them:

1. "What would you be doing differently if you owned it all yourself?" **[M2017-029]**
2. In the proxy, how do they "treat themselves versus how they treat the shareholders"? **[M1994-009]**
3. Is pay tied "to what is actually under the reasonable control of the person that’s being measured"? **[M2003-019]**; or is it a "lottery ticket" whose value "is totally out of the control of the person whose behavior we would like to affect"? **[L1996-018]**
4. "If options aren't a form of compensation, what are they?" **[L1998-028]**. Is there a step-up for retained earnings **[M1997-043]**, a strike at the business's worth **[M2011-067]**, and does the proxy name the dividend conflict **[L2005-014]**?
5. Is it "a partner in both directions, not just on the upside"? **[L1994-020]**; or does it "payoff no matter what happens"? **[M1999-018]**
6. What would "a chimpanzee" earn, and does the bar sit above it? **[M2004-097]**
7. Who designed it, and whose hand is "on the switch"? **[M1997-041]**, **[M1998-066]**
8. Does it take "100 pages to explain how you’re paying the people of the place"? **[M2009-087]**
9. Are the director's fees "a very important part of a director’s wellbeing"? **[M2009-086]**, **[M2005-091]**
10. Have the directors bought shares "with their savings"? **[L2019-008]**, **[M2017-053]**
11. "Does he think like an intelligent owner?" **[L2006-014]**
12. Is a mediocre chief executive "also Chairman" **[L2014-026]**, and is there a successor known well and "ready to take over tomorrow" **[L2011-001]**?
13. Are we told what we "would wish you to give us if our positions were reversed"? **[L1994-023]**; is there earnings "guidance" and a desire "to "hit the number."" **[L2019-006]**?
14. When a bad incentive is found, does the chief executive act? "It was bad enough having a bad system, but they didn’t act." **[M2017-005]**

#### What it rules OUT

As WEIGHING, these weigh heavily against; none is written as a stop by the rows except where noted in Part A.

- Earnings that omit the cost of options **[L1998-028]**, **[M1995-088]**.
- Unit managers paid on the whole company's stock price **[L1997-022]**, **[M1999-029]**.
- Upside-only pay **[L1994-020]**, pay that arrives whatever happens **[M1999-018]**, an override without one's own capital in **[M2025-050]**.
- Compensation consultants and peer ratchets **[M2004-016]**, **[L2005-015]**, **[M2012-095]**.
- Directors who need the fees **[M2005-090]**, **[L2019-007]**, and independence by checklist **[M2004-005]**.
- A mediocre chief executive at any price **[M1997-045]**, especially one who also chairs **[L2014-026]**.
- By inversion of Berkshire's own rules: earnings guidance **[M2022-054]**, selective disclosure **[R1996-016]**, the owners' money spent on the managers' causes **[L1993-030]**, **[M2017-103]**.

#### Mistakes the speakers narrate here

From the merged map's group 3 (people, incentives and culture), the ones on pay, boards and owners:

- Buffett's own silence on boards: "Too often I was silent when management made proposals that I judged to be counter to the interests of shareholders. In those cases, collegiality trumped independence." **[L2002-025]**
- Reforms adopted late: directors meeting without the chief executive, and "Berkshire would be more valuable today if I had put in a whistleblower line decades ago." **[L2004-013]**
- Strong incentives that overran: Teledyne, where "the incentives got so strong, and the culture of performance got so strong, that people actually — it went too far" **[M2015-032]**; a claims man hiding claims to avoid teasing **[M2015-034]**.
- Profit shares on paper profits **[M2016-088]**; bonuses tied to bringing in a dubious client, "it’s very hard to control people when their income depends on bringing in dubious people into the door" **[M2004-110]**; bonuses on improper accounting **[M2005-114]**.
- Wells Fargo: "they had the wrong incentives", and "a much greater error" in ignoring them **[M2018-012]**, **[M2017-005]**, **[M2019-005]**.
- Slow with managers who fade or coast: "We’ve been slow every time." **[M2009-097]**; **[M2024-025]**.

The other mistakes in group 3 concern judging people and culture generally and are not drawn on here.

## Q7 — WHAT IS IT WORTH: HOW MUCH CASH, HOW SURE, HOW SOON, AT THE LONG GOVERNMENT RATE, HELD AS A RANGE; AND IS THE PRICE SO FAR BELOW IT THAT IT NEEDS NO PENCIL? STOP.

The speakers ask it in Aesop's words, and they name the rate in the same breath.

> "To flesh out this principle, you must answer only three questions. **How certain are you that there are indeed birds in the bush? When will they emerge and how many will there be? What is the risk-free interest rate** (which we consider to be the yield on long-term U.S. bonds)?" — **[L2000-021]**, 2000 letter

> "Now, the question is **how much do you get back, how sure are you of getting it, when do you get it?**" — **[M2009-004]**, 2009 meeting

> "You better just stick with businesses that you can understand, **use the government bond rate**. And when you can buy them — something you understand well — at a significant discount, then, you should start getting excited." — **[M1996-025]**, 1996 meeting

> "If you need to use a computer or a calculator to make the calculation, **you shouldn’t buy it**. I mean, it should be so obvious that you don’t have to carry it out to tenths of a percent or hundredths of the percent. **It should scream at you.**" — **[M2009-005]**, 2009 meeting

Q7 comes after the business and the people: price is named last in most of the speakers' purchase orderings (merged map, section 2), because the moat and the management are inputs to the value **[M1999-104]**. A business that has not cleared Q1 and Q2 cannot reach it; its cash cannot be pictured **[M1994-077]**.

### The definition

Value is the cash the owner can take out over the life of the business, discounted back to today, stated in nearly the same words for three decades.

> "It is the **discounted value of the cash that can be taken out of a business during its remaining life**." — **[R1996-018]**, 1996 annual report

> "Despite its fuzziness, however, intrinsic value is **all-important** and is the only logical way to evaluate the relative attractiveness of investments and businesses." — **[L1994-007]**, 1994 letter

> "But the intrinsic value pure definition would be the cash to be generated between now and Judgment Day, discounted at an interest rate that seems appropriate at the time." — **[M2017-027]**, 2017 meeting

The cash counted is the cash that comes out, net of what must go back in. Buffett gives the business credit only "for whatever net cash is left every year" **[M1998-080]**, and he adds a fourth Aesop question in 2014:

> "the real question is, A) **are you going to have to put more cash into after you buy it?**" — **[M2014-068]**, 2014 meeting

> "Because at some day, a financial asset has to give you back cash to justify you laying out cash for it now." — **[M1998-082]**, 1998 meeting

**The same formula for stocks and businesses, and for every asset.** The letter that sets out the three questions opens by saying so, and the meetings repeat it for farms, oil fields and apartment houses **[M2002-079]**, **[M2003-135]**.

> "Leaving aside tax factors, **the formula we use for evaluating stocks and businesses is identical**." — **[L2000-021]**, 2000 letter

> "It applies to outlays for farms, oil royalties, bonds, stocks, lottery tickets, and manufacturing plants." — **[L2000-022]**, 2000 letter

> "Businesses have coupons that are going to develop in the future, too. The only problem is **they aren’t printed on the instrument**." — **[M1997-050]**, 1997 meeting

The industry matters only as evidence about the cash **[M1998-149]**, and growth is inside the equation, never a separate style **[M2000-001]**:

> "Indeed, **growth can destroy value** if it requires cash inputs in the early years of a project or enterprise that exceed the discounted value of the cash that those assets will generate in later years." — **[L2000-023]**, 2000 letter

**The three questions carry the earlier ones inside them.** "How sure" is where the moat and the people enter **[M1999-104]**; what management will do with retained cash is a third element of the calculation that can add to value or require a discount **[L2010-002]**:

> "The moat and the management are part of the valuation process, in that they enter into our thinking as to **the degree of certainty** that we attribute to the stream of income — stream of cash, actually — that we expect in the future and the amount of it." — **[M1999-104]**, 1999 meeting

In 2019 the bush gains a fourth and fifth question, who will try to take it and what the worst case is **[M2019-023]**:

> "how certain he is that he’s going to get to the bush, you know, and then **who else is going to come and try and take the bush away** [...] And your success in investing depends on how well you were able to figure out how certain that bush is, how far away it is, and **what the worst case is**" — **[M2019-023]**, 2019 meeting

Value comes from what the asset produces. Money expected from what someone else will later pay, "irrespective of what the asset produces", is named speculation **[M2002-078]**; the Frozen Corporation, which can never pay its owners, makes the same point **[M1998-084]**.

### The rate

The bond's two roles are owned this way: the rate (here); the comparison (Q8). *(settled 2026-10-04 at the reconciliation, which found the two roles owned by neither Q7 nor Q8.)*

**The long government bond is the rate, used as a common yardstick.** The rows give two reasons: the bond can always be bought, and one rate lets every opportunity be compared with every other.

> "The risk-free rate is used merely to equate one item to another. [...] So that becomes the yardstick rate. **It doesn’t mean we want to buy government bonds.**" — **[M1997-086]**, 1997 meeting

> "We use the Treasury rate just to get **comparability across time and across companies**." — **[M1999-103]**, 1999 meeting

> "I mean, **the yardstick for me is always the U.S. Treasury**." — **[M2020-006]**, 2020 meeting

**No risk premium is stacked into the rate.** The rows state this most firmly in 1996 and 1998 **[M1996-024]**, **[M1996-025]**, **[M1998-151]**, in the words the merged map flags:

> "But **we don’t put the risk factor in, per se**, because essentially, the purity of the idea is that you’re discounting future cash. And it doesn’t make any difference whether cash comes from a risky business or a safe business — so-called safe business." — **[M1996-024]**, 1996 meeting

> "not try to have the whole panoply with all different kinds of risk rates, because, frankly, we think that’d just be playing games with numbers. [...] I mean, it may look mathematical. But **it’s mathematical gibberish** in my view." — **[M1996-025]**, 1996 meeting

> "We feel that once it passes a threshold test of being something about which we feel quite certain, that **the same discount factor tends to apply to everything**." — **[M1998-151]**, 1998 meeting

> "So we think all the capital asset pricing model-type reasoning with different rates of riskadjusted return and all that, we tend to think it is — well, we don’t tend to — **we think it is nonsense**." — **[M1998-152]**, 1998 meeting

**Certainty is handled in three other places, not in the rate.** First, at the understanding gate: risk is "a go/no-go valve" **[M1998-150]**, and a business whose future cannot be pictured is dropped, not discounted harder **[M1998-152]**. Second, in the cash estimate: "We may be more conservative in estimating the returns of cash from some, but the discount rate we would use is a constant" **[M2003-147]**. Third, in the discount from value demanded at purchase:

> "we adjust by simply trying to **buy it at a big discount from that present value** calculated using the risk-free interest rate." — **[M1997-126]**, 1997 meeting

One 1994 answer does put certainty into the rate, and it is carried below as a tension rather than smoothed: "The more certain we feel about a business, the closer we are willing to play it" **[M1994-004]**.

**How the level of rates changes what is paid.** Every asset is "100 percent sensitive to interest rates" **[M1994-047]**; rates are "to the value of assets what gravity is to matter" **[M2021-020]**; and low long rates "push the prices of all productive investments upward" **[L2021-006]**. Two adjustments for unusual rate levels are stated, and they point in opposite directions. In 1996 Buffett says that when rates look very low "we might use a little higher rate" **[M1996-024]**; in 1999 he says "We would probably use a lower discount factor in evaluating any business now, under present-day interest rates" **[M1999-011]**. By 2016 the effect is admitted openly, and inexactly:

> "very cheap money makes me **pay a little more for businesses** than when money was at what we previously thought was normal rates. And very tight money would cause me to pay somewhat less. [...] And if you ask me whether I paid a little more for Precision Castparts because interest rates were around zero, than if they’d been 6 percent, the answer is yes. **I try not to pay too much more, but it has an effect.**" — **[M2016-078]**, 2016 meeting

> "And it does **modestly affect what we pay** for businesses." — **[M2016-079]**, 2016 meeting

The rate that matters is the future rate, not today's quote, and the rows admit nobody can supply it:

> "**The most important thing is future interest rates.** And, you know, and people frequently plug in the current interest rate saying that’s the best they can do. [...] But that doesn’t mean I want use the current figure, either." — **[M2017-084]**, 2017 meeting

### The floor or hurdle

The merge left one point open: does M2016-078 retire the ten percent figure of 1994, 2002 and 2003, or only qualify it? *(corrected 2026-10-04 at the reconciliation: this sentence read "of 2002 and 2003"; the 1994 row below states the same figure.)* Every row found in the v5 ledger that names a floor, a hurdle, a minimum or the figure they quit on is quoted here in date order (search words: hurdle, floor, threshold, minimum, expectancy, "drop out", every "10 percent" and "10%"). Rows using those words for pay plans, acquisition size or position limits are left out.

> "Well, I would say that in a world of 7 percent long-term bond rates that we would certainly want to think we were **discounting future after-tax streams of cash at at least a 10 percent rate**. But that will depend on the certainty we feel about the business. The more certain we feel about a business, the closer we are willing to play it." — **[M1994-004]**, 1994 meeting

> "And we are affected in that valuation process to a considerable degree by interest rates, but not by whether they’re 7.3, or 7.0, or 7.5. But I mean, we’ll be thinking much differently if they’re — long-term rates are 11 percent or 5 percent. And — but **we don’t have any magic multiples in mind**." — **[M1995-114]**, 1995 meeting

> "So you can say that isn’t even enough to charge people, but we find that 15 percent gets their attention. And it should get their attention, but **it shouldn’t be such a high-hurdle rate that things that we want to do don’t get done**." — **[M1996-075]**, 1996 meeting (a charge on capital used by the subsidiaries, not a purchase price)

> "Unless, however, we see **a very high probability of at least 10% pre-tax returns** [...] we will sit on the sidelines." — **[L2002-020]**, 2002 letter (the elided after-tax figure carries a damaged character in the row)

> "So we sort of have **a minimum threshold** in our mind about which we’re — below which — we’re unwilling to commit money. [...] rather than bend a little bit and start settling for lower rates for 30 years because rates for 30 days are so low, we would rather just sit it out and wait a while." — **[M2003-148]**, 2003 meeting

> "And we quit on buying — **we don’t want to buy equities where our real expectancy is below 10 percent**. Now, that’s true whether short rates are 6 percent or whether short rates are 1 percent. We just feel that it would get very sloppy to start dipping below that. [...] So, there’s just a point at which we drop out of the game. **And it’s arbitrary.** There’s no — we have no scientific studies or anything." — **[M2003-149]**, 2003 meeting

> "to some considerable extent — **we are guessing at our future opportunity cost**. [...] And if interest rates were to more or less permanently settle at 1 percent or something like that, and Warren were to reappraise his notions of future opportunity cost, **he would change the numbers**." — **[M2003-151]**, 2003 meeting (Munger)

> "on the theory that we’ll find something that gives us 10 percent or better" — **[M2003-152]**, 2003 meeting

> "For instance, **hurdle rates by — based on opportunity costs. Perfectly reasonable concept.**" — **[M2003-130]**, 2003 meeting (Munger)

> "if somebody guaranteed me that the 10-year rate would never go above 4.2 percent for the next 50 years, **we would have to readjust, recalibrate every decision we make** around Berkshire." — **[M2005-104]**, 2005 meeting

### The range, not the point

Value is a range, never a point, and the width of the range is itself a finding.

> "Using precise numbers is, in fact, foolish; **working with a range of possibilities is the better approach**." — **[L2000-024]**, 2000 letter

> "Charlie and I admit that we feel confident in estimating intrinsic value for only a portion of traded equities and then only when we employ **a range of values, rather than some pseudo-precise figure**." — **[L1999-027]**, 1999 letter

> "I say "estimate" because calculations of intrinsic value, though all-important, are **necessarily imprecise and often seriously wrong**. The more uncertain the future of a business, the more possibility there is that the calculation will be wildly off-base." — **[L2005-001]**, 2005 letter

> "And we project out what we think will happen. And **we know whatever our projection is, that it will be wrong**, but we try to be conservative." — **[M2017-013]**, 2017 meeting

> "Now, the intrinsic value is not a specific point, it’s probably a range in my mind that might have a band maybe of 10 percent." — **[M2019-003]**, 2019 meeting

Two people get close but different ranges **[M1996-009]**, **[M2006-082]**, **[M2011-018]**, and fuzziness does not make the method wrong: "the fact that it’s fuzzy to calculate doesn’t mean that it’s not the proper way to think about it" **[M2003-136]**. The rows give an order of work: conservative inputs, without padding each one, and the safety margin applied once at the end **[M2004-055]**. The price is then set against the bottom of the range:

> "If the answer is yes, we will buy the stock (or business) if it sells at a reasonable price in relation to **the bottom boundary of our estimate**." — **[L2013-012]**, 2013 letter

**What a wide range means.** Usually it means no decision:

> "Usually, **the range must be so wide that no useful conclusion can be reached**." — **[L2000-025]**, 2000 letter

> "At the other extreme, there are many times when the most brilliant of investors can't muster a conviction about the birds to emerge, not even when a very broad range of estimates is employed. [...] In cases of this sort, **any capital commitment must be labeled speculative**." — **[L2000-026]**, 2000 letter

A wide range is not offset by a bigger discount. The speakers move on instead:

> "if a business gets to the point where we think the industry in which it operates, the competitive position or anything is so chancy that we can’t really come up with a figure, **we don’t really try to compensate for that sort of thing by having some extra large margin of safety**. We really want to try to go on to something that we understand better." — **[M2007-022]**, 2007 meeting

**CONVENTION (ours, confessed 2026-10-05): how the range is built, and the two ways it closes.** The cash input is owner cash after every real cost, as Q4 recasts it **[L2021-003]**, **[L2015-004]**, **[M2012-034]**, averaged over the last five years so that no single year sets the base **[L2005-003]**. It is carried forward at the growth the business has actually shown over those years, never above it, and capped by the growth arithmetic of Q3: no rate that runs past the discount rate or traces to an absurdity **[M1997-095]**, **[M1999-067]**, **[M2003-120]**. It is carried for ten years, the horizon of understanding **[M2000-037]**, **[M2012-065]**, and then at no real growth, discounted throughout at the long government rate **[L2000-021]**, **[M1996-025]**. The range's two ends are the no-growth case and the shown-growth case **[L2000-024]**, **[L1999-027]**. Q7 then closes in one of two ways besides IN. TOO HARD when the top of the range is more than about three times its bottom: "the range must be so wide that no useful conclusion can be reached" **[L2000-025]**, and a wide range is not cured by a bigger discount **[M2007-022]**. OUT when the range is narrower than that and the price sits inside it or just below its bottom: the case is not a screamer and would need a pencil **[M2009-005]**, **[M1996-084]**. Rationale: Test A' and the regression found that, with no rule for the growth input or the horizon, the same wide range closed OUT for one analyst and TOO HARD for another, and Visa's run said the growth input decided everything. The rows give the definition, the rate, the range and the screamer; the five-year average, the ten-year term, the two ends and the three-to-one width are ours and are confessed as such. *(correction pass 2026-10-05: gap (b) of the ruling case, section 5.)* **Four specifics the PG re-test asked for (2026-10-05, both analysts):** the growth shown is measured on the aggregate owner cash, not per share, because a per-share rate set against the whole market value counts the buyback twice; the cash input deducts all capital spending, with the depreciation variant shown beside it and the maintenance judgment stated where the filing allows one (Q3); "no real growth" after year ten is computed as zero nominal growth, the discount rate being nominal and the rows supplying no inflation figure; and a price above the top of the range closes OUT through the floor convention above, the expected return at the price then being below the minimum, the same close as a price inside the range. Q6's buyback test, which needs the bottom of the range, is run when Q7 is reached and recorded back at Q6.

**Six more specifics, from the small-cap runs** *(CONVENTION, ours in method, the principle of each a row's; adopted 2026-10-06 at the operator's decision on section D of `Framework/v5/CASE 2026-10-05 - gaps found by the small-cap runs, for the operator's approval.md`, after the four re-runs of `Framework/v5/PREREGISTRATION 2026-10-06 - four re-runs under sections A, D and H of the gaps case.md`, whose three amendments are in the text)*. (a) **Cycles.** Where the first or last year of the five-year window is a cyclical peak or trough, or otherwise aberrational **[L2005-003]**, **[L2003-004]**, the base is the average over the last full cycle, peak to peak or trough to trough, and the growth is measured between the averages of successive cycles **[M2011-101]**, **[M2011-102]**, **[M2021-039]**; the literal window is shown beside it. The series that defines the cycle is owner cash as Q4 recasts it, and a year is aberrational on the filer's own description of it. (b) **Decline.** A negative growth shown is carried as negative and makes the bottom of the range; the no-growth case is then the top **[M2006-058]**, **[M2006-059]**. Where the series changes sign, or the compound rate between end years is meaningless, the growth is measured between the first and second halves of the window, and where that too is meaningless the range is the no-growth case alone, said so. (c) **Acquisitions.** Cash paid for businesses, net of businesses sold, is capital spending for the range. Either it is deducted and the total growth is credited, or it is left out and only organic growth, as the filer reports it, is credited; never left out with the total growth credited **[M1998-080]**, **[M2014-068]**, **[M2023-081]**. The default pair is the first, acquisitions deducted and the total growth credited; the second is shown beside it where the filer reports organic growth. A deal financed by debt is valued as if paid with equity: unlevered owner cash, less today's net debt **[L2017-004]**. A merger inside the window is valued on the combined companies' own averages, with the deal's financing deducted. A deal closed after the window and before the price date is valued on its own filed record, its financing added to today's net debt, and credited at nothing where no earnings are filed. (d) **Working capital.** The increase in working capital is capital spending for the range **[M2008-036]**, **[M2003-122]**; a single year's draw that the filer ties to one contract or event is an aberrational year by (a), and the run shows the range at the business's working-capital intensity before the event beside it. (e) **Growth spending.** Where the filing allows the maintenance guess **[M2000-144]**, **[L1999-024]**, the central case is owner cash after maintenance only, with the growth spending charged against the growth it buys; the all-spending case is shown beside it. Never charge all spending and also cap the growth that spending bought. Where the growth bought is worth less than its cost, the maintenance-only no-growth case is the top of the range and the growth case the bottom; the inversion is the finding, "growth can destroy value" **[L2000-023]**, not an error. (f) **The cap.** No rate above the discount rate is carried after year ten **[M1997-095]**; inside the ten years the shown rate is the cap, and any result that traces to an absurdity is cut back **[M1999-067]**, **[M1997-096]**. A price above the top of the range closes OUT however wide the range, by the floor convention above.

### Margin of safety and the screamer

The price must be so far below the bottom of the range that the decision is obvious without arithmetic.

> "there ought to be such a margin of safety that it ought to be so attractive that **you don’t have to carry it out to three decimal places**." — **[M1995-115]**, 1995 meeting

See the foundations (margin of safety) for **[M1996-084]**.

> "I didn’t need to know whether it was worth 97 billion or 103 billion if I was buying it at 35 billion. [...] **If you have to carry it out to three decimal places, it’s not a good idea.** [...] I might not know how much they weigh, but I would know they were fat." — **[M2008-068]**, 2008 meeting

> "Occasionally, though, even very conservative estimates about the future emergence of birds reveal that the price quoted is **startlingly low in relation to value**." — **[L2000-025]**, 2000 letter

Munger says the calculation is never written out; Buffett agrees, and says why: "If it isn’t pluperfect obvious that it’s going to work out well, if you do the calculation, he tends to go on to the next idea" **[M1996-083]**; "we really like the decision to be obvious enough to us that it doesn’t require making a detailed calculation" **[M1995-032]**.

**The size of the discount follows the certainty of the business.** It is larger when the future is less sure **[M1997-080]**, small for See's or Coca-Cola **[M2007-022]**, and defined by Munger as "getting more value than you’re paying" **[M2007-027]**. One test of whether it is big enough is to imagine the market shut and rates higher: "if they closed down on the stock market for a couple of years, if interest rates go up another hundred basis points or 200 basis points, we’re still happy with what we’ve bought" **[M2007-096]**. The margin is there because things go wrong: "we had a margin of safety when we bought into it, [...] and it turns out we needed it" **[M2002-104]**.

> "Obviously, if you understood a business perfectly — the future of a business — you would need very little in the way of a margin of safety. So the more volatile the business is — or possibility is — but assuming you still want invest in it, **the larger the margin of safety**." — **[M1997-080]**, 1997 meeting

**Where screamers are found.** The stock market gives bargain prices and private sellers do not **[M1994-040]**, **[M2008-041]**. For whole businesses the rows accept a fair price for a wonderful business **[M2003-040]** and, for the rare business that can reinvest at high returns, "you probably should stretch a little" **[M2013-059]**.

### Why it is a STOP

Three ways close the file here.

**Cannot be valued: stop.**

> "And if we don’t have the faintest idea what the future stream is going to look like, **we don’t have the faintest idea what it’s worth, now**." — **[M1994-077]**, 1994 meeting

> "But up to the point where you decide what you’re willing to pay — **you may decide you can’t estimate it at all**. I mean, that’s what happens with us with most companies." — **[M1996-025]**, 1996 meeting

> "If, however, we lack the ability to estimate future earnings � which is usually the case � **we simply move on to other prospects**." — **[L2013-012]**, 2013 letter (the replacement characters are in the row)

**Valued, but the price does not clear the floor: stop.**

> "So, **there’s just a point at which we drop out of the game**." — **[M2003-149]**, 2003 meeting

**Valued, price below value, but the case is close: stop.**

> "So if you really need a calculator to figure out that it’s — the discount rate is 9.6 percent instead of 9.8 percent — **forget about the whole exercise**. Just go onto something that shouts at you." — **[M2009-005]**, 2009 meeting

The 2008 meeting adds that a price is never waived: "we don’t do anything when the phrase “regardless of price” enters into the sentence" **[M2008-041]**. In the rows' three boxes **[M2006-013]**, the first case is "too hard" and the second and third are "out".

### What it rules OUT

- **Book value as value.** "book value is meaningless as an indicator of intrinsic value" **[L1994-008]**; **[M1996-008]**.
- **Ratios, peer multiples and single yardsticks.** Dividend yield, P/E, price to book and growth rates "have nothing to do with valuation except to the extent they provide clues to the amount and timing of cash flows" **[L2000-023]**; no single relative P/E **[M2002-021]**; a GEICO priced against "other insurance stocks" was mispriced **[M1996-016]**.
- **Risk-adjusted discount rates and the capital asset pricing model** **[M1996-025]**, **[M1998-152]**; a "cost of equity capital" is "a perfectly amazing mental malfunction" **[M2003-117]**.
- **Formulas that claim precision.** "there is an ultimate formula, but the trouble is you don’t know what to stick in for the variables" **[M2017-083]**; Munger's "physics envy" **[M2018-082]**; a staff hurdle that every proposal clears by a tenth **[M2019-076]**.
- **Other people's projections.** "we’ve never looked at a projection in connection with either a security we’ve bought or a business we’ve bought" **[M1995-050]**; **[M2003-065]**.
- **Growth carried past the discount rate.** When the compound rate exceeds the discount rate, "you get into infinity" **[M1997-095]**, **[M2004-023]**; Munger: back off **[M2004-024]**.
- **Assets valued on the next buyer.** Speculation **[M2002-078]**.
- **A low quote on short money as a reason to accept a low return** **[M2003-148]**.
- **The close call.** **[M2009-005]**, **[M1996-084]**.

## Q8 — IS IT BETTER THAN THE ALTERNATIVES: THE BOND, MORE OF WHAT I ALREADY OWN, AND BUYING IN MY OWN STOCK? STOP.

In the speakers' words: "would we rather own this business than more Coca-Cola?" and "Is this better than buying in our own stock" **[M1997-056]**; "one opportunity cost of buying the stock is to compare it with a bond" **[M1997-089]**.

> "So **one opportunity cost of buying the stock is to compare it with a bond.** But you may find that half the stocks in America, you’re so fearful about or know so little about or think so poorly of, that you’d rather have the government bond. So on an opportunity cost basis, **they’re taken out of the filter.**" — **[M1997-089]**, 1997 annual meeting

> "if the new thing **isn’t better than what you already know is available, it hasn’t met your threshold**, then that screens out, you know, 99 percent of what you see, and it’s an enormous thought conserver." — **[M1995-106]**, 1995 annual meeting

> "**The cost of every deal we do is measured by the second best deal that’s around at a given time**, including more — doing more of some of the things we’re already in." — **[M2001-084]**, 2001 annual meeting

> "it’s **your alternatives that are competing for the use of your time or money**, that matter in judging whether you take action or not." — **[M2003-117]**, 2003 annual meeting

Q7 asks what the business is worth. Q8 asks a different thing: whether this use of the money beats every other use open on the same day. The speakers treat the second question as the one that decides action. Munger: "in the real world, your opportunity costs are what you want to make your decisions based on" **[M2007-098]**, and "All intelligent people should think primarily in terms of opportunity cost" is "obviously correct" though "very hard to teach" **[M2001-086]**. He calls the standard cost-of-capital formula "a perfectly amazing mental malfunction" **[M2003-117]**; Buffett defines Berkshire's cost of capital as "what could be produced by our second best idea. And then our best idea has to exceed that." **[M2014-014]**. The comparison is not reserved for the end of the work. When a business is shown to them, "the first thing that goes through our head" is the comparison **[M1997-056]**.

### The three alternatives

**First, the government bond.** The bond is the first thing a stock must beat **[M1997-089]**. Buffett states the test as a forced ten-year choice between any listed stock and a ten-year government: "probably in at least 80 percent of the cases, we’d take the ten-year Government", either "because we didn’t understand the business well enough elsewhere" or because "we may understand it and still prefer the 10 percent Government. So — but we would measure everything that way." **[M1997-090]**. A decade later the bond is still the floor of the comparison: "we are going to want to get a significantly higher return [...] than we are from a government bond. [...] that has to be the yardstick at a base", and when the margin is thin, "We’d rather sit around and wait a little while." **[M2007-095]**. In 2020: "the yardstick for me is always the U.S. Treasury" **[M2020-006]**. The same reach extends to marketable securities as a class: "we also measure businesses against opportunities available in marketable securities, a comparison most managements don't make", and when businesses are priced too high, "we buy securities and bide our time" **[L2010-005]**.

**Second, more of the best thing already owned.** "Before looking at new investments, we consider adding to old ones." **[L1994-032]**. The question Buffett puts to any new business is "“Why would I rather have this than more Coca-Cola?”" **[M1995-105]**, and "It’s crazy not to compare it to things that you’re very certain of." **[M1997-056]**. Munger calls the best existing holding the ordinary person's "measuring stick" **[M1995-106]** and draws the consequence: if you like an opportunity you already hold in quantity "better than 98 percent of the other things you see, well, you can just screen out the other 98 percent because you already know something better" **[M1997-055]**. The ideal purchase is "to have something that you already liked be selling at a price where you feel like buying more of it" **[M1998-014]**. The comparison runs in both directions in the later rows: "measuring something you hold against something that has come along and to see which is more attractive" **[M2015-087]**. The deal measure explicitly includes "doing more of some of the things we’re already in" **[M2001-084]**.

**Third, the company's own stock bought below value.** The rows state this alternative of a company's own capital; for a private holder who runs no company, the analogue is "more of what I already own", the second alternative above. *(settled 2026-10-04 at the reconciliation: the heading's wording is kept, and this sentence fixes its scope.)* Buffett puts it to every management that buys an unrelated business: "Is this better than buying in our own stock, you know?" **[M1997-056]**. The 2021 letter sets the order of uses: first "Increase the long-term earning power of Berkshire's controlled businesses through internal growth or by making acquisitions", second "buy non-controlling part-interests", and the "final path" to "repurchase Berkshire shares", which "When the price/value equation is right [...] is the easiest and most certain way for us to increase your wealth" **[L2021-005]**. The 2023 meeting gives the same order with dividends placed between: what the business needs first, then dividends, then repurchase if capital is ample and the stock "enhances the intrinsic value for the remaining shareholders" **[M2023-044]**. The condition is price: "Continuing shareholders are hurt unless shares are purchased below intrinsic value." **[L2011-003]**; repurchase requires funds "beyond the near-term needs of the business" and a price "below its intrinsic value, conservatively-calculated" **[L1999-023]**; and even then it competes, since substantial buying is described for a stock "selling at, say, 25 or 30 percent less than it was worth, and we didn’t have something else that was even better" **[M2019-002]**. A buyback is also a way to buy more of what is already owned: "we can always buy a little more of TTI for you, the shareholders, by just buying in our stock, too" **[M2024-047]**.

### The filter, then the ranking

M1997-089 gives the procedure as two steps. First the bond strikes out every stock one would rather not own than the bond; then "you start finding corporations where you like the stocks way better than government bonds. You got to compare them one against the other. And when you find one that you regard as the best opportunity, that you can understand as the best opportunity, now you’ve got one to buy." **[M1997-089]**. He calls it "child’s play as a mental process. Now, it’s hard to make the business appraisals." **[M1997-089]**. The ranking is against "the second best deal that’s around at a given time" **[M2001-084]**, and the best idea "has to exceed" the second **[M2014-014]**.

The ranking is made against the field as it stands that day, not against history. Decisions "go through a mind that is looking at everything available that day, including the amount of cash we have, the likelihood of being able to do something else next week or next month [...] And past deals we’ve made don’t really make any difference." **[M2011-073]**. The alternatives "vary greatly from time to time and from company to company" **[M2003-117]**, and every price bears on every other: "you’ve got to compare that to every other bush that’s available" **[M2009-025]**. A hurdle rate "makes nothing but sense", yet "I don’t think there’s any substitute for thinking about a whole lot of investment options and thinking about why one is better than another" **[M2007-097]**. The comparison is described as habitual rather than formal: "we’re always thinking of it against a screen of dozens of businesses — it’s just sort of automatic" **[M1995-093]**. The winner must win by a clear margin: with a sure 8 percent available in quantity, "8 1/2 wouldn’t tempt you" **[M2007-099]**.

**The test:** would I rather have this than the government bond **[M1997-089]**, **[M2007-095]**; than more of the best thing I already own **[M1995-105]**, **[M1997-056]**; than the company's own stock below value, where the capital is a company's **[M1997-056]**, **[L2021-005]**? Is it the best opportunity "that you can understand as the best opportunity" **[M1997-089]**?

**Rules out:** anything that does not beat the bond **[M1997-089]**; the next idea when the best holding can be bought in quantity **[M1997-055]**, **[M1996-041]**; a decision measured against a past bargain rather than today's field **[M2011-073]**; a cost-of-capital formula in place of the comparison **[M2003-117]**.

### Why it is a STOP

The rows describe a failure here as ending the work, not as a factor to set against others. Stocks that lose to the bond are "taken out of the filter" **[M1997-089]**. The measuring stick "screens out, you know, 99 percent of what you see" **[M1995-106]**. Munger: with a sure 8 percent in hand, a perfectly good 7 percent investment means "I don’t have to waste 5 minutes with you." **[M2007-098]**; of two suitors, when "one is way the hell better than the other, you do not have to spend much time with the other. And that’s the way we filter stock buying opportunities." **[M1997-147]**. Buffett, in the same spirit: "Don’t spend 10 minutes, you know, once you know in the first sentence that it isn’t a great, wonderful idea." **[M2004-118]**; a deal is discussed "for three minutes. And we’ll sort of know whether it passes the first threshold." **[M2000-149]**.

The STOP is relative to the day. Because the alternatives move **[M2003-117]** and past deals do not count **[M2011-073]**, a business that fails Q8 fails against this field at this price. The rows' verdict for it is "out" among the three boxes, "in, out, and too hard" **[M2006-013]**, and the default while waiting is to hold the bond or cash and "bide our time" **[L2010-005]**, **[M2007-095]**.

### Where the bond sits

The rows use the bond in two ways, and this draft does not merge them.

**As the discount rate (Q7).** "What is the risk-free interest rate (which we consider to be the yield on long-term U.S. bonds)?" is one of the three valuation questions **[L2000-021]**; "use the government bond rate" **[M1996-025]**; once a business passes a threshold of certainty, "the same discount factor tends to apply to everything" **[M1998-151]**.

**As a filter among alternatives (Q8).** "one opportunity cost of buying the stock is to compare it with a bond" **[M1997-089]**; the ten-year forced choice **[M1997-090]**; "the yardstick for me is always the U.S. Treasury" **[M2020-006]**.

One row holds both at once: "We don’t formally have discount rates", yet the bond "has to be the yardstick at a base" **[M2007-095]**. Munger's 2003 statement reaches under both: "in the last analysis, everything we do comes back to opportunity cost", and "we are guessing at our future opportunity cost" **[M2003-151]**. The rows carry a floor beside the bond: no equities "where our real expectancy is below 10 percent [...] whether short rates are 6 percent or whether short rates are 1 percent", which Buffett calls "arbitrary" **[M2003-149]**, set against "very cheap money makes me pay a little more for businesses" **[M2016-078]**. Whether the bond is the rate, the comparison, or both, and how the 2003 floor stands against the 2016 row, is left open here and to Q7 (merged map, alternative (b) and section 4).

The bond's two roles are owned this way: the comparison (here); the rate (Q7). *(settled 2026-10-04 at the reconciliation: ownership is settled; how the 2003 floor stands against the 2016 row stays OPEN under Q7.)*

## Q9 — COULD IT RUIN US? The target's debt and exposures. WEIGHING.

> "The main thing is you have — are you reasonably sure that you know what you’re doing? And if it gets past that hurdle, then we go on to figure out **the math of gain versus loss and how much loss we can afford to take in anything**. And we’re willing to take what sounds like large losses if we think that the rewards are more likely and proportional." — **[M2019-077]**, 2019 annual meeting

> "We don’t think in terms of — well, we think first in terms of business risk, you know. [...] Now, business risk can arise in various ways. It can arise from **the capital structure when somebody sticks a ton of debt into some business**, and so that if there’s a hiccup in the business that the lenders foreclose. It can come about just by the nature of the — certain businesses are just very risky." — **[M1997-009]**, 1997 annual meeting

> "We are eager to hear from principals or their representatives about businesses that meet all of the following criteria: [...] (3) Businesses earning good returns on equity while employing **little or no debt**" — **[R1997-001]**, 1997 annual report (the same criterion in **[R1995-014]**, 1995 annual report)

> "In our opinion, **the real risk that an investor must assess** is whether his aggregate after-tax receipts from an investment (including those he receives on sale) will, over his prospective holding period, give him at least as much purchasing power as he had to begin with, plus a modest rate of interest on that initial stake." — **[L1993-020]**, 1993 letter

> "We tend to go into businesses that inherently are low-risk, and are **capitalized in a way that that low risk of the business is transformed into a low risk to the enterprise**." — **[M1997-011]**, 1997 annual meeting

> "At Berkshire, in contrast, we **evaluate acquisitions on an all-equity basis**, knowing that our taste for overall debt is very low and that to assign a large portion of our debt to any individual business would generally be fallacious" — **[L2017-004]**, 2017 letter

> "Financial staying power requires **a company** to maintain three strengths under all circumstances: (1) a large and reliable stream of earnings; (2) massive liquid assets and (3) **no significant near-term cash requirements**." — **[L2014-023]**, 2014 letter

L1993-020 makes the purchase test and the risk test one: its five factors bearing on "the real risk" are the business, the managers, their treatment of owners, the price, and tax and inflation **[L1993-020]**. Q9 asks the narrower thing the merge separated out (alternative (a)): whether the target's own capital structure and exposures can produce the loss that the other questions cannot see. The business's slow erosion is not asked here; the rows put that under business risk to "the economic strengths that we perceive currently exist in a business" **[M2000-014]**, which is Q2.

**The test:**
1. Relate the debt to the ability to pay it, under bad conditions, not normal ones: "you can’t talk about debt levels without relating it to the ability to pay debt." **[M1995-104]**; "even under harsh economic conditions, aggregate earnings will be ample to very comfortably service all debt." **[L2003-016]**; **[L2010-012]**. Diversity of the earning sources counts as well as coverage: "A catastrophic event can render a single utility insolvent" **[L2005-019]**.
2. Value it as if it had no debt. Debt that makes a high price look accretive earns nothing **[L2017-004]**; "we’ve got the same metrics that apply to them, whether they happen to have some debt on their own particular balance sheet or not" **[M2002-066]**.
3. Read the target against the three strengths **[L2014-023]**, and do not assume its maturities will roll: "Companies with large debts often assume that these obligations can be refinanced as they mature. That assumption is usually valid. Occasionally, though, either because of company-specific problems or a worldwide shortage of credit, maturities must actually be met by payment." **[L2010-020]**. A buyer that loads the debt on is the case narrated: "now in mortal danger because of the debt piled on them by their private-equity buyers." **[L2008-007]**.
4. Can anyone demand cash from it suddenly: collateral, maturities, cash-out features, deposits that run **[L2014-024]** as the shape; for a lender, "you’ve got to pay attention to whether they’ve gotten out of whack in terms of the value of what they own and what can be demanded of them tomorrow morning." **[M2023-103]**; wholesale funding "can run pretty fast." **[M2012-012]**; options given away, "You don’t give options like that." **[M2023-049]**.
5. What is one event: the aggregation and correlation in its book. "But the question is, what is one event?" **[M2024-021]**; an insurer must "avoid an aggregation of exposures" **[L2002-004]**; "there’s nothing more deadly than unrecognized concentrations of risk" **[M2003-034]**; "if you have an unwitting super-cat exposure that you don’t even recognize you have, it could destroy your company." **[M1997-078]**.
6. Whose promise does it depend on: "you better be very sure that they can and will pay, because the terrible thing that’s happening to you may be presenting terrible things to them." **[M2003-084]**; **[L2001-018]**.
7. Which liabilities grow because the owner is rich: "we have passed up some businesses, because we were worried about the product liability potential." **[M2001-008]**; **[M2002-009]**; "the last thing Berkshire should do is own a helmet company." **[M2014-083]**.
8. Ask the managers: "that’s one I always ask the managements of our subsidiaries, as well as any new investment. I want to know what their nightmare is." **[M1998-020]**. For each threat named, weigh "(1) the probability of the threatening event actually occurring; (2) the range of costs if it does occur; and (3) the timing of the possible loss." **[L2015-008]**, compounding a small yearly chance over the holding period **[L2015-009]**.
9. Set the worst loss against the possible gain: "And that was a big oil field they tapped into, but it wouldn’t remotely pay for this accident." **[M2014-080]**; where "the numbers are big in relation to the amount of profits, you want to look very carefully" **[M1999-139]**.
10. If the risk cannot be known from the disclosures, it is not weighed but set aside: "the only thing we understand is that we don't understand how much risk the institution is running." **[L2002-018]**; "You spot troubles in financial institutions late. It’s just the nature of the beast." **[M2001-066]**. That is the "too hard" box **[M2006-013]**, reached through Q9 for a financial institution. The question is owned by Q1: see Q1, What cannot be judged from the outside, and The door the rows leave open for a bank. A financial institution that reaches Q9 has passed Q1, and this test catches only what Q1 missed. *(settled 2026-10-04 at the reconciliation, which found financial institutions carried in both Q1 and Q9.)*

**Why it is a WEIGHING, not a STOP.** M2019-077 frames it as an amount, "how much loss we can afford to take in anything", and accepts "what sounds like large losses" when the rewards are "more likely and proportional" **[M2019-077]**. Loss on a single purchase is accepted in terms: "We are perfectly willing to lose money on any given transaction." **[M1994-045]**. Debt in a business is tested, not barred: "the test will be: A, whether they control that business in a way that that leverage does not prove dangerous, and secondly, what kind of returns on equity they earn while using it." **[M1994-019]**. Owned businesses carry debt the speakers call right for them: "The size of this debt (which is not now, nor will it be, an obligation of Berkshire) is entirely appropriate." **[L2003-016]**; "We believe it's appropriate to finance a soundly-selected book of interest-bearing receivables almost entirely with debt (just as a bank would)." **[L2003-019]**; "loans that are non-recourse to Berkshire." **[R2009-001]**. They will lend to what they would not own: "we wouldn’t mind lending money to a lot of businesses that we wouldn’t buy common equity in." **[M1998-118]**. And the structure contains one bad unit: "one accident in one subsidiary that caused a big lot of damage, we’re better protected than most companies." **[M2017-088]**. Reader's note, not stated by a speaker: the rows treat the target's ruin as a loss of what was paid, which Q9 weighs; it becomes the buyer's ruin only through the buyer's own financing and sizing, which the standing rule already forbids.

**The one STOP on the purchase side.** The acquisition criteria require "all of the following criteria", and the third is "Businesses earning good returns on equity while employing little or no debt" **[R1997-001]**, **[R1995-014]**. This is the STOP's home; Q3 points here. *(settled 2026-10-04 at the reconciliation.)* It is stated for whole businesses offered to Berkshire; no instance found in the v5 ledger of the same criterion stated for part-interests bought in the market. The rows carry one named exclusion stated as flatly, "So we don’t go near asbestos." **[M2001-032]**; the structure decision does not name it a STOP and this draft carries it as a candidate for the reconciliation, not a ruling.

## Q10 — IS IT THE FAT PITCH, AND AM I BUYING ENOUGH? WEIGHING.

In the speakers' words: "Yeah. You wait for the fat pitch." **[M2003-070]**; the slice map C-T20 asks whether one bought enough of the rare bargain, from **[L2009-016]**: "When it's raining gold, reach for a bucket, not a thimble."

> "Yeah. **You wait for the fat pitch.**" — **[M2003-070]**, 2003 annual meeting

> "but I would say that the chief lesson would be that you’re unlikely to find very many in a whole lifetime. And **when you find one in which you really have thought it out and have confidence, for God’s sakes, don’t do it in a niggardly fashion.** The idea that very smart people with investment skills should have hugely diversified portfolios is madness." — **[M2004-041]**, 2004 annual meeting

> "And you will find opportunities that, **if you put 20 percent of your net worth in it, you’ll have wasted the opportunity of a lifetime**, you know, in terms of not really loading up." — **[M2008-052]**, 2008 annual meeting

> "The whole secret of investment is to find places where it’s **safe and wise to non-diversify.**" — **[M2008-051]**, 2008 annual meeting

Q8 says whether this is the best use of the money. Q10 asks two things the rows join: whether it is good enough to swing at all, and if so whether the swing is big enough. The rows weigh both against the cost of not acting.

### Waiting

Inaction is the default. "Anytime we find anything that makes sense to us, we will do it. The harder part is to make sure that we don’t do something when we don’t find something that makes sense. I mean, that’s the bigger worry." **[M1996-006]**. Cash piling up is no reason to buy: "one of the most important disciplines in running a business or managing investments" is "not to try to coordinate your actions simply with the availability of cash" **[M1996-004]**; "If the money piles up, the money piles up. [...] you don’t get paid for activity, you only get paid for being right." **[M1998-137]**. "your default position is always short-term instruments. And whenever you see anything intelligent to do, you should do it." **[M2004-045]**; "if there’s nothing smart to do, cash is the default option" **[M2003-072]**.

The 1997 letter gives the image: "waiting for the fat pitch would mean a trip to the Hall of Fame; swinging indiscriminately would mean a ticket to the minors. [...] If we swing, we will be locked into low returns." **[L1997-006]**. In 2024: "we only swing at pitches we like" **[M2024-034]**. Dry spells are accepted: "An occasional dull stretch for new buying, this is no great tragedy in an investment lifetime." **[M1998-138]**; "The businesses aren’t going to perform better in the future just because you got antsy and decided you had to buy something." **[M1998-139]**. Idle money is uncomfortable but less so than a bad deal: "we don’t like having excess cash around. We like even less doing dumb deals because we do them forever." **[M2006-028]**; "It's a painful condition to be in � but not as painful as doing something stupid." **[L2003-005]**.

Few decisions are needed. "in an investment lifetime it's just too hard to make hundreds of smart decisions", so the strategy "required our being smart - and not too smart at that - only a very few times" **[L1993-015]**; "you only have to be right on a very, very few things in your lifetime as long as you never make any big mistakes" **[M2003-068]**; "you don’t have to find 20 of them; you only have to find one, really" **[M2006-071]**; "If you get real opportunities every five or six years, you have to be patient." **[M2025-029]**. **The punch card:** no instance found in the v5 ledger (a search for "punch" returns four rows, none of them the image); the nearest row is **[L1993-015]**.

Waiting is not passivity. The same row that tolerates piled-up money adds: "when we see something that makes sense, we’re willing to act very fast, very big" **[M1998-137]**. Dry spells end: "in 1973 and ’4, you know, there were all kinds of things to do" **[M1995-034]**; "We will find things, though, under any circumstances. They don’t come at an even flow." **[M2015-028]**. And the hunt is active: "we were rooting around for those opportunities. We weren’t sitting behind our desks" **[M2003-090]**.

**Omission as the costliest error.** The merged map's section 3, group 7, reports that the speakers call errors of omission the costliest. Munger: "The mistakes that have been most extreme in Berkshire’s history are mistakes of omission. They don’t show up in our figures. They show up in opportunity costs." **[M2001-004]**; "almost all huge errors were in not making a purchase" **[L2014-045]**. Buffett: "most of our mistakes have been mistakes of omission rather than commission" **[M1997-083]**. The boundary is the circle of competence: "We only regard errors as being things that are within our circle of competence. [...] What’s an error is when it’s something we understand, and we stand there and stare at it, and we don’t do anything." **[M2001-006]**.

### Concentration and sizing

**How much, when sure.** "You got to be willing to take a really big bite. And it’s crazy if you don’t." **[M1997-085]**; people "have to learn to have the courage and the intelligence to step up in a major way when those rare opportunities come by" **[M1997-084]**; "we like to go in heavy" **[M2003-144]**; "our idea is not to tiptoe into anything. So we buy them as fast as we can, when prices are right." **[M2009-102]**; "Good ideas are too scarce to be parsimonious with once you find them." **[M2003-145]**; "if we like a business, we’re going to buy as much of it as we can and keep it as long as we can" **[M2020-020]**. The speakers name sizes in their own words: "about 40 percent in a single stock" in the partnership **[M1994-069]**; "it would be a mistake not to have half your net worth in" and "75 percent of your net worth or something like that in a given situation", each said of "smaller sums" and "lead-pipe cinches" **[M2008-048]**; Cap Cities in 1974, where "You could have put a hundred percent of your net worth in there and not worry" **[M2008-050]**. The 20 percent row sets a lower bound for the lifetime opportunity **[M2008-052]**. These are the speakers' figures for named situations; this draft writes no sizing number of its own.

**The conditions on sizing.** Big commitment is tied to understanding, price and ranking together: "we’re not going to do that unless we think we understand the business very well, and we think the nature of the business, what we’re paying for it, the people running it, and all of that lead up to virtually no risk", and "assuming it were that much more attractive than the second, and third, and fourth choices" **[M1994-069]**; "something that you really feel you know enough to buy the whole business [...] You ought to really understand the business." **[M1994-070]**; "when you find one in which you really have thought it out and have confidence" **[M2004-041]**. Munger adds self-knowledge: "if you know the edge of your own ability pretty well, you should ignore most of the notions of our experts about what I call “deworsification” of portfolios" **[M2023-037]**.

**The case against diversification for the one who knows.** "it’s crazy to own 50 stocks or 40 stocks or 30 stocks, probably [...] And to have some super-wonderful business and then put money in number 30 or 35 on your list of attractiveness and forego putting more money into number one, just strikes Charlie and me as madness." **[M1996-041]**; "I cannot understand why an investor of that sort elects to put money into a business that is his 20th favorite rather than simply adding that money to his top choices" **[L1993-026]**; "if you’ve only got three, I’d rather be in my best ideas instead of my worst" **[M2023-036]**; "I have always been willing to own just two or three stocks" **[M2019-080]**; "We would own the half of dozen or so stocks we like best" **[M2009-037]**. Here Q10 and Q8 meet: the reason against the thirtieth idea is that it loses the comparison with more of the first **[M1996-041]**, **[M1997-055]**.

**The case for the index for the one who does not know.** "Diversification is a protection against ignorance. [...] that is a perfectly sound approach for somebody who does not feel they know how to analyze businesses." **[M1996-040]**; "a know-nothing investor will get decent results as long as they know they’re a knownothing investor, diversify as to time they purchase their equities, and as to the equities they purchase" **[M2008-052]**; "By periodically investing in an index fund, for example, the know-nothing investor can actually out-perform most investment professionals." **[L1993-025]**; "for most people, the best thing to do is to own the S&P 500 index fund" **[M2020-009]**. The full treatment of the index belongs to the foundations (the index for the non-professional); Q10 points there and does not repeat it. Between the two sits the package: where many businesses met the margin of safety test but none was well known, "there I diversified" **[M2010-085]**, and where an industry is understood but not its companies, "I would buy a group of the leading companies" **[M1999-044]**.

**The test:** have I really thought it out, do I understand it well enough to buy the whole business, and is it clearly ahead of my second, third and fourth choices **[M1994-069]**, **[M1994-070]**, **[M2004-041]**? If yes, am I loading up or dabbling **[M2008-052]**, **[M1997-085]**? If I do not know, am I owning the index instead **[L1993-025]**, **[M2008-052]**?

**Rules out:** buying because cash has piled up **[M1996-004]**, **[M1998-137]**; swinging at marginal pitches **[L1997-006]**; the token position in an understood idea **[M2001-007]**, **[M2002-060]**; the thirtieth idea over more of the first **[M1996-041]**.

### Why it is a WEIGHING

No row read here ends the work on Q10 alone; whether to buy at all is settled by Q7 and Q8, and Q10 grades the response. The rows weigh degree of conviction against size: the partnership's limit was reached only where the case added up to "virtually no risk" and stood well ahead of the next choices **[M1994-069]**; the large figures are qualified, "or something like that" and "if you’re working with smaller sums" **[M2008-048]**; the quantity is "limited primarily by the availability of the quantity we want, rather than restricting ourselves based on some percentage of a total portfolio" **[M2003-144]**. They weigh the cost of waiting against the cost of a bad commitment: taking a low return "for a while, on the theory that we’ll find something that gives us 10 percent or better" rather than commit and find "a big principal loss" **[M2003-152]**, set against the burden that idle cash puts on them, "at a point, the burden of proof is definitely on us" **[M2017-068]**. And they weigh size against survival: the lifetime of few decisions works "as long as you never make any big mistakes" **[M2003-068]**, and Buffett would "rather be, you know, a hundred times too cautious than 1 percent too incautious" **[M2015-027]**, which joins Q10 to the standing rule and Q9. Size itself narrows the field: "can you invest truly large sums reasonably well. You can’t do it as well as you can do small sums." **[M2019-079]**.

### Mistakes the speakers narrate here

The omissions, in the speakers' words:

- **Fading on a small rise.** "we start buying, and the price goes up a little and we quit [...] A mistake like that cost us $8 billion in the case of Walmart stock" **[M2003-047]**; "I’d gotten anchored, in effect, to some initial price when I could have paid more subsequently and it really was inconsequential" **[M2004-038]**; "that thumb sucking, reluctance to pay a little more — the current cost is in the area of 10 billion" **[M2004-039]**; "we could have bought lots of stock, except it went up a little bit and then we faded because of price" **[M2003-145]**; "we should’ve continued, but we didn’t because it went up an eighth" **[M1997-120]**.

## Q11 — HAVING BOUGHT: HAS THE BUSINESS CHANGED, OR ONLY ITS PRICE? WEIGHING.

The question is asked of something already owned. The rows split what happened since the purchase into two things, what the quotation did and what the business did, and only the second is a reason to act.

> "But we continue to think that it is **usually foolish to part with an interest in a business that is both understandable and durably wonderful.** Business interests of that kind are **simply too hard to replace.** [...] An investor should ordinarily hold a small piece of an outstanding business **with the same tenacity that an owner would exhibit if he owned all of that business.**" — **[L1993-014]**, 1993 letter

> "if we **lose confidence in the management**, if we **lose confidence in the durability of the competitive advantage**, if we **recognize we made a mistake** when we went into it — we sell plenty of times. So it’s not unheard of. On the other hand, if you really get a wonderful business with outstanding management — but mostly the wonderful business part of it — **when in doubt, keep holding. But it’s no inviolable rule.**" — **[M2009-040]**, 2009 annual meeting, AM

> "But the truth is, if you owned the businesses you liked prior to the virus arriving — **it changes prices** and it changes — but **nobody’s forcing you to sell.** And if you really like the business, and you like the management you’re in with, and **the business hasn’t fundamentally changed** —" — **[M2020-008]**, 2020 annual meeting, AM

The default is to keep. "the real thing to do with a great business is just hang on for dear life." **[M1998-012]**; "the best thing to do is buy a stock that you don’t ever want to sell." **[M1998-011]**. The holding is judged as the businesses are judged, by two tests the 2007 letter names: "The first test is improvement in earnings, with our making due allowance for industry conditions. The second test, more subjective, is whether their "moats" [...] have widened during the year." **[L2007-016]**. Munger states the same in 2009: "I wouldn’t get too excited about the fact that the stock goes down. [...] What matters are things like this" **[M2009-026]**.

### The three reasons the rows give for selling

M2009-040 names three, and the rows around it supply the cases.

**1. Confidence in the management is lost.** The Tesco sale is the worked case, and its lesson is speed: "In 2013, I soured somewhat on the company's then-management and sold 114 million shares [...] My leisurely pace in making sales would prove expensive. [...] You see a cockroach in your kitchen; as the days go by, you meet his relatives." **[L2014-003]**

**2. The durability of the advantage is in doubt.** "So now we sell — really when we think that we’ve — when we’re reevaluating the economic characteristics of the business. [...] But we probably don’t think that their competitive advantage is as strong as we might have thought" **[M2002-004]**. The horizon is the purchase horizon: "we will occasionally reevaluate the economic characteristics that we see 10 years out from the ones that we saw 10 years ago" **[M2000-054]**. The change may be in the industry, not the company: the reinsurers were sold as "a mildly negative judgment on the reinsurance business", "not a negative judgment on their managements" **[M2016-008]**, and the airlines because "I think it changed in a very major way." **[M2020-017]**

**3. The purchase is recognised as a mistake.** "I just decided that I’d made a mistake in evaluating" **[M2020-017]**. The cost of delay is named in the 2008 letter: "sucking my thumb when new facts came in that should have caused me to re-examine my thinking and promptly take action." **[L2008-003]**. Munger quotes Keynes for the licence: "we would reserve the right to change our mind." **[M1994-020]**

**The better use of the money**, where the rows say so. "So you sell if you need money for something else." **[M1998-012]**; "the ideal way is when you found something you like immensely better." **[M1998-013]**; "If we can find a good business to buy, you know, we will sell the least attractive." **[M2007-107]**. Buffett ties this reason to an era of more ideas than money: "Forty years ago my sales were all because I found something that I liked even better. [...] Now I’ve got more money than ideas, and that’s a different equation." **[M2002-004]** (also **[M2000-055]**, **[M2007-104]**). The row's own note records a fourth reason of this kind added to M2009-040 later in the same answer; it is not rowed separately.

**For a controlled business the list is narrower.** "we make only two exceptions: when they promise to start losing money indefinitely or if we have major labor problems." **[M2009-041]** (also **[M2005-093]**, **[M2020-028]**). The forward test is capital: "we will not fund a company that — where we think that it’s going to chew up money in the future." **[M2020-026]**; "you do not want to try and fix something that’s unfixable." **[M2016-031]**. Liking the manager is named as the trap: "if he’d been a jerk, you know, we’d have quit the textile business much faster and (laughs) we’d have been better off." **[M2024-031]**

When the decision is made, it is whole: "when we change our mind, we don’t take half measure or anything of the sort." **[M2020-020]**

**A reading, not a row.** The three reasons are the purchase questions asked again of a holding with the price taken out: the management (Q5), the castle (Q2), and the original analysis (Q1 and Q7). The rows do not say this; it is the drafter's arrangement, and it is offered because it keeps Q11 from becoming a separate checklist. The better use of the money is the one reason that brings price back in, and it does so through the comparison of Q8, not through the quotation of the holding.

### Price alone is not a reason

> "one of the important things in stocks is that **the stock does not know that you own it.** [...] All these feelings, and it has **no impact whatsoever.**" — **[M1995-022]**, 1995 annual meeting, AM

A fall is a reason to buy more if anything: "if it goes down 25 percent in price or 30 percent in price, if you have more money available, buy more. And if you don’t, you know, so what? [...] Just look at the business and judge how it’s doing." **[M1997-144]**. "if the stock goes down 20 or 30 or 40 percent, we don’t feel we’re poor in that situation." **[M2020-018]**. "Selling fine businesses on "scary" news is usually a bad decision." **[L1996-009]**. A gain is no reason either: a large unrealized gain "doesn’t have anything to do with our decision-making." **[M2018-014]**; selling dominant winners is "akin to suggesting that the Bulls trade Michael Jordan because he has become so important to the team." **[L1996-025]**. Selling to rebuy lower is ruled out: "You have to make two decisions right." **[M1999-079]** (also **[M1996-097]**). A full price stops adding, not holding: "We don’t find them uncomfortable, in the sense that we want to sell them." **[M1998-160]**; "If the choice is between a questionable business at a comfortable price or a comfortable business at a questionable price, we much prefer the latter." **[L1999-021]**. Selling for tax is called "a dumb decision." **[M2023-035]**

**The qualifications.** The same speakers say a silly price is a reason: "if they get to a silly price, you probably should sell them." **[M2007-089]**; "if you ever attend a meeting here, and there are 60 or 70 times earnings, keep an eye on me." **[M1996-095]**. They call not selling in the bubble a mistake: "I made a big mistake in not selling several of our larger holdings during The Great Bubble." **[L2003-021]**; "I talked when I should have walked." **[L2004-011]**; Coca-Cola "at 50 times earnings, it was a silly price on the stock." **[M2006-029]**. PetroChina was sold when "we no longer felt it was undervalued compared to other oil companies" **[M2008-114]**. And M2009-040 closes on "no inviolable rule." The rows give no multiple at which a price becomes silly; the "60 or 70 times" and "50 times" are remarks about two cases, not a stated rule, and this draft writes no threshold.

### Grading a threat

> "you have to be thinking all the time and **alert to whether there’s been something that really changes the game** in a big way. And that’s not only true for American Express, that’s true for other things we own, including things we own 100 percent of. And **we’ll be wrong sometimes. We’ll be late sometimes** [...] Assessing the probabilities of those threats being **a minor problem, or a major problem, or a life-threatening problem**, you know, it’s a tough game" — **[M2016-081]**, 2016 annual meeting, PM

The rows show each grade in a case. **Minor**: Walmart's bribery scandal, "I don’t think the earning power of Walmart five years from now will be materially affected" **[M2012-051]**; and Munger's warning against reacting to slow decline, "we can’t change — make a portfolio change — every time something is a little less advantaged than it used to be." **[M2016-080]**. **Major, held through**: the self-driving car "is a real threat to the auto insurance industry. [...] it would not cause us to be thinking for one second about selling GEICO." **[M2014-048]**. **Life-threatening**: the Baltimore department store, "if we’d kept it, we would have gone out of business." **[M2016-031]**; "those businesses will wither and die. And we’d better use the money someplace else." **[M2019-012]**. The rows warn that the slow threat is the hard one to grade: "slow change can be much harder to perceive, and can lull you to sleep easier" **[M2014-038]**. The rows give the three grades but no criteria for sorting a threat into one of them; the grading is the "tough game" itself.

### The holding the speakers would not sell at any price

> "**regardless of price, we have no interest at all in selling any good businesses that Berkshire owns**, and are very reluctant to sell sub-par businesses as long as we expect them to generate at least some cash and as long as we feel good about their managers and labor relations." — **[R1995-011]**, 1995 annual report, owner-related business principles

"we don’t break off the relationships that we develop simply because we get offered a fancy price for something." **[M2000-052]**; "we would never, never sell an operation like that, ever." **[M2022-015]**. The cost is named and accepted: "We don’t see any reason to make an extra half a percent a year or 1 percent a year" **[M1995-044]**; "this mindset that may produce slightly suboptimal returns because of our attitude" **[M2005-094]**.

**The qualification.** The promise covers controlled businesses, not stocks: "we regard any marketable security as available for sale, however unlikely such a sale now seems." **[L2016-010]** (also **[R2016-001]**); "at some price, you have to be willing to sell something that’s a marketable security, forgetting about a controlled business." **[M1996-095]**. Even the controlled business has the two exceptions above **[M2009-041]**, and the proviso that "we’re not going to have to put money in incessantly" **[M2005-094]**.

### What is ABSENT

No instance found in the v5 ledger of a sell rule or a "what would prove me wrong" test committed at the time of purchase; the nearest rows are after-purchase triggers (**[M2009-040]**, **[M2016-081]**, **[M2020-008]**, **[L2007-016]**, **[M2020-026]**) and the analyst's habits in the foundations (**[M1997-127]**, **[M1998-144]**, **[M1999-129]**, **[M2016-055]**, **[M2025-013]**). The absence stands; the falsifier is carried as a CONVENTION in the foundations (the analyst's habits), and a Q11 review reads first the metric and the fact written there at purchase. *(correction pass 2026-10-05: ruling case section 3, item 1.)*

The sweep behind this sentence: the four slice maps (A's map: the speakers "do not pose a "what would prove me wrong" question as a gate"; B, C and D record none), every M19 row read in full, and a pattern search of all 4,279 ledger rows for "prove wrong", "stop-loss", "exit strategy", "in advance", "ahead of time", "write down", "price target" and "wrong about". What the search did find runs the other way: the commitment made at purchase is to keep. "Berkshire has no "exit strategy." We buy to keep." **[L2005-006]**; "we tell people ahead of time that that’s the way we’re going to play it." **[M2005-094]**; "The best way to look at any investment is, how will I feel if I own it forever" **[M1998-158]**. The nearest thing to a thesis test is the business itself: "if we’re right about the business, we’re going to make a lot of money. And if we’re wrong about the business, we don’t have any hopes" **[M1997-052]**.

### Why it is a WEIGHING

No single finding forces a sale. Keeping is "no inviolable rule" **[M2009-040]**; threats are graded on "probabilities" in "a tough game" **[M2016-081]**; the moat test is "more subjective" **[L2007-016]**; giving up a business is among "the hard decisions" **[M2022-064]**. The weighing is not even: "when in doubt, keep holding" **[M2009-040]** puts the burden on the reason to sell, and the narrated mistakes run mostly one way, the wonderful business sold too soon (GEICO **[L1995-012]**, Disney **[M1998-100]**, McDonald's **[L1998-019]**, Cap Cities **[L1995-032]**), with the bubble holdings **[L2003-021]** and the late textile and Tesco exits **[M2024-031]**, **[L2014-003]** on the other side. The one near-STOP is on further money, not on the sale: "we will not knowingly throw good money after bad." **[L2023-010]** (also **[M2020-026]**, **[M2024-010]**).

## Q12 (optional) — WOULD WE BE PROUD OF HOW THE MONEY IS MADE? STOP for named businesses, otherwise WEIGHING.

> "not only do they behave in a way that conforms with the laws, but that they behave in a way where, **if a story were written by an unfriendly but intelligent reporter, the next morning, in their local paper, they would have no problem with their neighbors, their family reading it.**" — **[M2008-011]**, 2008 annual meeting, AM

> "It’s deeply wrong. **We don’t want to make our money selling things that are bad for people.**" — **[M2021-059]**, 2021 annual meeting, PM

> "And we’ve **turned down businesses, including a big tobacco business.** [...] **We want to have people think of us as having won fairly and used wisely. It works.**" — **[M2016-064]**, 2016 annual meeting, PM

The question is optional because its rows are late (2007 to 2025 in the merged count, with seven earlier moral-line rows inside B's owner theme) and because no slice placed it in a purchase ordering. Its weight in the rows is high all the same: "We care more about that than business mistakes, way more." **[M2010-033]**; "we won’t trade reputation for money." **[M2010-032]**

### The newspaper test and the businesses named

The law is the floor, not the test: "every business ought to decline a lot of business that’s perfectly legal and proper to accept." **[M2010-007]**; "we’ve got a huge area of things which is perfectly legal to do, that we think beneath us." **[M2004-076]**. Two further tests sit beside the newspaper: industry custom is no excuse, "the one reason you can’t give at Berkshire [...] is that everybody else is doing it." **[M2009-013]** (also **[M2017-052]**); and nearness to the line is itself the answer, "the very fact that they think it’s that close to the line probably tells them it’s over the line." **[M2010-034]**

The businesses named, each with its row:
- **Casinos.** "the gambling casinos use clever psychological tricks to cause people to hurt themselves. [...] It’s a dirty business, and I don’t think you’ll find a casino soon in Berkshire Hathaway." **[M2007-018]**
- **Tobacco, whole.** The refusal is told twice. "probably one of the best businesses I’ve ever seen, in terms of the economics of it. [...] we sat down in the lobby and just decided that we didn’t want to be in that business." **[M2005-097]**; "“This is probably the best business we’ve ever seen.” [...] And we decided not to do it." **[M2021-013]**; and **[M2016-064]** above.
- **Distributor-loading schemes.** The test is "whether a direct marketing operation is really based on selling product to would-be distributors of one sort, and loading them up, instead of [...] selling it to end users." **[M2013-015]**; with Munger's rider, "there’s likely to be more flimflam in selling magic potions than pots and pans." **[M2013-016]**
- **Gambling dressed as investing.** **[M2021-059]** above, of trading apps; and bitcoin, "in my life I try and avoid things that are stupid and evil and make me look bad in comparison with somebody else. And bitcoin does all three." **[M2022-078]**
- **Promotion on a borrowed reputation.** "we could attract a ton of money, and we could get a big overwrite on it. But it’s not our game." **[M2015-062]**

### Where the line is imperfect

> "there’s something about every business that, if you knew it, you wouldn’t like. [...] if you expect perfection, you know, in your spouse or in your friends or in companies, you’re not going to find it." — **[M2021-012]**, 2021 annual meeting, AM

Against it, in the same answer, the declined tobacco business, "it’s a very tough thing to decide whether you get in or out of a business." **[M2021-013]**. The speakers draw the line at control and doubt it: "we would not have trouble owning stock in a cigarette company. We wouldn’t want to manufacture cigarettes" with "I’m not sure that the logic is perfect on that" **[M2005-096]** (also **[M2004-075]**); "Is that the correct moral line to draw? I don’t know." **[M2007-139]**; "we don’t necessarily draw it perfectly" **[M2018-026]**; "You can draw these lines where you wish." **[M2004-076]**. M2021-059's own concept line extends the rule to investing in such sellers, against the 2005 willingness to own the cigarette stock. Where perfection is not demanded and where a business is refused although it is the best seen is not settled by the rows. [OPEN]

### STOP or WEIGHING

**STOP for the named businesses when the whole is to be bought and run**: casinos **[M2007-018]**, tobacco **[M2005-097]**, **[M2016-064]**, **[M2021-013]**, loading schemes **[M2013-015]**. The refusals are stated as done, not weighed against price; the tobacco business was refused as "one of the best businesses" seen **[M2005-097]**. **WEIGHING otherwise**, and for a marketable stake: the rows own as stock what they would not run **[M2004-075]**, **[M2005-096]**, call the line imperfect **[M2021-012]**, and give the newspaper test as the instrument **[M2008-011]**. The question is a filter on how the money is made, not on the managers' honesty, which Q5 carries.

# V. A CLOSING NOTE ON THE INVESTOR

This is not a question. No slice treats these rows as a filter on businesses: C's map says of its theme "Not a filter on businesses", D's says "not a business filter", and the merge says of M10 that "it governs the analyst". They are carried here as the speakers' advice to the person doing the work, grouped as the rows group themselves, with the strongest rows of each. Temperament also appears in the foundations, as a condition of the activity; it appears here as conduct, the habits that keep it. The rows join the two: the record is put down not to brilliance but to having "done fewer dumb things than most people" **[M1999-109]**, and "It’s not a business that requires extraordinary intellect. It does require extraordinary discipline." **[M2004-035]**. The M18 rows all fall in 2007 to 2025 (D's map: heaviest in the closing question periods of 2023 to 2025), and many are addressed to students and the young (**[M2008-092]**, **[M2008-093]**, **[M2017-098]**), so they speak to a life, not to a portfolio; Munger says as much when he calls wealth from "passive holding of little bits of paper" alone "a failed life" **[M2001-022]**.

**Invest in yourself.**
> "I think that **the most important investment you can make is in yourself.** I mean, very, very, very few people get anything like their potential horsepower translated into the actual horsepower of their output in life." — **[M2008-092]**, 2008 annual meeting, PM

> "whatever abilities you have **can’t be taken away from you, they can’t actually be inflated away from you.** [...] So, the best investment by far is anything that develops yourself." — **[M2022-033]**, 2022 annual meeting, AM

The test of it is another's purchase: which classmate would you buy "10 percent of for the rest of their life"? Not the highest IQ, but "the person that’s going to be effective." **[M2008-093]**

**Whom to work with.**
> "you are going to **gravitate toward the behavior of those around you.** I tell people to be sure and **associate with people who are better than you are.**" — **[M2007-108]**, 2007 annual meeting, PM

> "Oh yes, **the toxic people** who are trying to fool you or lie to you or aren’t reliable in meeting their commitments. A great lesson of life is **get them the hell out of your life.** [...] And do it fast." — **[M2023-071]**, 2023 annual meeting, PM

And the work itself: "look for the job that you would take if you didn’t need a job." **[M2017-098]** (also **[L2021-010]**).

**Reputation and honesty as a habit.**
> "**the best way to get what you want is to deserve what you want.**" — **[M1998-170]**, 1998 annual meeting, PM

> "it helps if you conducted yourself in life so that other people trust you. [...] And then it helps even more if **other people are right to trust you.**" — **[M2011-031]**, 2011 annual meeting, AM

"if you make yourself a very reliable person and stay reliable all your life [...] it will be very hard for you to fail at anything you want." **[M2007-115]**. The habits are chosen: "very few of those things, you know, are in your DNA. They are choices. And they are also habits." **[M2002-038]**

**The inner scorecard, and the temperament it keeps.**
> "in the end you want to have **an internal score card.** I mean, you are not a better person or a worse person because you live a different kind of life than your neighbor." — **[M2008-059]**, 2008 annual meeting, AM

> "you do have to have an **emotional stability.** You have to have sort of an **inner peace about your decisions.** [...] You have to be able to **think for yourself.**" — **[M2009-020]**, 2009 annual meeting, AM

Envy is ruled out by name ("we — rule out as envy as part of your repertoire." **[M2008-112]**), and so is anger: "you do not want to add one single iota because you’re angry." **[M2011-008]**

**How to learn.**
> "So you just have to get up each morning and try and **go to bed that night a little wiser** than you were when you got up." — **[M2010-086]**, 2010 annual meeting, PM

> "We try and avoid the worst anchoring effect, which is **always your previous conclusion.** We really try and destroy our previous ideas." — **[M2016-054]**, 2016 annual meeting, PM

> "this **constant study of other people’s disasters** and other people’s errors has helped us enormously" — **[M2012-104]**, 2012 annual meeting, PM

Munger makes it a duty: "I think it’s dishonorable to stay stupider than you have to be" **[M2015-093]**. The postmortem is the learning habit applied to one's own selling: "I contrasted all sale decisions versus all purchase decisions." **[M1999-125]**

**What to avoid.**
> "all the trite stuff is what works. I mean, you **avoid doing the really dumb things** [...] And then you want to develop a good character, and good mental habits, and you want to **learn from your mistakes, every single one**" — **[M2004-124]**, 2004 annual meeting, PM

> "You want to go into a game where you’re **very likely to win without having any unusual luck.**" — **[M2023-069]**, 2023 annual meeting, PM

"You don't have to make it back the way that you lost it." **[L1994-036]**; "if I had one piece of advice to give to young people [...] it would be just to don’t get in debt." **[M2004-125]**; "Figure out what you’re bad at and avoid all of it." **[M2022-080]**

# VII. THE RUN

A run under this draft proceeds in one order. The foundations (section II) are read first; they admit and reject nothing, and the index fork decides whether the questions are used at all. The standing rule (section III) is then checked against the buyer: how the purchase would be financed, sized and held. The purchase questions follow in order, Q1 to Q8. A STOP that fails closes the file in OUT or TOO HARD and nothing after it is answered; a WEIGHING is written down with its rows and carried forward. Q9 weighs the target's own debt and exposures, and Q10 weighs whether this is the pitch and whether the size matches it. Q12 is asked where the operator chooses. Q11 is asked only after purchase, of the business and not of its price. Every judgment cites a v5 id from `principle_ledger_v5.csv`, or it is an opinion and is labelled as one. No number is written without the row that carries it or a CONVENTION label. The run template for v5 does not yet exist; it is written at adoption, if a ruling case adopts this draft.

**Facts for a later question** *(CONVENTION, ours, adopted 2026-10-06 at the operator's approval of `Framework/v5/CASE 2026-10-05 - gaps found by the small-cap runs, for the operator's approval.md`, section G)*. When a STOP closes the file, facts already found that bear on a later question are written down, not weighed, in a section headed AFTER THE STOP: FACTS FOUND, NOT WEIGHED, under the question each bears on, each with its source **[M1997-127]**. Where those facts bear on integrity (Q5), the run's box line adds "integrity facts recorded, not judged" and the register line repeats it, so that a later run, a research pass, or a holding review of the same name reads them first; any later run that reaches Q5 must answer them **[M2013-088]**. No verdict is given on them and the box is not changed (operator rule 2). Rationale: one run's format already did this; naming it makes it the rule, and the flag in the register keeps a closed file's integrity facts from being lost when the name is run again.

**The research pass, for a TOO HARD (WORK)** *(added 2026-10-05; CONVENTION in its form, section VI)*. When a STOP closes TOO HARD (WORK) (section I, the two causes), the run file is not the end of the name. A research file is opened in `Test Runs/` beside the run, dated, and written in this order, each step committed before the next: (1) the list, "What do I not know that I need to know?" **[M1999-129]**, each question marked knowable or not **[M2006-076]**; if the deciding question is not knowable, the file closes TOO HARD (NATURE) at once; (2) for each knowable question, the evidence that would answer it, where it is, and the answer that would close the file OUT **[M1998-144]**; (3) the reading, every fact with its source, aggregators flagged; (4) the close, IN, OUT or TOO HARD (NATURE), with the STOP re-answered on the new evidence and the run continued from that STOP if IN. For a held name a second session runs steps 3 and 4 from the same steps 1 and 2 without the holder's list or the holding review, and the two closes are compared before either is acted on **[M2002-092]**.

**The form of the pass, refined after the HRB pilot** *(2026-10-05, approved by the operator after both analysts found the same defects; CONVENTION, ours, like the form above)*. (a) **Each OUT answer is one fact**: the single fact that would show the castle filling in, never a chain of conditions; conjunctions belong to the IN rule, so that a pass cannot slip between them into TOO HARD (NATURE) while the evidence runs one way. (b) **Each knowable question carries its span and its source**, fixed at step 2 before any reading, so that two analysts read the same years from the same documents. (c) **A question that proves unanswerable** from primary documents is recorded as such, with the search that failed, and the close is decided on the remaining answers; it does not make the pass end TOO HARD (WORK) **[M2008-086]**. (d) **The blind session receives steps 1 and 2 without the holding facts**: no position, no outcome of a holding review; the incentive line names only that the first analyst may hold the name **[M2002-092]**.

**CONVENTION (ours, confessed 2026-10-05): the anchor date bars later rows.** In any point-in-time test (a blind case, a backtest, any run dated to an anchor before today), no row dated after the anchor is cited, whatever it says. Rationale: a row from after the anchor can carry the speaker's own later verdict on the very company, which is a look-ahead the case was built to exclude; the five Test B' sessions met such rows in this draft and declined them by choice, and the rule makes the choice the procedure. *(correction pass 2026-10-05: gap (h) of the ruling case, section 5.)*

**The tooling, until it is retargeted.** `tools/run.py` was written for v4 and prints v4 material into every run, including v4 ids and the v4 floor. Until it is retargeted to this draft, a v5 run reads only its arithmetic lines (prices, share counts, filed figures and the sums made from them) and nothing it prints as a rule, an id or a verdict; the floor a v5 run applies is the CONVENTION at Q7. *(correction pass 2026-10-05: gap (i) of the ruling case, section 5; all four Test A' sessions declared the contamination and ignored it.)*

**The owner's price** *(CONVENTION, ours, a reporting convention and not a rule; adopted 2026-10-06 at the operator's decision on section F of `Framework/v5/CASE 2026-10-05 - gaps found by the small-cap runs, for the operator's approval.md`)*. Every run that reaches Q7, or that computes Q7 after an earlier STOP under the heading COMPUTATION, NOT A CLEARANCE, reports two figures for the operator: the **value range** as Q7 builds it, and the **fair price**, the price at which the midpoint of the range earns the floor, with the tax treatment stated and the figure given on the equity alone and on equity plus net debt where the two differ. There is no cheap price: a price below fair is one at which the central case already clears the floor, and how far below it must be is the screamer test, which stays a judgment, "It should scream at you." **[M2009-005]**; the margin a run asks for grows as its certainty falls **[M1997-080]**. Neither figure is a verdict or entry language (operator rule 3). Rationale: the operator asked for the figures, the rows give no fixed discount, and the runs of 2026-10-05 each wrote their own cheap-price rule; one stated definition makes the runs comparable without pretending to be the speakers'.
